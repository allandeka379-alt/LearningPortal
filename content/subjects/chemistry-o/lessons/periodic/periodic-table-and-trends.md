## Introduction

Every chemistry laboratory, from Mutare to Hwange, has a Periodic Table on the wall. It is far more than a list: it arranges all the known elements so that elements with similar properties line up in columns. In 1869 the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the elements known in his time in a table and left gaps for elements nobody had yet discovered, predicting their properties. When those elements were found, his predictions turned out to be remarkably accurate.

In this lesson you will learn how the modern Periodic Table is organised, why its patterns exist, and how to use an element's position to predict how it behaves. (Exam papers give you a Periodic Table, so you do not need to memorise it, but you must know how to read it.)

You should already be able to write electronic configurations for elements 1 to 20.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Period"}
A horizontal row of the Periodic Table. The period number equals the number of occupied electron shells.
:::

:::definition{term="Group"}
A vertical column of the Periodic Table. Elements in Groups I to VII have the same number of outer-shell electrons as their group number.
:::

:::keyfact{title="How the table is arranged"}
- Elements are placed in order of increasing **proton (atomic) number**.
- **Metals** are on the left and in the centre; **non-metals** are on the right.
- Important groups: Group I (the alkali metals), Group VII (the halogens), Group VIII or 0 (the noble gases). The **transition elements** form the block of metals between Groups II and III.
:::

## Why the patterns exist

| Feature of position | Link to electrons | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Group number (I to VII) | Number of outer-shell electrons | Mg, Group II: 2,8,**2** |
| Period number | Number of occupied shells | Mg, Period 3: **three** shells |
| Similar chemical properties in a group | Same number of outer electrons | Li, Na and K all react with water to form hydroxides |
| Charge on ion | Electrons lost or gained to reach a full outer shell | Group II: $2+$; Group VI: $2-$ |

| Group | I | II | III | IV | V | VI | VII | VIII (0) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Typical ion | $1+$ | $2+$ | $3+$ | (usually no simple ion) | $3-$ | $2-$ | $1-$ | (no ions) |

## Across a period: from metals to non-metals

Moving from left to right across a period, the elements change from **metals** to **non-metals**. Period 3 shows this clearly:

| Element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl | Ar |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Type | Metal | Metal | Metal | Between (semi-metal) | Non-metal | Non-metal | Non-metal | Noble gas |
| Conducts electricity? | Yes | Yes | Yes | Slightly | No | No | No | No |
| Oxide | Basic | Basic | Amphoteric | Acidic | Acidic | Acidic | Acidic | None |

Metals tend to **lose** electrons and form positive ions; non-metals tend to **gain** or share electrons. The oxides change from basic (metal oxides) to acidic (non-metal oxides).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: predicting from position]
An element has proton number 13. Predict its properties.
1. Electronic configuration: 2,8,3. Three shells: **Period 3**. Three outer electrons: **Group III**.
2. Group III is on the metal side of the table, so the element is a **metal** (it is aluminium).
3. It loses its three outer electrons to form a $3+$ ion.
4. With oxide ions ($2-$) it forms an oxide in which the charges balance as $2 \times (+3) = 3 \times (-2)$: formula $\ce{X2O3}$ (here $\ce{Al2O3}$).
:::

## Trends in a group (Supplement: from data)

Elements in a group show **trends**: a property changes steadily as you go down the group. Exam questions often give you data for some elements and ask you to describe the trend and predict a missing value.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: describing a trend and predicting a value]
The radii of the atoms of four Group I elements are: lithium 152, sodium 186, potassium 227 and rubidium 248 (in picometres, pm). Predict the radius of a caesium atom, the next element down the group.
1. Describe the trend: the radius **increases** down the group.
2. Explain it: each element down the group has **one more occupied shell** of electrons.
3. Look at the steps: $+34$, $+41$, $+21$. Each step adds roughly 20 to 40 pm, and the most recent step was the smallest.
4. Predict: a value somewhat above 248 pm, for example about 265 to 280 pm. (The measured value is 265 pm.)
5. Any sensible value that continues the trend is accepted; a value below 248 would not be.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the table is in order of mass"}
The modern Periodic Table is arranged by **proton number**. (Argon, $A_r$ 40, comes before potassium, $A_r$ 39, for exactly this reason.)
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up groups and periods"}
Groups are columns (outer electrons); periods are rows (number of shells).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying every noble gas has 8 outer electrons"}
Helium has only 2, but its outer (first) shell is still full.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
To **describe a trend**, state the direction and the property ("melting point decreases down the group"), then quote data if given. To **predict**, give a value that continues the pattern and say why.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked why elements in a group react similarly, the answer is "same number of outer-shell electrons", not "same number of shells".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Periodic Table lists elements in order of increasing proton number, in periods (rows) and groups (columns).
- Group number (I to VII) = outer-shell electrons; period number = occupied shells.
- Elements in a group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of outer electrons.
- Across a period, elements change from metals (positive ions, basic oxides) to non-metals (negative ions, acidic oxides).
- Use position to predict ion charge, formulae and properties, and use data to describe and extend trends within a group.
:::
