## Introduction

The glowing red-orange signs above some shops at night, the balloons at a school prize-giving and the shielding gas used by welders in a Bulawayo engineering workshop all depend on the **noble gases**. These elements make up Group VIII (also called Group 0), the last column of the Periodic Table. They are so unreactive that for a long time chemists could not make them form any compounds at all.

This short lesson explains why the noble gases are unreactive and links their uses to their properties. You should already know how to write electronic configurations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Noble gases"}
The elements of Group VIII (Group 0): helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon. They are colourless, unreactive gases.
:::

:::definition{term="Monatomic"}
Existing as single, separate atoms rather than as molecules. The noble gases are monatomic: He, Ne, Ar.
:::

:::definition{term="Unreactive (inert)"}
Not taking part in chemical reactions under normal conditions.
:::

## Why the noble gases are unreactive

Look at their electronic configurations:

| Noble gas | Proton number | Electronic configuration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Helium, He | 2 | 2 |
| Neon, Ne | 10 | 2,8 |
| Argon, Ar | 18 | 2,8,8 |

Every noble gas atom has a **full outer shell** (two electrons for helium, eight for the others). Atoms of other elements react by losing, gaining or sharing electrons to reach exactly this arrangement. A noble gas atom already has it, so it has no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons. That is why:

- the noble gases are **unreactive**;
- they do not form bonds with each other, so they exist as **single atoms** (they are monatomic).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: argon and chlorine]
Explain why chlorine exists as $\ce{Cl2}$ molecules but argon exists as single atoms.
1. Chlorine is 2,8,7: one electron short of a full outer shell.
2. Two chlorine atoms share a pair of electrons, so each gains a full outer shell: a covalent bond forms and the molecule is $\ce{Cl2}$.
3. Argon is 2,8,8: its outer shell is already full.
4. Argon atoms have no need to share electrons, so no bonds form and argon is monatomic.
:::

:::note{title="A note for the curious"}
A few compounds of xenon and krypton with fluorine have been made in special conditions. For IGCSE and O-Level, describe all the noble gases as unreactive.
:::

## Physical properties and trends

The noble gases are colourless, odourless gases with very low melting and boiling points. Down the group the atoms get bigger and heavier, so:

- the **boiling points increase** (helium $-269\ ^\circ\text{C}$, neon $-246\ ^\circ\text{C}$, argon $-186\ ^\circ\text{C}$, xenon $-108\ ^\circ\text{C}$);
- the **density increases**. Helium is much less dense than air; argon and xenon are denser than air.

Argon makes up nearly 1% of the air. Argon, neon, krypton and xenon are obtained by the fractional distillation of liquid air; most helium is separated from natural gas.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: predicting from a trend]
Krypton lies between argon (boiling point $-186\ ^\circ\text{C}$) and xenon ($-108\ ^\circ\text{C}$). Predict its boiling point and its state at room temperature.
1. Boiling points increase down the group, so krypton's lies between $-186$ and $-108\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
2. Halfway is about $-147\ ^\circ\text{C}$: a sensible prediction. (The measured value is $-153\ ^\circ\text{C}$.)
3. This is far below room temperature, so krypton is a **gas**, like every noble gas.
:::

## Uses linked to properties

| Noble gas | Use | Property that makes it suitable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Helium | Filling balloons and airships | Much less dense than air, and unreactive, so it cannot burn (hydrogen is even less dense but is flammable) |
| Neon | Advertising signs | Glows red-orange when electricity passes through it; unreactive |
| Argon | Filling filament light bulbs | Unreactive, so the white-hot metal filament does not react with it (in air, oxygen would make the filament burn out) |
| Argon | Shielding gas in welding | Unreactive, and denser than air: it keeps oxygen away from the hot metal |
| Xenon | Very bright lamps (some car headlamps, camera flashes) | Gives a bright white light when electricity passes through it |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying every noble gas has eight outer electrons"}
Helium has only two, but that is a full first shell. The key idea is a **full** outer shell.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing noble gases as molecules"}
The noble gases are monatomic: write He and Ar, never $\ce{He2}$ or $\ce{Ar2}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying helium is used because it is the lightest gas"}
Hydrogen is less dense than helium. Helium is chosen for balloons because it is low in density **and** does not burn.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Explain why the noble gases are unreactive" needs the electron idea: full outer shells, so they do not need to lose, gain or share electrons. "Because they are noble" or "because they are gases" earns nothing.
:::

:::exam-tip
For a use, always give the matching property: argon in light bulbs **because it is unreactive**; helium in balloons **because it is less dense than air and unreactive**.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The noble gases (Group VIII or 0) are helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon.
- Their atoms have full outer shells, so they are unreactive and exist as single atoms (monatomic).
- They are colourless gases; boiling point and density increase down the group.
- Uses: helium in balloons, neon in advertising signs, argon in light bulbs and as a welding shield gas.
:::
