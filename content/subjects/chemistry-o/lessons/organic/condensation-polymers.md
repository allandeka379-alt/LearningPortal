## Introduction

The nylon fishing nets used on Lake Kariba, nylon ropes, the polyester in a school shirt and the clear plastic of a drinks bottle are all **condensation polymers**. Like poly(ethene), they are long chains made from many monomers, but they are made in a different way: every time two monomers join, a small molecule, usually **water**, is released.

This lesson is **Cambridge Supplement** and **ZIMSEC Form 4** content. You will learn how nylon and polyesters form, how to show them as block diagrams, how to identify their linkages and monomers, how condensation and addition polymerisation differ, and why PET bottles can be recycled into new PET. You should already know about addition polymers and about the reaction of a carboxylic acid with an alcohol to make an ester.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Condensation polymerisation"}
The joining of monomers that each have two functional groups, with a small molecule (such as water) lost every time a link forms.
:::

:::definition{term="Polyamide"}
A condensation polymer whose monomers are joined by amide links, $\ce{-CONH\bond{-}}$. Nylon is a polyamide.
:::

:::definition{term="Polyester"}
A condensation polymer whose monomers are joined by ester links, $\ce{-COO\bond{-}}$. PET (Terylene) is a polyester.
:::

:::keyfact
Monomers for condensation polymers have a reactive group at **each end**: a dicarboxylic acid has two $\ce{-COOH}$ groups, a diamine two $\ce{-NH2}$ groups and a diol two $\ce{-OH}$ groups.
:::

## Block diagrams

The middle part of each monomer is a carbon chain or ring whose details do not matter here, so it is drawn as a block. In this lesson the blocks are written as [A] and [B].

| Monomer | Block diagram |
| --- | --- |
| dicarboxylic acid | $\ce{HOOC\bond{-}}$[A]$\ce{-COOH}$ |
| diamine | $\ce{H2N\bond{-}}$[B]$\ce{-NH2}$ |
| diol | $\ce{HO\bond{-}}$[B]$\ce{-OH}$ |

## Nylon, a polyamide

A $\ce{-COOH}$ group of the acid reacts with an $\ce{-NH2}$ group of the diamine. The $\ce{-OH}$ from the acid and an $\ce{H}$ from the amine leave as water, and an **amide link** forms:

![Block diagrams of two condensation polymers: nylon has amide links (CONH) between the acid block A and the diamine block B, PET has ester links (COO) between the acid block A and the diol block B; one water molecule is lost at every link.](media:diagrams/condensation-polymers-block-diagrams.png "Figure: block diagrams of nylon (amide links) and PET (ester links)")

```
 HOOC-[A]-COOH  +  H2N-[B]-NH2  +  HOOC-[A]-COOH  + ...
                         |
                         v   (water is lost at every link)

 -CO-[A]-CO-NH-[B]-NH-CO-[A]-CO-NH-[B]-NH-   +  many H2O
```

The repeat unit is $\ce{-CO\bond{-}}$[A]$\ce{-CONH\bond{-}}$[B]$\ce{-NH\bond{-}}$. The amide link, drawn out in full, is a carbon atom with a double bond to oxygen, joined to a nitrogen atom that carries a hydrogen atom:

```
       O
       ||
  -----C---N-----
           |
           H
```

## PET (Terylene), a polyester

A $\ce{-COOH}$ group reacts with an $\ce{-OH}$ group of a **diol**, losing water and forming an **ester link**, just as in making ethyl ethanoate, but at both ends of each monomer:

```
 HOOC-[A]-COOH  +  HO-[B]-OH  +  HOOC-[A]-COOH  + ...
                         |
                         v   (water is lost at every link)

 -CO-[A]-CO-O-[B]-O-CO-[A]-CO-O-[B]-O-   +  many H2O
```

The repeat unit is $\ce{-CO\bond{-}}$[A]$\ce{-COO\bond{-}}$[B]$\ce{-O\bond{-}}$. PET is the polyester of drinks bottles; Terylene is a polyester fibre used in clothing.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: finding the monomers of a polymer]
A section of a polymer is $\ce{-O\bond{-}}$[Q]$\ce{-O-CO\bond{-}}$[P]$\ce{-CO-O\bond{-}}$[Q]$\ce{-O-CO\bond{-}}$[P]$\ce{-CO\bond{-}}$. Identify the linkage, the type of polymer and the monomers.

1. The links between the blocks are $\ce{-CO-O\bond{-}}$: **ester** links, so this is a **polyester**.
2. Split each ester link back into $\ce{-COOH}$ and $\ce{HO\bond{-}}$ (add back the water).
3. Block P has a $\ce{-CO\bond{-}}$ at each end: the monomer is $\ce{HOOC\bond{-}}$[P]$\ce{-COOH}$, a dicarboxylic acid.
4. Block Q has an $\ce{-O\bond{-}}$ at each end: the monomer is $\ce{HO\bond{-}}$[Q]$\ce{-OH}$, a diol.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: counting water molecules]
A nylon molecule contains 400 amide links. How many water molecules were released when it formed?

1. One water molecule is released for each link formed.
2. 400 links → **400** molecules of water.
:::

## Addition and condensation compared

| | Addition polymerisation | Condensation polymerisation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monomers | alkenes, with a $\ce{C=C}$ bond | two functional groups on each monomer |
| Number of kinds of monomer | usually one | usually two (one of each type) |
| Products | the polymer only | the polymer **and** a small molecule (water) |
| Links in the chain | C–C bonds only | amide or ester links |
| Examples | poly(ethene), poly(propene), PVC | nylon, PET (Terylene), proteins |

## Uses

| Polymer | Properties | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| nylon | strong, hard-wearing fibres, does not rot | ropes, fishing nets, clothing and stockings, carpets, toothbrush bristles |
| PET (Terylene) | strong, light, clear, waterproof; makes fibres | drinks bottles, food packaging, polyester clothing (often mixed with cotton), sails |

## Recycling PET (Supplement)

PET can be **converted back into its monomers**: the ester links are broken by reacting the polymer with water or another reagent (the reverse of its formation). The monomers are purified and **polymerised again** to make new, high-quality PET. This saves crude oil and keeps bottles out of landfill. Like other synthetic polymers, nylon and PET are non-biodegradable, so collecting them for recycling matters.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing amide and ester links"}
Amide: $\ce{-CO-NH\bond{-}}$ (contains N, from an amine). Ester: $\ce{-CO-O\bond{-}}$ (contains O, from an alcohol). Nylon is a polyamide; PET is a polyester.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the water"}
Condensation polymerisation always has **two** products: the polymer and the small molecule (water). Addition polymerisation has only one.
:::

:::mistake{title="Drawing the repeat unit with leftover end groups"}
Inside the chain there are no $\ce{-COOH}$, $\ce{-NH2}$ or $\ce{-OH}$ groups: they have become links. A repeat unit drawn as $\ce{HOOC\bond{-}}$[A]$\ce{-COOH}$ is a monomer, not a repeat unit.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
To find the monomers of a condensation polymer, cut through the middle of each link and add back H and OH: $\ce{-CO\bond{-}}$ becomes $\ce{-COOH}$, $\ce{-NH\bond{-}}$ becomes $\ce{-NH2}$ and $\ce{-O\bond{-}}$ becomes $\ce{-OH}$.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Describe the differences between addition and condensation polymerisation": monomers (C=C vs two functional groups), products (one vs polymer + water) and links (C–C vs amide/ester).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Condensation polymerisation joins monomers with two functional groups and releases a small molecule (water) at every link.
- Nylon: dicarboxylic acid + diamine → polyamide with amide links $\ce{-CONH\bond{-}}$ + water.
- PET (Terylene): dicarboxylic acid + diol → polyester with ester links $\ce{-COO\bond{-}}$ + water.
- Addition: C=C monomers, one product; condensation: two functional groups, polymer + water.
- PET can be broken back down into its monomers and polymerised again (recycling).
:::
