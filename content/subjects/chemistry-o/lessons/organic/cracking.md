## Introduction

When crude oil is separated by fractional distillation, it gives more of the heavy, long-chain fractions (such as fuel oil) than customers need, and less of the light fractions, such as petrol, than the world needs. Refineries solve this by **cracking**: breaking big molecules into smaller, more useful ones.

Cracking also makes **alkenes**, which hardly occur in crude oil at all. Most of the plastic bags, buckets and water tanks made of poly(ethene) or poly(propene) in a Zimbabwean market began as alkenes made by cracking. Zimbabwe has no oil refinery, but the plastics, fuels and chemicals it imports depend on this process.

You should already know that alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons, $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$, and that alkenes, $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$, contain a $\ce{C=C}$ double bond.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cracking"}
The thermal decomposition of long-chain alkanes into shorter-chain alkanes and alkenes (and sometimes hydrogen) by heating them to a high temperature with a catalyst.
:::

:::definition{term="Thermal decomposition"}
Breaking down a compound into simpler substances by heating it.
:::

:::keyfact
Cracking conditions: a **high temperature** (about 500 °C) and a **catalyst** such as aluminium oxide or silica. Cracking is a **chemical** change: new substances form.
:::

## Why crack long-chain alkanes?

1. **Supply and demand.** Distillation produces more long-chain fractions than are needed and less petrol than is needed. Cracking turns the surplus long chains into shorter alkanes that can be used in petrol.
2. **Alkenes for the chemical industry.** Ethene and propene are the starting materials for poly(ethene) and poly(propene), and ethene is used to make ethanol.
3. **Hydrogen.** Some cracking reactions produce hydrogen, which is used to make ammonia for fertilisers.

## Writing and balancing cracking equations

In a cracking equation, the numbers of carbon and hydrogen atoms on the two sides must be equal. Find a missing product by subtraction.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: finding a missing product]
Complete the equation $\ce{C14H30 -> C8H18 + ?}$ and say what type of compound the missing product is.

1. Carbon: $14 - 8 = 6$.
2. Hydrogen: $30 - 18 = 12$.
3. The missing product is $\ce{C6H12}$.
4. $\ce{C6H12}$ fits $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$ ($2 \times 6 = 12$), so it is an **alkene** (a hexene). $\ce{C8H18}$ fits $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$, so it is an alkane.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: more than one alkene molecule]
Hexadecane, $\ce{C16H34}$, is cracked into decane, $\ce{C10H22}$, and ethene only. How many molecules of ethene form from each molecule of hexadecane?

1. Carbon left over: $16 - 10 = 6$; hydrogen left over: $34 - 22 = 12$.
2. Each ethene molecule is $\ce{C2H4}$: $6 \div 2 = 3$ and $12 \div 4 = 3$.
3. Equation: $\ce{C16H34 -> C10H22 + 3C2H4}$.
:::

Notice that there are never enough hydrogen atoms for **all** the products to be alkanes: an alkane has two "extra" hydrogen atoms ($2n + 2$), and splitting one molecule in two would need two more. So cracking always produces at least one alkene (or hydrogen). Ethane can be cracked to give ethene and hydrogen: $\ce{C2H6 -> C2H4 + H2}$.

## Cracking in the laboratory

Liquid paraffin (a mixture of long-chain alkanes) can be cracked in a school laboratory.

1. Soak some ceramic (mineral) wool in liquid paraffin and push it to the closed end of a hard-glass boiling tube.
2. Place the catalyst, such as broken porcelain or aluminium oxide granules, in the middle of the tube. Clamp the tube horizontally.
3. Fit a stopper and delivery tube that leads under water in a trough, with a test tube full of water upside down over its end.
4. Heat the **catalyst** strongly, then flick the flame briefly onto the wool so that paraffin vapour passes over the hot catalyst.
5. Collect several tubes of gas. Throw away the first tube, which is mostly air pushed out of the apparatus.
6. Shake a tube of the gas with a few drops of bromine water.

:::example{title="Results and what they show"}
The collected gas is colourless and burns. Bromine water shaken with it changes from **orange to colourless**, showing that the gas contains an **alkene**. Liquid paraffin itself does not decolourise bromine water, because it contains only saturated alkanes. So the cracking made a new, unsaturated substance: a chemical change.
:::

:::note{title="Safety"}
Wear eye protection. Before you stop heating, lift the delivery tube out of the water; otherwise cold water is **sucked back** into the hot tube and can crack it. The gas is flammable. Some schools crack cooking oil in the same way: heating oil strongly makes irritating fumes, so work in a well-ventilated room.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing cracking with fractional distillation"}
Fractional distillation is a **physical** separation of a mixture by boiling point. Cracking is a **chemical** reaction that breaks covalent bonds and makes new compounds.
:::

:::mistake{title="Unbalanced hydrogen"}
Students often write $\ce{C10H22 -> C5H12 + C5H12}$. Count the hydrogen: 22 on the left but 24 on the right. One product must be an alkene: $\ce{C10H22 -> C5H12 + C5H10}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the catalyst"}
"Heating" alone is not the full answer. State a **high temperature and a catalyst** (aluminium oxide or silica).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Describe how alkenes are manufactured" means: cracking of long-chain alkanes (from crude oil), high temperature, catalyst. Then give an example equation.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Explain why cracking is necessary" needs both reasons: **demand** for short-chain fuels such as petrol is greater than supply, and **alkenes** are needed to make polymers and other chemicals.
:::

:::exam-tip
To check a cracking equation, add up the carbon atoms and the hydrogen atoms on each side. Then check that at least one product fits $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Cracking: long-chain alkanes → shorter alkanes + alkenes (+ hydrogen), at a high temperature with a catalyst.
- Needed because demand for petrol exceeds supply and alkenes are needed for polymers and ethanol.
- In equations, C and H must balance; at least one product is an alkene (or hydrogen).
- Laboratory: paraffin on ceramic wool, heated catalyst, gas collected over water; bromine water turns orange → colourless.
- Remove the delivery tube from the water before you stop heating (suck-back).
:::
