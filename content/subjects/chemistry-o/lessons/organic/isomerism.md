## Introduction

The gas in an LPG cylinder is mostly propane and butane. Butane, $\ce{C4H10}$, can be built in two ways: four carbon atoms in a straight chain, or three in a chain with the fourth attached to the middle one as a branch. Both molecules have the formula $\ce{C4H10}$, but they are different compounds with different boiling points. Compounds like these are called **isomers**.

Isomerism explains why there are so many organic compounds: the number of possible isomers grows quickly with the number of carbon atoms. This lesson is **Cambridge Supplement** content (the isomers of $\ce{C4H10}$ and $\ce{C4H8}$) and **ZIMSEC Form 4** content (the isomers of alkanes with up to six carbon atoms). You should already be able to name alkanes, alkenes and alcohols and draw displayed formulae.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Structural isomers"}
Compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae.
:::

:::definition{term="Isomerism"}
The existence of two or more compounds with the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms.
:::

:::definition{term="Branched chain"}
A carbon chain with at least one carbon atom joined to the side of the main chain, such as the methyl group, $\ce{-CH3}$, in methylpropane.
:::

## Drawing carbon skeletons

Isomers are easiest to find by drawing only the carbon atoms (the **carbon skeleton**) and adding the hydrogen atoms afterwards, so that every carbon has four bonds.

![Displayed formulae of the two isomers of C4H10: butane, a straight chain of four carbon atoms with a boiling point of −1 °C, and methylpropane, a chain of three carbon atoms with a CH3 branch on the middle carbon and a boiling point of −12 °C; the same molecular formula but different structural formulae.](media:diagrams/isomers-butane-methylpropane.png "Figure: structural isomers of C₄H₁₀")

### The isomers of $\ce{C4H10}$ (Supplement and ZIMSEC)

```
C - C - C - C          C - C - C
                           |
                           C

  butane              methylpropane
```

| Name | Structural formula | Boiling point / °C |
| --- | --- | :---: |
| butane | $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH3}$ | −1 |
| methylpropane | $\ce{CH3CH(CH3)CH3}$ | −12 |

Both are $\ce{C4H10}$ and both are alkanes, so they burn in the same way and react with chlorine in the same way. Their **physical** properties differ: the branched isomer has the lower boiling point, because its more compact molecules have weaker forces between them.

### The isomers of $\ce{C4H8}$ (Supplement and ZIMSEC)

Moving the double bond along the chain gives a different compound:

| Name | Structural formula |
| --- | --- |
| but-1-ene | $\ce{CH2=CHCH2CH3}$ |
| but-2-ene | $\ce{CH3CH=CHCH3}$ |

:::note{title="Other isomers of C₄H₈"}
The branched alkene methylpropene, $\ce{CH2=C(CH3)CH3}$, is also an isomer of $\ce{C4H8}$, and so are two ring compounds. The syllabuses only expect but-1-ene and but-2-ene.
:::

### Isomers in different homologous series

Isomers do not have to be in the same series. Propanoic acid, $\ce{CH3CH2COOH}$, and the ester methyl ethanoate, $\ce{CH3COOCH3}$, are both $\ce{C3H6O2}$. They are isomers with very different properties: the acid is sour and reacts with carbonates, while the ester has a sweet smell and does not.

## Isomers of alkanes with five and six carbon atoms (ZIMSEC)

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the isomers of pentane, $\ce{C5H12}$]
1. Start with the straight chain of five carbons: C–C–C–C–C, **pentane**.
2. Shorten the chain to four carbons and put one carbon on as a branch. On carbon 2 this gives **2-methylbutane**. (A branch on carbon 3 is the same molecule numbered from the other end, and a branch on carbon 1 just makes the straight chain again.)
3. Shorten the chain to three carbons and put two carbons on the middle carbon: **2,2-dimethylpropane**.
4. A chain of two carbons cannot hold three branches without making a longer chain, so there are **three** isomers of $\ce{C5H12}$.
:::

```
C - C - C - C - C      C - C - C - C          C
                           |                  |
                           C              C - C - C
                                              |
                                              C

    pentane           2-methylbutane    2,2-dimethylpropane
```

Hexane, $\ce{C6H14}$, has **five** isomers:

| Name | Structural formula | Boiling point / °C |
| --- | --- | :---: |
| hexane | $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2CH2CH3}$ | 69 |
| 2-methylpentane | $\ce{CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH2CH3}$ | 60 |
| 3-methylpentane | $\ce{CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH2CH3}$ | 63 |
| 2,3-dimethylbutane | $\ce{CH3CH(CH3)CH(CH3)CH3}$ | 58 |
| 2,2-dimethylbutane | $\ce{CH3C(CH3)2CH2CH3}$ | 50 |

The more branched the molecule, the lower the boiling point.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: are they isomers?]
Decide whether each pair are isomers: (a) $\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH}$ and $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3}$; (b) $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH3}$ and $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2CH3}$.

1. (a) Count atoms: both are $\ce{C3H8O}$. The –OH is on an end carbon in one and the middle carbon in the other, so the structures differ: **isomers** (propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol).
2. (b) $\ce{C4H10}$ and $\ce{C5H12}$ have different molecular formulae: **not isomers**. They are neighbouring members of the alkane series.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Drawing the same molecule twice"}
Bending a chain does not make a new isomer: C–C–C–C drawn with a kink is still butane. Always find the **longest continuous chain** (it may turn corners) and name the molecule; two drawings with the same name are the same compound.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting a methyl group on the end carbon"}
A $\ce{-CH3}$ "branch" on the first carbon of a chain simply makes the chain one carbon longer. "1-methylbutane" is really pentane.
:::

:::mistake{title="Numbering branches from the wrong end"}
$\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH(CH3)CH3}$ is 2-methylpentane, not 4-methylpentane: number from the end nearer the branch.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking isomers have identical properties"}
Isomers are different compounds. They have different physical properties (such as boiling point), and if they are in different homologous series they also have different chemical properties.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Define structural isomers" needs both halves: **same molecular formula** and **different structural formulae** (or different arrangements of atoms). "Same formula" alone does not score.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to draw isomers, check each one: count the carbon and hydrogen atoms, check every carbon has four bonds, and name each one to make sure no two are the same molecule.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Suggest why the isomers have different boiling points": the branched isomer is more compact, so the forces between its molecules are weaker and it boils at a lower temperature.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Structural isomers: same molecular formula, different structural formulae.
- $\ce{C4H10}$: butane and methylpropane; $\ce{C4H8}$: but-1-ene and but-2-ene (Supplement and ZIMSEC).
- ZIMSEC: $\ce{C5H12}$ has 3 isomers (pentane, 2-methylbutane, 2,2-dimethylpropane) and $\ce{C6H14}$ has 5.
- Isomers can belong to different series, e.g. propanoic acid and methyl ethanoate ($\ce{C3H6O2}$).
- Branching lowers the boiling point; isomers in the same series have similar chemical properties.
:::
