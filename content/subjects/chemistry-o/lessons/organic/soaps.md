## Introduction

Bar soap is made all over Zimbabwe. Large factories make it from vegetable oils and animal fats, and many community groups and school enterprise clubs make and sell their own soap from cooking oil and caustic soda (sodium hydroxide). Long before factories, soap was made at home by boiling animal fat with an alkaline liquid soaked out of wood ash.

Every one of these methods uses the same reaction, **saponification**. In this lesson (ZIMSEC Form 4) you will learn what fats and oils are, how they react with an alkali to make soap and glycerol, how a soap particle is built, why soap removes grease and how to make soap safely. You should already know about esters and about hard water.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fats and oils"}
Esters made from glycerol (an alcohol with three –OH groups) and long-chain carboxylic acids called fatty acids. Fats are solid at room temperature; oils are liquid.
:::

:::definition{term="Saponification"}
The reaction in which a fat or oil is heated with an alkali, such as sodium hydroxide, to form soap and glycerol. It is the alkaline hydrolysis (breaking down) of the ester.
:::

:::definition{term="Soap"}
The sodium (or potassium) salt of a long-chain fatty acid, for example sodium stearate, $\ce{C17H35COONa}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Glycerol"}
Propane-1,2,3-triol, $\ce{C3H5(OH)3}$: the by-product of soap making. It is also called glycerine.
:::

## The reaction

$$
\text{fat or oil} + \text{sodium hydroxide} \longrightarrow \text{soap} + \text{glycerol}
$$

Each fat molecule has three fatty-acid chains joined to glycerol by ester links, so it reacts with **three** hydroxide ions. For a fat made from stearic acid:

$$
\ce{(C17H35COO)3C3H5 + 3NaOH -> 3C17H35COONa + C3H5(OH)3}
$$

glyceryl tristearate + sodium hydroxide → sodium stearate (soap) + glycerol

Using potassium hydroxide instead gives potassium soaps, which are softer and are used in liquid soaps. Wood-ash lye contains potassium compounds, so traditional ash soaps are soft.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how much sodium hydroxide?]
Calculate the mass of sodium hydroxide needed to saponify 89 g of glyceryl tristearate completely. ($M_r$: glyceryl tristearate = 890, NaOH = 40)

1. $n(\text{fat}) = 89 \div 890 = 0.10$ mol.
2. 1 mol of fat reacts with 3 mol of NaOH: $n(\ce{NaOH}) = 0.30$ mol.
3. Mass $= 0.30 \times 40 = 12$ g.
:::

## The structure of a soap particle

A soap particle has two very different ends:

| Part | Structure | Behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **tail** | a long hydrocarbon chain, such as $\ce{C17H35\bond{-}}$ | **hydrophobic** ("water-hating"): does not dissolve in water but dissolves in grease and oil |
| **head** | the ionic group $\ce{-COO^-}$ with a $\ce{Na+}$ ion | **hydrophilic** ("water-loving"): attracted to water molecules |

```
CH3-CH2-CH2-CH2- ... -CH2-CH2-COO⁻  Na⁺
[ long hydrocarbon tail: oil-loving ] [ ionic head: water-loving ]
```

## How soap removes grease

:::example{title="Example 2: washing a greasy plate"}
1. The hydrocarbon **tails** dissolve in the grease; the ionic **heads** stay in the water.
2. Rubbing or stirring breaks the grease into tiny droplets, each surrounded by soap particles with their heads on the outside.
3. Each droplet's surface is covered in negative charges, so the droplets **repel** one another and do not join up again.
4. The droplets stay spread through the water (the grease is **emulsified**) and are rinsed away.
:::

## Making soap in the laboratory

1. Put about 10 cm³ of vegetable (cooking) oil in a beaker and add about 15 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution. A little ethanol may be added to help the oil and the alkali mix.
2. Heat gently, stirring all the time, for about 15–20 minutes. Add a little water if the mixture starts to dry out.
3. Add saturated sodium chloride solution and stir. The soap is much less soluble in salt water, so it separates and floats as a solid curd (**salting out**).
4. Skim or filter off the soap, wash it with a little cold water to remove excess alkali, press it into a mould and leave it to dry.

:::note{title="Safety"}
Sodium hydroxide is **corrosive**: it burns skin and can blind. Wear eye protection and gloves, and wash any splashes off at once with plenty of water. Hot oil can spit, so heat gently and stir. Ethanol is flammable: if it is used, heat with an electric hot plate or a water bath rather than a naked flame. Soap that still contains a lot of sodium hydroxide is harmful to skin, so test its pH before use.
:::

Glycerol, the by-product, is separated from the salty liquid and used in skin creams, cosmetics, medicines, food and the manufacture of explosives.

## Soap and hard water

Hard water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium ions. They react with soap to form an insoluble grey **scum** (calcium or magnesium salts of the fatty acids):

$$
\ce{2C17H35COO^- + Ca^2+ -> (C17H35COO)2Ca}
$$

Soap is wasted until all these ions have been removed as scum; only then does a lather form. That is why hard water needs more soap.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying soap is an ester"}
The **fat or oil** is the ester. Saponification breaks the ester links, and the soap is a **salt** (sodium stearate).
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the two ends of a soap particle"}
The **tail** dissolves in grease; the **head** dissolves in water. Answers that say "the head dissolves in the grease" lose the mark.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the by-product"}
Saponification gives two products: soap **and glycerol**. Water is not the by-product.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Fats and oils are esters of glycerol and fatty acids.
- Saponification: fat or oil + sodium hydroxide → soap + glycerol (heat).
- A soap particle has a hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail and a hydrophilic ionic head ($\ce{-COO^-}$ $\ce{Na+}$).
- Soap emulsifies grease into small, negatively charged droplets that repel each other and rinse away.
- Make soap safely (corrosive alkali, hot oil, flammable ethanol); salt it out with brine; hard water forms scum with soap.
:::
