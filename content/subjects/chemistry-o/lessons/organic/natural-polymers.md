## Introduction

Nylon and PET are made in factories, but living things made polymers long before chemists did. The protein in beans, eggs and kapenta, and the starch in sadza, rice and potatoes, are **natural polymers**. Your body breaks them down during digestion and builds new proteins from the pieces.

This lesson covers **proteins** (Cambridge Supplement and ZIMSEC Form 4) and, for **ZIMSEC**, **starch**, the linkages in both, and how they are broken down by **hydrolysis**, with the products identified by chromatography. You should already know about condensation polymers, amide links and paper chromatography.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Amino acid"}
A molecule with an amine group, $\ce{-NH2}$, and a carboxylic acid group, $\ce{-COOH}$, on the same carbon atom: general structure $\ce{H2N-CHR-COOH}$, where R is a side chain that differs from one amino acid to another.
:::

:::definition{term="Protein"}
A natural polyamide: a condensation polymer of amino acids joined by amide links.
:::

:::definition{term="Amide (peptide) link"}
The $\ce{-CO-NH\bond{-}}$ group that joins amino acid units in a protein (and the units of nylon).
:::

:::definition{term="Hydrolysis (ZIMSEC)"}
Breaking a compound down by reaction with water. Hydrolysis of a polymer breaks its links and gives back the monomers.
:::

## Proteins

An amino acid has its two reactive groups on the same molecule:

```
    H   H   O
    |   |   ||
H - N - C - C - O - H
        |
        R
```

The $\ce{-COOH}$ group of one amino acid reacts with the $\ce{-NH2}$ group of the next. Water is lost, and an **amide link** forms, exactly as in nylon. Because every amino acid has both groups, the chain can keep growing at each end. A protein is a chain of hundreds or thousands of amino acid units, drawn in block form as:

$$
\ce{-NH-CHR-CO-NH-CHR'-CO-NH-CHR''-CO\bond{-}}
$$

There are about 20 different amino acids in proteins, with different R groups, and their **order** along the chain makes each protein different.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: joining three amino acids]
Three amino acids, with side chains $\ce{R1}$, $\ce{R2}$ and $\ce{R3}$, join in that order. Draw the section of chain and say how many water molecules are released.

1. The $\ce{-COOH}$ of the first reacts with the $\ce{-NH2}$ of the second; the $\ce{-COOH}$ of the second reacts with the $\ce{-NH2}$ of the third.
2. Section: $\ce{H2N-CHR1-CO-NH-CHR2-CO-NH-CHR3-COOH}$.
3. Two amide links form, so **2** water molecules are released (one fewer than the number of amino acids in a single chain).
:::

:::note{title="Nylon and proteins compared"}
Both are polyamides with $\ce{-CONH\bond{-}}$ links. Nylon is made from two monomers (a dicarboxylic acid and a diamine) and the same unit repeats along the chain. A protein is made from one kind of monomer, the amino acid, but from many different amino acids, so its units are not all the same.
:::

## Starch (ZIMSEC)

Starch is a polymer of **glucose**, $\ce{C6H12O6}$. Plants make glucose by photosynthesis and join thousands of glucose units into starch to store energy. Each time two glucose units join, a molecule of **water** is lost (condensation), and the units are linked through an oxygen atom: a **glycosidic link**, $\ce{-O\bond{-}}$ (sometimes called an ether link).

In block form, with G for each glucose unit:

```
-O-[G]-O-[G]-O-[G]-O-
```

Its formula can be written $\ce{(C6H10O5)_n}$: each unit is glucose minus water.

## Hydrolysis of natural polymers (ZIMSEC)

Hydrolysis is the reverse of condensation: water is added back across each link.

| Polymer | Conditions for hydrolysis | Products |
| --- | --- | --- |
| starch | boil with dilute hydrochloric acid, or use the enzyme amylase at about 37 °C | glucose (amylase gives maltose, a sugar made of two glucose units) |
| protein | heat with concentrated hydrochloric acid for several hours, or use protease enzymes | amino acids |

$$
\ce{(C6H10O5)_n + nH2O -> nC6H12O6}
$$

:::worked-solution[Example 2: following the hydrolysis of starch]
1. Boil starch solution with dilute hydrochloric acid in a water bath.
2. Every few minutes, put a drop of the mixture on a white tile with a drop of iodine solution.
3. At first the iodine turns **blue-black**: starch is present.
4. As hydrolysis continues the colour fades; when the iodine stays **orange-brown**, all the starch has been broken down into glucose.
:::

## Identifying the products by chromatography (ZIMSEC)

The mixture of amino acids (or sugars) from hydrolysis is spotted on chromatography paper next to spots of known pure substances, and a solvent is allowed to move up the paper. Amino acids are colourless, so the dried paper is sprayed with a **locating agent** (ninhydrin), which turns them into purple spots. Each substance is identified by its $R_f$ value:

$$
R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by the spot}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent front}}
$$

:::worked-solution[Example 3: identifying an amino acid]
A spot from a hydrolysed protein moved 3.0 cm while the solvent front moved 7.5 cm. On the same paper, standard amino acids gave $R_f$ values of P 0.25, Q 0.40 and S 0.62.

1. $R_f = 3.0 \div 7.5 = 0.40$.
2. This matches Q, so the protein contained amino acid Q.
3. A spot is only identified when it matches a standard run on the same paper with the same solvent.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the protein link an ester link"}
Proteins and nylon have **amide** links, $\ce{-CO-NH\bond{-}}$. Ester links, $\ce{-CO-O\bond{-}}$, are in polyesters and fats.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting water in hydrolysis"}
Hydrolysis **uses** water (one molecule for each link broken); condensation **releases** water.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying iodine turns blue-black when the starch has gone"}
Blue-black means starch is **present**. When hydrolysis is complete, the iodine stays orange-brown.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Describe the structure of a protein" (Supplement): amino acid monomers, $\ce{H2N-CHR-COOH}$, joined by amide links, $\ce{-CONH\bond{-}}$, with water released when each link forms.
:::

:::exam-tip
In chromatography questions, measure both distances from the **baseline**, give $R_f$ to 2 decimal places, and compare with standards run on the same paper.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Amino acids, $\ce{H2N-CHR-COOH}$, join by condensation to form proteins, natural polyamides with amide ($\ce{-CONH\bond{-}}$) links.
- Proteins differ in the number, type and order of their amino acids (about 20 kinds).
- ZIMSEC: starch is a condensation polymer of glucose with glycosidic ($\ce{-O\bond{-}}$) links, $\ce{(C6H10O5)_n}$.
- ZIMSEC: hydrolysis adds water back: starch → glucose (dilute acid or amylase); protein → amino acids (concentrated acid or proteases).
- ZIMSEC: products are identified by chromatography and $R_f$ values; a locating agent shows colourless amino acids.
:::
