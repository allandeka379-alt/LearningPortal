## Introduction

Ethene is a small molecule with a big job. It is the starting material for poly(ethene), the plastic of bags and bottles, and for much of the world's industrial ethanol. Plants make it too: ethene is the gas that makes fruit ripen, which is why bananas ripen faster in a closed bag.

What makes ethene and the other alkenes useful is their **carbon–carbon double bond**. It lets other molecules add on, so alkenes take part in many reactions that alkanes cannot. In this lesson you will learn the test that tells an alkene from an alkane, how alkenes burn and (Supplement; ZIMSEC Form 4) their addition reactions with bromine, hydrogen and steam. You should already know the general formula of the alkenes, $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$, and how cracking makes them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Alkene"}
An unsaturated hydrocarbon containing a carbon–carbon double bond, $\ce{C=C}$; general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Unsaturated"}
Containing at least one carbon–carbon double bond, so that more atoms can be added to the molecule.
:::

:::definition{term="Addition reaction (Supplement)"}
A reaction in which two molecules join to form a single product. With an alkene, atoms add across the double bond, which becomes a single bond.
:::

## The structure of alkenes

```
    H   H              H   H   H
    |   |              |   |   |
    C = C          H - C - C = C
    |   |              |       |
    H   H              H       H

    ethene               propene
```

The first two alkenes are ethene, $\ce{CH2=CH2}$, and propene, $\ce{CH3CH=CH2}$. Each carbon atom still forms four bonds: the double bond counts as two.

## Testing for an alkene

Shake the substance with a few drops of **bromine water** (aqueous bromine, which is orange).

| Substance | Observation | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| an alkene | orange → **colourless** | bromine adds across the C=C bond |
| an alkane | stays **orange** | no double bond for bromine to add to |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: telling two gases apart]
Two gas jars contain ethane and ethene. Describe how to find out which is which.

1. Add a little bromine water to each gas jar, put the lid back on and shake.
2. In one jar the bromine water turns from orange to colourless: this gas is **ethene** (an alkene).
3. In the other jar the bromine water stays orange: this gas is **ethane** (an alkane).
:::

## Combustion

Like all hydrocarbons, alkenes burn to give carbon dioxide and water in plenty of air:

$$
\ce{C2H4 + 3O2 -> 2CO2 + 2H2O}
$$

Alkenes contain a higher proportion of carbon than alkanes with the same number of carbon atoms, so they often burn with a smokier flame. They are too valuable as chemical feedstocks to be used as fuels.

## Addition reactions (Supplement; ZIMSEC)

In each reaction, one molecule adds across the double bond and the product is **saturated**.

### With bromine

$$
\ce{CH2=CH2 + Br2 -> CH2BrCH2Br}
$$

The product is **1,2-dibromoethane**. The same reaction is the bromine water test.

### With hydrogen (hydrogenation)

$$
\ce{CH2=CH2 + H2 -> CH3CH3}
$$

Conditions: a **nickel catalyst** and a temperature of about 150 °C. The alkene becomes an alkane.

The same reaction is used to make **margarine**. Vegetable oils, such as sunflower and soya bean oil, contain unsaturated molecules and are liquid at room temperature. Adding hydrogen to some of their double bonds raises their melting point, so the oil becomes a soft solid that can be spread.

### With steam (hydration)

$$
\ce{CH2=CH2 + H2O -> CH3CH2OH}
$$

Conditions: **steam** at about **300 °C** and **60 atm** (6000 kPa) with an **acid catalyst** (phosphoric acid). This is one of the industrial methods of making ethanol.

:::worked-solution[Example 2 (Supplement): bromine and propene]
Give the product when propene reacts with bromine, and draw its displayed formula.

1. The two bromine atoms add to the two carbon atoms of the double bond: $\ce{CH3CH=CH2 + Br2 -> CH3CHBrCH2Br}$.
2. The product is 1,2-dibromopropane, with one bromine atom on each of the two carbon atoms that were joined by the double bond.
3. Check: one product only, and every carbon now has four single bonds (displayed formula below).
:::

```
    H   H   H
    |   |   |
H - C - C - C - H
    |   |   |
    H   Br  Br
```

:::worked-solution[Example 3: mass of bromine that reacts]
Calculate the mass of bromine, $\ce{Br2}$, that reacts with 2.8 g of ethene. ($A_r$: H = 1, C = 12, Br = 80)

1. $M_r(\ce{C2H4}) = 28$, so $n = 2.8 \div 28 = 0.10$ mol.
2. $\ce{C2H4 + Br2 -> C2H4Br2}$: the ratio is 1 : 1, so 0.10 mol of $\ce{Br2}$ reacts.
3. $M_r(\ce{Br2}) = 160$; mass $= 0.10 \times 160 = 16$ g.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying bromine water goes \"clear\""}
Clear means see-through, and bromine water is already clear. The correct word is **colourless**: orange → colourless.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing two products for an addition reaction"}
$\ce{C2H4 + Br2 -> C2H3Br + HBr}$ is wrong: that would be substitution. Addition gives **one** product, $\ce{C2H4Br2}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming the product \"dibromoethene\""}
After addition the double bond has gone, so the product is an alkane derivative: 1,2-dibromo**ethane**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the catalysts"}
Hydrogen: **nickel**. Steam: **acid** (phosphoric acid). Fermentation uses yeast, not either of these.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Describe a test for an unsaturated hydrocarbon": name the reagent (bromine water or aqueous bromine), give the colour **before and after** (orange to colourless), and say what an alkane does (stays orange).
:::

:::exam-tip
For each addition reaction learn three things: the reagent, the conditions and the product. Hydrogen: Ni, about 150 °C, alkane. Steam: 300 °C, 60 atm, acid catalyst, alcohol.
:::

:::exam-tip
When drawing an addition product, put one added atom on **each** carbon of the former double bond.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Alkenes, $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$, are unsaturated: they have a C=C double bond.
- Test: bromine water turns from orange to colourless with an alkene; it stays orange with an alkane.
- Alkenes burn to carbon dioxide and water: $\ce{C2H4 + 3O2 -> 2CO2 + 2H2O}$.
- Supplement; ZIMSEC: addition gives one product: + $\ce{Br2}$ → dibromoalkane; + $\ce{H2}$ (Ni) → alkane (margarine); + steam (300 °C, 60 atm, acid) → alcohol.
:::
