## Introduction

The gas in an LPG cylinder, the ethanol blended into petrol, the vinegar on your chips, the plastic of a water bottle and the bar of soap by the sink all have one thing in common: they are **organic compounds**, compounds built around chains of carbon atoms. Millions of them are known, so chemists sort them into families called **homologous series**. Learn how one member of a family behaves and you can predict how the others behave.

In this lesson you will learn the different ways of writing the formula of an organic compound, the four homologous series in the O-Level and IGCSE syllabuses (alkanes, alkenes, alcohols and carboxylic acids) and how to tell saturated compounds from unsaturated ones. You should already know that a covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons and that carbon has four outer-shell electrons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Hydrocarbon"}
A compound that contains hydrogen and carbon only.
:::

:::definition{term="Homologous series"}
A family of organic compounds with the same functional group and the same general formula, and therefore similar chemical properties.
:::

:::definition{term="Functional group"}
The atom or group of atoms that decides the chemical properties of a homologous series, for example $\ce{C=C}$ in alkenes and $\ce{-OH}$ in alcohols.
:::

:::definition{term="Saturated compound"}
A compound whose molecules contain only single carbon–carbon bonds.
:::

:::definition{term="Unsaturated compound"}
A compound whose molecules contain at least one carbon–carbon bond that is not single (in this syllabus, a $\ce{C=C}$ double bond).
:::

:::keyfact
In every organic molecule each carbon atom forms **four** covalent bonds, each hydrogen atom forms **one** and each oxygen atom forms **two**.
:::

## Four ways to write a formula

Take ethanol, the alcohol in beer and in fuel blends.

| Type of formula | What it shows | Ethanol |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Molecular formula | The number of atoms of each element in one molecule | $\ce{C2H6O}$ |
| Structural formula (Supplement) | How the atoms are grouped, unambiguously, without drawing every bond | $\ce{CH3CH2OH}$ |
| Displayed formula | Every atom and every bond | drawn below |
| General formula | A formula for every member of the series | $\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}OH}$ |

The displayed formula of ethanol is drawn like this:

```
    H   H
    |   |
H - C - C - O - H
    |   |
    H   H
```

Each carbon atom has four bonds (three to hydrogen or carbon, one to the next atom along), the oxygen has two and each hydrogen has one. Notice the bond between O and H: it must be drawn. The structural formulae of other compounds you will meet are $\ce{CH2=CH2}$ (ethene), $\ce{CH3COOH}$ (ethanoic acid) and $\ce{CH3COOCH3}$ (methyl ethanoate, an ester).

## The four homologous series

| Series | Functional group | General formula | First member | Name ends in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alkanes | only $\ce{C-C}$ and $\ce{C-H}$ single bonds | $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$ | methane, $\ce{CH4}$ | -ane |
| Alkenes | $\ce{C=C}$ double bond | $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$ | ethene, $\ce{C2H4}$ | -ene |
| Alcohols | $\ce{-OH}$ (hydroxyl) | $\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}OH}$ | methanol, $\ce{CH3OH}$ | -ol |
| Carboxylic acids | $\ce{-COOH}$ (carboxyl) | $\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}COOH}$ | methanoic acid, $\ce{HCOOH}$ | -oic acid |

Two details to watch. Alkenes start at two carbon atoms, because a $\ce{C=C}$ bond needs two carbons. In the carboxylic acid formula, $n$ counts the carbon atoms **outside** the $\ce{-COOH}$ group, so methanoic acid has $n = 0$ and ethanoic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH}$, has $n = 1$ but two carbon atoms in total.

The displayed formula of ethanoic acid shows the carboxyl group: a carbon atom with a double bond to one oxygen atom and a single bond to an $\ce{-OH}$ group.

```
    H    O
    |   //
H - C - C
    |    \
    H     O - H
```

## Characteristics of a homologous series (Supplement)

All the members of a homologous series:

1. have the **same functional group**;
2. fit the **same general formula**;
3. differ from the next member by a $\ce{-CH2\bond{-}}$ unit (so their relative molecular masses go up in steps of 14);
4. show a **trend in physical properties**, such as boiling point rising as the chain gets longer;
5. have **similar chemical properties**, because they have the same functional group.

:::example{title="The trend in boiling points of the alkanes"}
| Alkane | Formula | Boiling point / °C | State at 25 °C |
| --- | --- | :---: | --- |
| methane | $\ce{CH4}$ | −162 | gas |
| ethane | $\ce{C2H6}$ | −89 | gas |
| propane | $\ce{C3H8}$ | −42 | gas |
| butane | $\ce{C4H10}$ | −1 | gas |
| pentane | $\ce{C5H12}$ | 36 | liquid |
| hexane | $\ce{C6H14}$ | 69 | liquid |

Each extra $\ce{CH2}$ unit makes the molecules bigger, so the forces between them are stronger and more energy is needed to separate them. Propane and butane are gases at room temperature, but they are easily liquefied under pressure, which is why a lot of LPG fits into a small cylinder.

:::

![Bar chart of alkane boiling points rising with chain length: methane −162, ethane −89, propane −42, butane −1, pentane 36 and hexane 69 degrees Celsius; the first four are gases at 25 degrees Celsius and pentane and hexane are liquids.](media:diagrams/alkane-boiling-points-trend.png "Figure: boiling points of the first six alkanes against 25 °C room temperature")

## Saturated and unsaturated compounds

Ethane, $\ce{CH3CH3}$, has only single bonds: it is **saturated**. Ethene, $\ce{CH2=CH2}$, has a double bond: it is **unsaturated**. Unsaturated compounds can add extra atoms across the double bond, which is why alkenes are much more reactive than alkanes. All alkanes are saturated and all alkenes are unsaturated.

```
    H   H               H   H
    |   |               |   |
H - C - C - H           C = C
    |   |               |   |
    H   H               H   H

  ethane (saturated)   ethene (unsaturated)
```

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Example 1: using general formulae]
Write the molecular formulae of (a) the alkane with 9 carbon atoms, (b) the alkene with 5 carbon atoms and (c) the alcohol with 4 carbon atoms.

1. Alkane, $n = 9$: $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$ gives $2 \times 9 + 2 = 20$ hydrogen atoms, so $\ce{C9H20}$.
2. Alkene, $n = 5$: $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$ gives $2 \times 5 = 10$ hydrogen atoms, so $\ce{C5H10}$.
3. Alcohol, $n = 4$: $\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}OH}$ gives $\ce{C4H9OH}$ (molecular formula $\ce{C4H10O}$).
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: classifying compounds]
Classify $\ce{C3H8}$, $\ce{C6H12}$, $\ce{C2H5OH}$, $\ce{C3H7COOH}$ and $\ce{C5H12}$, and say which are saturated hydrocarbons.

1. $\ce{C3H8}$: $2 \times 3 + 2 = 8$, so it fits $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$: an **alkane** (propane).
2. $\ce{C6H12}$: $2 \times 6 = 12$, so it fits $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$: an **alkene** (a hexene).
3. $\ce{C2H5OH}$ has an $\ce{-OH}$ group: an **alcohol** (ethanol).
4. $\ce{C3H7COOH}$ has a $\ce{-COOH}$ group: a **carboxylic acid** with four carbon atoms (butanoic acid).
5. $\ce{C5H12}$ fits $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$: an **alkane** (pentane).
6. The saturated hydrocarbons are the alkanes, $\ce{C3H8}$ and $\ce{C5H12}$. Ethanol and butanoic acid are not hydrocarbons, because they contain oxygen.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Drawing carbon with three or five bonds"}
Count the bonds around every carbon atom in a displayed formula: there must be exactly four (a double bond counts as two). Hydrogen always has exactly one bond.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaving out the O–H bond"}
In a displayed formula **every** bond is shown, including the bond between O and H in $\ce{-OH}$ and $\ce{-COOH}$. Writing "–OH" at the end of a displayed formula loses the mark.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking alcohols are alkalis"}
The $\ce{-OH}$ in an alcohol is a covalently bonded hydroxyl group, not a hydroxide ion, $\ce{OH-}$. Ethanol solution is neutral.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying members of a series have the same physical properties"}
Members of a homologous series have **similar chemical** properties but a **trend** in physical properties (boiling point, density and viscosity change with chain length).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Draw the displayed formula" means every atom and every bond. "Give the structural formula" means a formula like $\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH}$ that shows the arrangement without drawing every bond. Read which one the question asks for.
:::

:::exam-tip
To decide the series from a molecular formula, test it against the general formulae: $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$ for alkanes and $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$ for alkenes. Then look for $\ce{-OH}$ or $\ce{-COOH}$ in a structural formula.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "state two characteristics of a homologous series" (Supplement), give two of: same functional group, same general formula, successive members differ by $\ce{CH2}$, trend in physical properties, similar chemical properties.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Organic compounds have carbon chains; hydrocarbons contain hydrogen and carbon only.
- Formulae: molecular ($\ce{C2H6O}$), structural ($\ce{CH3CH2OH}$), displayed (every atom and bond) and general ($\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}OH}$).
- Alkanes $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$, alkenes $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$, alcohols $\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}OH}$, carboxylic acids $\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}COOH}$.
- A homologous series has the same functional group and general formula; Supplement: members differ by $\ce{CH2}$, show a trend in physical properties and have similar chemical properties.
- Saturated: only $\ce{C-C}$ single bonds (alkanes); unsaturated: a $\ce{C=C}$ double bond (alkenes).
:::
