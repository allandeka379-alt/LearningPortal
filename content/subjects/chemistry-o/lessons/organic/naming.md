## Introduction

Read the label on a gas cylinder and you may see "butane" or "propane". Methylated spirits, sold in hardware shops in Harare and Bulawayo, is mostly ethanol. Vinegar is a solution of ethanoic acid. Each of these names is a code: it tells a chemist how many carbon atoms are in the chain and which homologous series the compound belongs to. With a few rules you can turn any simple name into a formula, and any formula into a name.

This lesson builds on the four homologous series (alkanes, alkenes, alcohols and carboxylic acids) and their general formulae. Cambridge Core expects the names of methane, ethane, ethene, ethanol, ethanoic acid and the products of their reactions; Cambridge Supplement extends this to unbranched compounds with up to four carbon atoms and to esters; ZIMSEC expects compounds with up to six carbon atoms.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Stem"}
The first part of a name, which gives the number of carbon atoms in the longest chain: meth- (1), eth- (2), prop- (3), but- (4), pent- (5), hex- (6).
:::

:::definition{term="Ending (suffix)"}
The last part of a name, which gives the homologous series: -ane (alkane), -ene (alkene), -ol (alcohol), -oic acid (carboxylic acid).
:::

:::definition{term="Position number"}
A number in the name that shows which carbon atom of the chain carries the functional group, as in propan-2-ol. The chain is numbered from the end nearer the functional group.
:::

## Stems and endings

| Carbon atoms | Stem | Alkane | Alkene | Alcohol | Carboxylic acid |
| :---: | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | meth- | methane | none | methanol | methanoic acid |
| 2 | eth- | ethane | ethene | ethanol | ethanoic acid |
| 3 | prop- | propane | propene | propanol | propanoic acid |
| 4 | but- | butane | butene | butanol | butanoic acid |
| 5 | pent- | pentane | pentene | pentanol | pentanoic acid |
| 6 | hex- | hexane | hexene | hexanol | hexanoic acid |

Pentane to hexanoic acid (five and six carbon atoms) are **ZIMSEC** names; Cambridge stops at four carbon atoms. There is no "methene": a double bond needs two carbon atoms.

## Naming step by step

1. Find the **longest carbon chain** that contains the functional group. Its length gives the **stem**.
2. Choose the **ending** for the homologous series.
3. If the functional group could be in more than one place, number the chain from the end **nearer the functional group** and put the number before the ending.

The carbon atom of a $\ce{-COOH}$ group is part of the chain and is always carbon 1, so carboxylic acids never need a position number: $\ce{CH3CH2COOH}$ has three carbon atoms and is **propanoic acid**.

### Where the position number matters (Supplement and ZIMSEC)

| Structural formula | Name | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\ce{CH2=CHCH2CH3}$ | but-1-ene | the double bond starts at carbon 1 |
| $\ce{CH3CH=CHCH3}$ | but-2-ene | the double bond starts at carbon 2 |
| $\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH}$ | propan-1-ol | –OH on an end carbon |
| $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3}$ | propan-2-ol | –OH on the middle carbon |
| $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2OH}$ | butan-1-ol | –OH on an end carbon |
| $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3}$ | butan-2-ol | –OH on the second carbon |

Here is the displayed formula of propan-2-ol, with the –OH group on the middle carbon atom:

```
    H   H   H
    |   |   |
H - C - C - C - H
    |   |   |
    H   O   H
        |
        H
```

Propene, $\ce{CH2=CHCH3}$, needs no number: wherever the double bond is in a three-carbon chain, numbering from the nearer end gives position 1.

### Methyl branches (ZIMSEC; Cambridge Supplement: methylpropane)

A $\ce{-CH3}$ group attached to the side of a chain is called a **methyl** group. Name the longest chain, then put the position number and "methyl" in front: $\ce{CH3CH(CH3)CH3}$ is **methylpropane** (in a three-carbon chain the branch can only be on carbon 2, so no number is needed), and $\ce{CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3}$ is **2-methylbutane**. Two methyl groups give "dimethyl", as in 2,2-dimethylpropane. You will use these names again for structural isomers.

## Esters (Supplement and ZIMSEC)

An ester forms when an alcohol reacts with a carboxylic acid. Its name has two words:

1. the **alcohol** part comes first and ends in **-yl** (methanol → methyl, ethanol → ethyl, propan-1-ol → propyl);
2. the **acid** part comes second and ends in **-oate** (ethanoic acid → ethanoate, propanoic acid → propanoate).

So ethanol and ethanoic acid make **ethyl ethanoate**, $\ce{CH3COOCH2CH3}$. In the structural formula the acid part is usually written first ($\ce{CH3COO\bond{-}}$) and the alcohol part last ($\ce{-CH2CH3}$), the opposite order to the name.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Example 1: from formula to name]
Name (a) $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2OH}$, (b) $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3}$ and (c) $\ce{CH3CH=CHCH3}$.

1. (a) Four carbon atoms (but-), an –OH group (-ol) on an end carbon (1): **butan-1-ol**.
2. (b) Three carbon atoms (prop-), –OH on the middle carbon (2): **propan-2-ol**.
3. (c) Four carbon atoms (but-), a double bond (-ene) between carbons 2 and 3. Number from the nearer end: it starts at carbon 2, so **but-2-ene**.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: from name to formula]
Write structural formulae for (a) propanoic acid and (b) pentan-1-ol (ZIMSEC).

1. (a) Prop- means three carbon atoms, and the –COOH carbon counts as one of them: $\ce{CH3CH2COOH}$.
2. Check with the general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}COOH}$: $n = 2$ gives $\ce{C2H5COOH}$, the same compound.
3. (b) Pent- means five carbon atoms, -1-ol puts the –OH on an end carbon: $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2CH2OH}$, molecular formula $\ce{C5H12O}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: naming an ester]
Name the ester made from methanol and butanoic acid, and write its structural formula.

1. Alcohol part first: methanol → **methyl**.
2. Acid part second: butanoic acid → **butanoate**.
3. Name: **methyl butanoate**.
4. Structural formula: the acid part (four carbons, ending in $\ce{-COO\bond{-}}$) then the methyl group: $\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOCH3}$.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Numbering from the wrong end"}
$\ce{CH2=CHCH2CH3}$ is but-1-ene, not but-3-ene. Always number from the end that gives the functional group the **lower** number.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that the –COOH carbon is in the chain"}
$\ce{CH3COOH}$ has two carbon atoms, so it is **ethanoic** acid, not methanoic acid. Count every carbon, including the one in $\ce{-COOH}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reversing the two parts of an ester name"}
The alcohol part is named first: the ester of ethanol and propanoic acid is ethyl propanoate, $\ce{CH3CH2COOCH2CH3}$, not "propyl ethanoate" (which is a different ester).
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaving out a needed position number"}
"Propanol" could mean propan-1-ol or propan-2-ol. When two positions are possible, the number is part of the name.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Name" means the full systematic name with numbers and hyphens: but-2-ene, propan-1-ol. Spelling counts (ethanoic, not ethonoic), and use the systematic names, such as propanoic acid, rather than older names such as propionic acid.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to "state the type of compound" from a name, use the ending: -ane alkane, -ene alkene, -ol alcohol, -oic acid carboxylic acid, -yl …-oate ester.
:::

:::exam-tip
After writing a formula from a name, count the carbon atoms again and check that every carbon has four bonds.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Stems: meth (1), eth (2), prop (3), but (4); ZIMSEC also pent (5) and hex (6).
- Endings: -ane, -ene, -ol, -oic acid; the –COOH carbon is carbon 1 of the chain.
- Number the chain from the end nearer the functional group: but-1-ene, but-2-ene, propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol.
- Methyl branches: methylpropane, 2-methylbutane, 2,2-dimethylpropane.
- Esters: alcohol part (-yl) first, acid part (-oate) second, e.g. ethyl ethanoate, $\ce{CH3COOCH2CH3}$.
:::
