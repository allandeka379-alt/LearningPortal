## Introduction

Methane is the main gas in natural gas and in biogas. Propane and butane fill LPG cylinders. Paraffin, petrol and diesel are mixtures of larger alkanes. Almost every alkane we use is burnt as a fuel, because alkanes release a lot of energy when they burn and do very little else.

Burning fuels safely matters. On cold winter nights people sometimes bring a charcoal brazier or a gas heater into a closed room to keep warm. This is dangerous: in too little oxygen, carbon-containing fuels make **carbon monoxide**, a poisonous gas that cannot be seen or smelt. In this lesson you will learn why alkanes are unreactive, how they burn completely and incompletely, how to balance their combustion equations and (Supplement; ZIMSEC) how they react with chlorine in ultraviolet light.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Alkane"}
A saturated hydrocarbon: a compound of carbon and hydrogen only, with single covalent bonds only, general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Complete combustion"}
Burning in a plentiful supply of oxygen. A hydrocarbon forms carbon dioxide and water.
:::

:::definition{term="Incomplete combustion"}
Burning in a limited supply of oxygen. Carbon monoxide and/or carbon (soot) form as well as water, and less energy is released.
:::

:::definition{term="Substitution reaction (Supplement)"}
A reaction in which one atom or group of atoms in a molecule is replaced by another atom or group of atoms.
:::

:::definition{term="Photochemical reaction (Supplement)"}
A reaction that needs light: here, ultraviolet light provides the activation energy.
:::

## Why alkanes are unreactive

All the bonds in an alkane are strong single covalent bonds, $\ce{C-C}$ and $\ce{C-H}$, and there is no functional group for other substances to attack. Alkanes do not react with acids, alkalis or most other reagents, and they are insoluble in water. They have two important reactions: **combustion** and, in ultraviolet light, **substitution** by chlorine.

## Combustion

### Complete combustion

With plenty of oxygen, the carbon atoms end up in carbon dioxide and the hydrogen atoms in water:

$$
\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}
$$

The flame is blue and clean, and the most energy is released.

### Incomplete combustion

In a limited supply of oxygen (a blocked burner, a lamp with its wick turned up too high, a fire in a closed room), some carbon atoms end up as **carbon monoxide** or as **carbon** (soot):

$$
\ce{2CH4 + 3O2 -> 2CO + 4H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{CH4 + O2 -> C + 2H2O}
$$

The flame is yellow and smoky, pots go black and less energy is released.

:::keyfact
Carbon monoxide is **colourless, odourless and toxic**. It combines with the haemoglobin in red blood cells and stops them carrying oxygen. Never use a charcoal brazier, paraffin heater or gas heater in a closed room, keep windows or vents open, and have gas appliances checked.
:::

### Balancing combustion equations

Balance carbon first, then hydrogen, then oxygen last (oxygen appears on its own, so it is easiest to adjust).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: complete combustion of butane, $\ce{C4H10}$]
1. Carbon: 4 C on the left, so $\ce{4CO2}$ on the right.
2. Hydrogen: 10 H on the left, so $\ce{5H2O}$ on the right.
3. Oxygen on the right: $4 \times 2 + 5 \times 1 = 13$ atoms, which needs $6\tfrac{1}{2}$ molecules of $\ce{O2}$.
4. Double everything to remove the half: $\ce{2C4H10 + 13O2 -> 8CO2 + 10H2O}$.
5. Check: C 8 = 8; H 20 = 20; O 26 = 16 + 10.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2 (Supplement): mass of carbon dioxide from burning propane]
Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide formed when 11 g of propane burns completely. ($A_r$: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16)

1. Equation: $\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}$.
2. $M_r(\ce{C3H8}) = 3 \times 12 + 8 = 44$, so $n = 11 \div 44 = 0.25$ mol.
3. 1 mol of propane gives 3 mol of $\ce{CO2}$, so $n(\ce{CO2}) = 0.75$ mol.
4. Mass $= 0.75 \times 44 = 33$ g.
:::

## Substitution with chlorine (Supplement; ZIMSEC)

A mixture of methane and chlorine does **not** react in the dark. In ultraviolet light (or bright sunlight) a hydrogen atom is replaced by a chlorine atom:

$$
\ce{CH4 + Cl2 ->[UV light] CH3Cl + HCl}
$$

The products are **chloromethane** and hydrogen chloride. This is a **substitution** reaction: a $\ce{-Cl}$ atom takes the place of an $\ce{-H}$ atom. It is **photochemical**: the ultraviolet light provides the energy needed to start the reaction (the activation energy). The pale yellow-green colour of chlorine fades, and the hydrogen chloride forms misty fumes that turn damp blue litmus paper red.

```
    H                         H
    |                         |
H - C - H  + Cl - Cl  ->  H - C - Cl  + H - Cl
    |                         |
    H                         H
```

:::example{title="Example 3: in the dark and in the light (ZIMSEC)"}
Two test tubes are filled with a mixture of methane and chlorine. One is wrapped in foil; the other is left in sunlight. After an hour the wrapped tube is still pale yellow-green: there is no reaction in the dark. The tube in sunlight has lost its colour and its contents turn damp blue litmus red, because chloromethane and hydrogen chloride have formed.
:::

With more chlorine, further hydrogen atoms can be replaced, giving $\ce{CH2Cl2}$, $\ce{CHCl3}$ and $\ce{CCl4}$. Cambridge only asks for **monosubstitution** (one chlorine atom), such as ethane forming chloroethane, $\ce{CH3CH2Cl}$.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing an addition reaction for an alkane"}
Alkanes are saturated: they cannot add chlorine. With chlorine an alkane **swaps** an H for a Cl, so there are always **two** products, the chloroalkane and $\ce{HCl}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the conditions"}
The substitution needs ultraviolet light. "Heat" or "a catalyst" is not the condition, and in the dark there is no reaction.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying carbon monoxide has a smell"}
Carbon monoxide is odourless and colourless, which is exactly why it is so dangerous. The smell of a smoky brazier comes from other substances.
:::

:::mistake{title="Unbalanced oxygen"}
Count oxygen atoms in **both** $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ on the right. A half in front of $\ce{O2}$ is fine in working, but double the equation for the final answer.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Describe the reactions of alkanes": say they are generally unreactive (strong single bonds, no functional group), then give combustion and substitution by chlorine in UV light.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "explain why incomplete combustion is dangerous", name carbon monoxide **and** say why it is harmful (it combines with haemoglobin and reduces oxygen transport in the blood).
:::

:::exam-tip
In substitution equations, name both products. The organic product is named chloro- + the alkane: chloromethane, chloroethane.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons, $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$, with only single bonds: generally unreactive.
- Complete combustion gives $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$; incomplete combustion also gives toxic, odourless CO and soot.
- Balance combustion equations C, then H, then O; double to remove halves.
- Supplement; ZIMSEC: in UV light, $\ce{CH4 + Cl2 -> CH3Cl + HCl}$ (substitution, photochemical); no reaction in the dark.
- Ventilate rooms where braziers, paraffin or gas heaters burn.
:::
