## Introduction

Ethanol is one of the most useful organic compounds. It is the alcohol in beer and wine, the main ingredient of many hand sanitisers and a solvent for perfumes and medicines. It is also a fuel: in Zimbabwe, ethanol made from sugar cane grown in the Lowveld is blended with petrol.

There are two ways to make ethanol. **Fermentation** uses yeast to turn sugar into ethanol; it is as old as brewing itself, and Zimbabwean families have long brewed traditional beer from sorghum, rapoko (finger millet) and maize. **Hydration of ethene** adds steam to ethene from crude oil in a chemical plant. In this lesson you will learn the conditions for each, compare them and (ZIMSEC) see how fermentation is done industrially and traditionally. You should already know that ethene is made by cracking and that enzymes are biological catalysts.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fermentation"}
The breakdown of sugars such as glucose by the enzymes in yeast, in the absence of oxygen, to form ethanol and carbon dioxide.
:::

:::definition{term="Yeast"}
A single-celled fungus whose enzymes catalyse fermentation.
:::

:::definition{term="Hydration"}
The addition of water (as steam) to a molecule; here, the catalytic addition of steam to ethene.
:::

:::definition{term="Batch and continuous processes"}
In a batch process a fixed amount of reactants is processed, then the vessel is emptied and refilled. In a continuous process reactants are fed in and products taken out all the time.
:::

## Method 1: fermentation

$$
\ce{C6H12O6(aq) -> 2C2H5OH(aq) + 2CO2(g)}
$$

![Fermentation apparatus: yeast in glucose solution at 25 to 35 degrees Celsius in a flask closed to the air (anaerobic); the carbon dioxide made, 2 mol for each mole of glucose, bubbles through limewater and turns it milky while ethanol stays in the flask.](media:diagrams/fermentation-glucose-apparatus.png "Figure: fermenting glucose with yeast, with the gas tested using limewater")

Conditions:

- **yeast** (its enzymes are the catalyst) added to an aqueous solution of glucose or another sugar;
- a temperature of **25–35 °C**: below this the reaction is slow; much above about 40 °C the yeast's enzymes are denatured and fermentation stops;
- **no oxygen** (anaerobic): with air, the yeast respires aerobically, making carbon dioxide and water instead of ethanol, and bacteria can oxidise the ethanol to ethanoic acid (vinegar).

Fermentation slows and stops when the sugar is used up or when the ethanol reaches about 15%, because the ethanol kills the yeast. The yeast is then allowed to settle or is filtered off, and the ethanol is separated from the water by **fractional distillation**.

:::worked-solution[Example 1 (Supplement): masses and volumes in fermentation]
Calculate the mass of ethanol and the volume of carbon dioxide (at r.t.p.) made when 45 g of glucose ferments completely. ($A_r$: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16; 1 mol of gas = 24 dm³ at r.t.p.)

1. $M_r(\ce{C6H12O6}) = 72 + 12 + 96 = 180$, so $n = 45 \div 180 = 0.25$ mol.
2. 1 mol of glucose gives 2 mol of ethanol and 2 mol of $\ce{CO2}$: 0.50 mol of each.
3. $M_r(\ce{C2H5OH}) = 46$: mass of ethanol $= 0.50 \times 46 = 23$ g.
4. Volume of $\ce{CO2} = 0.50 \times 24 = 12\ \text{dm}^3$.
:::

## Method 2: hydration of ethene

$$
\ce{C2H4(g) + H2O(g) -> C2H5OH(g)}
$$

Conditions: **steam**, a temperature of about **300 °C**, a pressure of **60 atm** (6000 kPa) and an **acid catalyst** (phosphoric acid). The ethene comes from cracking crude-oil fractions. Unreacted ethene and steam are recycled, so the process runs continuously.

## Comparing the two methods (Supplement)

| | Fermentation | Hydration of ethene |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raw material | sugar from crops (renewable) | ethene from crude oil (non-renewable) |
| Conditions | 25–35 °C, normal pressure | 300 °C, 60 atm: more energy for heating and compressing |
| Rate and type of process | slow; batch | fast; continuous |
| Purity of product | dilute (up to about 15%); must be distilled | almost pure ethanol |
| By-products | carbon dioxide | none (only one product) |
| Other points | uses land that could grow food | depends on oil prices and supply |

:::example{title="Example 2: which method suits a country?"}
A country grows plenty of sugar cane but has no crude oil and no refinery. Fermentation suits it: the raw material is grown locally and is renewable, the low temperatures need little energy, and making fuel ethanol reduces the petrol that must be imported. A country with a large oil refinery and cheap ethene might choose hydration, because it is fast, continuous and gives pure ethanol.
:::

## Fermentation in Zimbabwe (ZIMSEC)

### Industrial ethanol from sugar cane

Sugar cane is crushed to extract its juice. After sugar has been crystallised from the juice, a thick syrup called **molasses** is left; molasses (or cane juice) still contains plenty of sugar. It is diluted with water, yeast is added, and fermentation takes place in large tanks. The liquid is then distilled to give concentrated ethanol, which is dried further if it is to be blended with petrol as fuel.

### Traditional beer

Traditional opaque beer is brewed from **sorghum**, **rapoko** or **maize**.

1. **Malting:** grain is soaked and allowed to sprout. Sprouting produces enzymes that turn the grain's **starch** into **sugars**. The malt is dried and ground.
2. **Cooking:** a thin porridge of maize meal or grain is cooked and allowed to cool.
3. **Fermenting:** malt is mixed in; its enzymes make sugars, and yeasts (from the malt or added) ferment the sugars to ethanol and carbon dioxide over a few days.

The beer is low in alcohol and is drunk within a few days: fermentation and souring continue, so it does not keep.

:::note{title="Mahewu is not beer"}
Mahewu, the fermented maize drink, is soured mainly by **lactic acid bacteria**, which turn sugars into lactic acid rather than ethanol. It is a non-alcoholic drink, not an alcoholic brew.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying yeast is the catalyst \"because it is alive\""}
The catalysts are the **enzymes** inside the yeast. That is why temperatures above about 40 °C stop fermentation: the enzymes are denatured, not "killed by heat" in a vague sense.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that fermentation must be anaerobic"}
With oxygen, yeast respires aerobically and bacteria can turn the ethanol into vinegar. Fermentation vessels are sealed or fitted with an air lock that lets carbon dioxide out but no air in.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the catalysts"}
Fermentation: enzymes in yeast. Hydration: phosphoric acid. Hydrogenation of alkenes: nickel.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Fermentation: $\ce{C6H12O6 -> 2C2H5OH + 2CO2}$, yeast, 25–35 °C, no oxygen; separate by fractional distillation.
- Hydration: $\ce{C2H4 + H2O -> C2H5OH}$, steam, 300 °C, 60 atm, acid catalyst; ethene from crude oil.
- Supplement: fermentation is renewable but slow, batch and impure; hydration is fast, continuous and pure but uses crude oil and more energy.
- ZIMSEC: molasses from sugar cane is fermented and distilled for fuel ethanol; traditional beer is brewed from malted sorghum, rapoko or maize.
- Mahewu is soured by lactic acid bacteria: it is not an alcoholic drink.
:::
