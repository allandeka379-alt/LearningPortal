## Introduction

Look around a Zimbabwean home or market: a black water tank on a stand, a stack of plastic chairs, woven sacks of maize meal and fertiliser, PVC water pipes, carrier bags. All of these are **plastics**, and all are made from **polymers**: enormous molecules built by joining thousands of small molecules together.

Plastics are cheap, light, strong and waterproof, but the same properties make them hard to get rid of. Plastic bags block drains in towns, litter the countryside and are eaten by cattle and goats. In this lesson you will learn how alkenes form addition polymers, what the polymers are used for and why plastics cause environmental problems. Cambridge Supplement students also learn to write repeat units and to work from a polymer back to its monomer. You should already know that alkenes contain a $\ce{C=C}$ double bond.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Polymer"}
A large molecule built up from many smaller molecules called monomers.
:::

:::definition{term="Monomer"}
A small molecule that can join with many others to form a polymer.
:::

:::definition{term="Addition polymerisation"}
The joining of many monomers that contain a $\ce{C=C}$ double bond to form a polymer as the **only** product.
:::

:::definition{term="Repeat unit (Supplement)"}
The section of a polymer chain that repeats over and over to make the whole chain. In an addition polymer it is the part made from one monomer molecule: the two carbon atoms of the $\ce{C=C}$ bond and the atoms attached to them.
:::

:::definition{term="Plastic"}
A material made from synthetic polymers that can be moulded into shape.
:::

## Making poly(ethene)

When ethene is heated under pressure with a catalyst, the double bond of each molecule opens and the molecules link into long chains:

![Addition polymerisation: n ethene molecules, each with a C=C double bond, join under heat, pressure and a catalyst to form poly(ethene); in the repeat unit the double bond has become a single bond, and the polymer is the only product.](media:diagrams/addition-polymer-ethene-poly-ethene.png "Figure: n ethene molecules form poly(ethene); the repeat unit is in brackets")

```
      H   H                     H   H
      |   |                     |   |
  n   C = C      ------>    -(- C - C -)-
      |   |                     |   |     n
      H   H                     H   H

  n ethene molecules        poly(ethene)
```

$$
\ce{n CH2=CH2 -> -[CH2-CH2]_{n}\bond{-}}
$$

Here $n$ is a very large number (thousands). One double bond in each monomer becomes a single bond, and the electrons freed form the new bonds to the neighbouring monomers. Nothing else is made: the polymer is the only product. Poly(ethene) is also called polythene.

## Repeat units and monomers (Supplement)

To draw the repeat unit of the polymer from any alkene monomer:

1. Draw the two carbon atoms of the double bond with all their other atoms or groups.
2. Change $\ce{C=C}$ to $\ce{C-C}$.
3. Put a bond sticking out of each carbon, brackets round the unit and $n$ outside.

| Monomer | Polymer | Repeat unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ethene, $\ce{CH2=CH2}$ | poly(ethene) | $\ce{-[CH2-CH2]\bond{-}}$ |
| propene, $\ce{CH2=CHCH3}$ | poly(propene) | $\ce{-[CH2-CH(CH3)]\bond{-}}$ |
| chloroethene, $\ce{CH2=CHCl}$ | poly(chloroethene), PVC | $\ce{-[CH2-CHCl]\bond{-}}$ |
| tetrafluoroethene, $\ce{CF2=CF2}$ | poly(tetrafluoroethene), PTFE | $\ce{-[CF2-CF2]\bond{-}}$ |

Notice that the $\ce{-CH3}$ group of propene becomes a **branch** on the chain: only the two carbon atoms of the double bond are in the main chain.

![Monomers and repeat units: propene gives poly(propene) with a CH3 branch, chloroethene gives PVC with a Cl on every second carbon, and tetrafluoroethene gives PTFE with four F atoms on each repeat unit; in each the C=C becomes C-C.](media:diagrams/addition-polymers-repeat-units.png "Figure: each C=C monomer opens to give a two-carbon repeat unit")

:::worked-solution[Example 1 (Supplement): from polymer to monomer]
Part of a polymer chain is $\ce{-CF2-CF2-CF2-CF2-CF2-CF2\bond{-}}$. Deduce the repeat unit and the monomer.

1. Each monomer adds two carbon atoms to the chain, so mark off the chain in two-carbon sections: $\ce{-CF2-CF2\bond{-}}$. This is the repeat unit.
2. Remove the bonds sticking out of the unit and put back the double bond between the two carbons.
3. Monomer: $\ce{CF2=CF2}$, tetrafluoroethene. The polymer is PTFE.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: how many monomers in a chain?]
A poly(ethene) molecule has a relative molecular mass of 280 000. How many ethene molecules joined to make it? ($A_r$: H = 1, C = 12)

1. $M_r(\ce{C2H4}) = 24 + 4 = 28$.
2. No atoms are lost in addition polymerisation, so $M_r(\text{polymer}) = n \times 28$.
3. $n = 280\,000 \div 28 = 10\,000$ monomer molecules.
:::

## Uses of synthetic polymers

| Polymer | Properties | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| poly(ethene) | light, flexible, waterproof, cheap, unreactive | carrier bags, cling film, bottles, buckets, water tanks |
| poly(propene) | tough, strong fibres, does not rot | ropes, woven sacks, crates, plastic chairs |
| PVC, poly(chloroethene) | rigid or flexible, an electrical insulator, weatherproof | water and drainage pipes, insulation on electrical cables, raincoats |
| PTFE | very slippery, unreactive, withstands heat | non-stick coating on frying pans |

## Why plastics are hard to dispose of

The properties that make plastics useful cause problems when they are thrown away:

- **Non-biodegradable:** microorganisms cannot break the strong carbon–carbon chains down, so plastics last for hundreds of years in **landfill sites**, which fill up.
- **Light and long-lasting:** discarded bags and bottles are blown and washed into drains, rivers and lakes and eventually the **oceans**, where they build up. They block drains and cause flooding, and animals eat them or get tangled in them.
- **Burning:** plastics burn, but burning them on rubbish heaps produces **toxic gases**: carbon monoxide and soot, and hydrogen chloride from PVC. It also releases carbon dioxide.

Reducing plastic use, reusing bags and containers, collecting and recycling plastics, and using biodegradable materials all help.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Keeping the double bond in the polymer"}
In the polymer the $\ce{C=C}$ has become $\ce{C-C}$. A repeat unit drawn with a double bond is wrong.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the CH₃ group into the main chain"}
Poly(propene) is not $\ce{-[CH2-CH2-CH2]\bond{-}}$. Only the two carbons of the double bond form the chain; the $\ce{-CH3}$ hangs off it as a branch.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying addition polymerisation gives water"}
Addition polymerisation has **one** product, the polymer. Losing a small molecule such as water happens in **condensation** polymerisation.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Describe the formation of poly(ethene)": many ethene molecules (monomers) join; the C=C double bond breaks (opens); a long chain forms; addition polymerisation; the polymer is the only product.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Describe the environmental problems of plastics" (Core): non-biodegradable so landfill fills up; build-up in rivers and oceans harms wildlife; burning gives toxic gases. Give a reason with each problem.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked for a repeat unit (Supplement), always include the two bonds sticking out of the brackets and check that the unit has the same atoms as the monomer.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Polymers are large molecules built from many monomers; plastics are made from polymers.
- Addition polymerisation: alkene monomers join as the C=C bonds open; the polymer is the only product, e.g. $\ce{n CH2=CH2 -> -[CH2-CH2]_{n}\bond{-}}$.
- Supplement: a repeat unit has the monomer's atoms with C=C changed to C–C; reverse the steps to find the monomer.
- Uses: poly(ethene) bags and tanks, poly(propene) ropes and sacks, PVC pipes and cable insulation, PTFE non-stick pans.
- Plastics are non-biodegradable: they fill landfill, build up in rivers and oceans and give toxic gases when burnt.
:::
