## Introduction

Sodium is a soft, dangerously reactive metal and chlorine is a poisonous gas, yet together they make the salt you add to your food. Magnesium oxide, another compound of a metal and a non-metal, has such a high melting point (about $2800\ ^\circ\text{C}$) that it is used to line furnaces at metal smelters. Both are held together by **ionic bonding**.

In this lesson you will see how atoms of metals and non-metals form ions, how to describe dot-and-cross diagrams, how to work out the formula of an ionic compound and how the structure explains the properties.

You should already be able to write electronic configurations for elements 1 to 20 and know that noble gases have full outer shells.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ion"}
A charged particle formed when an atom or group of atoms loses or gains electrons.
:::

:::definition{term="Cation and anion"}
A **cation** is a positive ion (electrons lost; formed by metals). An **anion** is a negative ion (electrons gained; formed by non-metals).
:::

:::definition{term="Ionic bond"}
The strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
:::

## How ions form

Atoms are most stable with a full outer shell, like a noble gas. Metal atoms have few outer electrons, so they **lose** them; non-metal atoms have nearly full outer shells, so they **gain** electrons. In an ionic compound, electrons are **transferred** from the metal atoms to the non-metal atoms.

| Group | I | II | III | V | VI | VII |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Outer electrons | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| Ion charge | $1+$ | $2+$ | $3+$ | $3-$ | $2-$ | $1-$ |

## Dot-and-cross diagrams

In a dot-and-cross diagram, the electrons of one atom are drawn as dots and those of the other as crosses, so you can see where each electron came from. Each ion is drawn in square brackets with its charge at the top right. Often only the outer shells are drawn.

![Dot-and-cross diagrams: sodium transfers its one outer electron to chlorine, giving Na+ (outer shell of 8 crosses) and Cl- (8 outer electrons, 7 dots and 1 cross), formula NaCl; magnesium transfers two electrons to oxygen, giving Mg2+ and O2- each with a full outer shell, formula MgO.](media:diagrams/ionic-bonding-dot-cross-nacl-mgo.png "Figure: electron transfer in sodium chloride and magnesium oxide")

:::worked-solution[Example 1: sodium chloride, $\ce{NaCl}$ (Group I with Group VII)]
1. Sodium is 2,8,1 and chlorine is 2,8,7.
2. The sodium atom transfers its one outer electron (a cross) to the chlorine atom.
3. Sodium becomes $\ce{Na+}$, 2,8: drawn in brackets with no electrons in its old outer shell (or with its full second shell of 8 crosses), charge $+$.
4. Chlorine becomes $\ce{Cl-}$, 2,8,8: drawn in brackets with 8 outer electrons, 7 dots and 1 cross, charge $-$.
5. One $\ce{Na+}$ balances one $\ce{Cl-}$, so the formula is $\ce{NaCl}$.
:::

In words: $\ce{Na -> Na+ + e-}$ and $\ce{Cl + e- -> Cl-}$.

:::worked-solution[Example 2 (Supplement and ZIMSEC): magnesium oxide, $\ce{MgO}$]
1. Magnesium is 2,8,2 and oxygen is 2,6.
2. The magnesium atom transfers **two** electrons to the oxygen atom.
3. $\ce{Mg^2+}$ (2,8) and $\ce{O^2-}$ (2,8): the oxide ion has 6 dots and 2 crosses in its outer shell.
4. Charges $+2$ and $-2$ balance, so the formula is $\ce{MgO}$.
:::

## Working out formulae

The total positive charge must equal the total negative charge.

:::worked-solution[Example 3 (Supplement and ZIMSEC): calcium chloride and aluminium oxide]
1. Calcium chloride: $\ce{Ca^2+}$ needs two $\ce{Cl-}$ ions. Each calcium atom (2,8,8,2) gives one electron to each of two chlorine atoms. Formula $\ce{CaCl2}$.
2. Aluminium oxide: $\ce{Al^3+}$ and $\ce{O^2-}$. The smallest equal total charge is 6: two $\ce{Al^3+}$ give $+6$ and three $\ce{O^2-}$ give $-6$.
3. So two aluminium atoms (2,8,3) transfer a total of six electrons to three oxygen atoms (2,6). Formula $\ce{Al2O3}$.
:::

## The giant ionic lattice (Supplement)

An ionic compound is not made of separate molecules. The ions form a **giant ionic lattice**: a regular, three-dimensional arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions. In sodium chloride each $\ce{Na+}$ ion is surrounded by six $\ce{Cl-}$ ions, and each $\ce{Cl-}$ ion by six $\ce{Na+}$ ions. The formula $\ce{NaCl}$ only gives the **ratio** of ions, 1 : 1.

![A slice through a sodium chloride lattice: smaller positive sodium ions and larger negative chloride ions alternate in rows and columns, each ion surrounded by ions of opposite charge, in the ratio 1 to 1.](media:diagrams/ionic-lattice-sodium-chloride.png "Figure: a layer of the giant ionic lattice of sodium chloride")

## Properties of ionic compounds

| Property | Explanation (Supplement) |
| --- | --- |
| High melting and boiling points | Strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions act in all directions throughout the giant lattice; a lot of energy is needed to overcome them |
| Do not conduct electricity when solid | The ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice and cannot move |
| Conduct when molten or dissolved in water | The ions are free to move and carry charge |
| Often soluble in water | Water molecules can separate the ions (insoluble in solvents such as petrol) |

The larger the charges on the ions, the stronger the attractions: magnesium oxide ($\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{O^2-}$) melts at about $2800\ ^\circ\text{C}$, far higher than sodium chloride ($\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$) at $801\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

:::example{title="Telling ionic and covalent compounds apart"}
Two white solids are tested. Solid X melts only when strongly heated, dissolves in water, and the solution conducts electricity: X is ionic. Solid Y melts in a warm water bath, does not dissolve in water, and its melt does not conduct: Y is a simple covalent (molecular) substance. Conduction when molten or dissolved is the clearest sign of an ionic compound.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying sodium and chlorine share electrons"}
In ionic bonding electrons are **transferred** from the metal to the non-metal. Sharing is covalent bonding.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing 'molecules' of sodium chloride"}
Ionic compounds form giant lattices, not molecules. The ionic bond is the attraction between oppositely charged ions throughout the lattice.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying solid ionic compounds conduct because they contain ions"}
The ions must be **free to move**. In the solid they are fixed, so the solid does not conduct.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying electrons carry the current in molten salt"}
In a molten or dissolved ionic compound, the **ions** move and carry the charge, not electrons.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
A full dot-and-cross diagram shows: the correct number of electrons in each shell drawn, dots and crosses to show where they came from, square brackets around each ion and the charge on each ion. Missing charges is the commonest lost mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
When **explaining** a high melting point, say "strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions" and "a lot of energy is needed to overcome it". Never say "strong intermolecular forces" for an ionic compound.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Metal atoms lose electrons to form positive ions; non-metal atoms gain electrons to form negative ions, both reaching noble-gas configurations.
- An ionic bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
- In a formula the total positive charge equals the total negative charge: $\ce{NaCl}$, $\ce{MgO}$, $\ce{CaCl2}$, $\ce{Al2O3}$.
- Supplement: ionic compounds form giant lattices of alternating positive and negative ions.
- Ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points and conduct electricity when molten or in solution (ions free to move), but not when solid.
:::
