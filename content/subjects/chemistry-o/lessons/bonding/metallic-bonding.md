## Introduction

Copper wires carry electricity into homes in Harare and Bulawayo. Gold from Zimbabwe's mines can be hammered into gold leaf thinner than a sheet of paper. An aluminium pot is pressed into shape from a flat sheet without cracking. All of these depend on the way the atoms in a metal are held together: **metallic bonding**.

:::note{title="Who needs this lesson?"}
Cambridge IGCSE: Supplement (Extended papers 2 and 4) only. ZIMSEC O-Level: Form 3, for all candidates.
:::

You should already know that metal atoms have few outer-shell electrons and lose them to form positive ions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Metallic bonding"}
The electrostatic attraction between the positive ions in a giant metallic lattice and a "sea" of delocalised electrons.
:::

:::definition{term="Delocalised electrons"}
Electrons that do not belong to any one atom and are free to move throughout the structure.
:::

:::definition{term="Malleable and ductile"}
**Malleable**: can be hammered or pressed into shape without breaking. **Ductile**: can be drawn out into wires.
:::

## The structure of a metal

In a solid metal, the atoms are packed closely together in a regular arrangement, in **layers**. Each atom releases its outer-shell electrons, becoming a **positive ion**. The released electrons are **delocalised**: they move freely through the whole lattice, forming a "sea" of electrons.

![Metallic bonding: a regular lattice of positive metal ions surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons that are free to move; when a force is applied, layers of ions slide over each other while the electrons still hold the structure together, so the metal bends instead of shattering.](media:diagrams/metallic-bonding-sea-of-electrons.png "Figure: positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons, and layers sliding")

The negative sea of electrons attracts all the positive ions around it. This strong **electrostatic attraction** holds the metal together. It acts in all directions throughout the giant lattice, so most metals have high melting points.

## Explaining the properties of metals

| Property | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Good conductor of electricity (solid or molten) | The delocalised electrons move through the lattice and carry charge when a voltage is applied |
| Good conductor of heat (thermal energy) | The delocalised electrons move quickly and carry energy through the metal |
| Malleable and ductile | When a force is applied, layers of positive ions slide over each other; the sea of electrons moves with them and keeps attracting the ions in their new positions, so the metal changes shape without breaking |
| Usually high melting and boiling points | Strong attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons needs a lot of energy to overcome |

:::example{title="Why copper is used for electrical wiring"}
Copper is an excellent electrical conductor because its delocalised electrons flow easily through the lattice. It is also very ductile: its layers of ions slide past each other as it is pulled through a die, so it can be drawn into long, thin wires without snapping. Both properties come from metallic bonding.
:::

:::example{title="Why gold can be beaten into gold leaf"}
When a goldsmith hammers gold, layers of gold ions slide over one another. Because the delocalised electrons are shared by the whole lattice, the attraction is not lost when the layers move; the ions simply settle into new positions. Gold is so malleable that it can be beaten until it is thin enough to let light through.
:::

## Metals compared with ionic compounds

Both metals and ionic compounds are giant lattices containing positive ions, but:

- In a **metal**, the mobile particles are **electrons**, so the solid conducts. In an **ionic compound**, the charge carriers are **ions**, which can only move when the compound is molten or dissolved.
- If layers of an ionic crystal are pushed out of place, ions of the same charge come next to each other, repel, and the crystal shatters. In a metal, the sea of electrons prevents this, so the metal bends instead.

## The three types of strong bonding compared

| | Ionic | Covalent | Metallic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Elements involved | Metal and non-metal | Non-metals | Metal atoms only |
| What happens to electrons | Transferred from metal to non-metal | Shared in pairs between atoms | Released into a sea of delocalised electrons |
| Particles in the structure | Positive and negative ions | Molecules (or atoms in a giant network) | Positive ions and delocalised electrons |
| Conducts when solid? | No | No (except graphite) | Yes |
| Conducts when molten? | Yes | No | Yes |

Use this table to identify the bonding in an unknown substance from its properties: a solid that conducts is almost certainly a metal (or graphite); a substance that conducts only when molten or dissolved is ionic.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying metals contain negative ions"}
A metal contains **positive ions** and **delocalised electrons**. There are no negative ions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying ions carry the current in a metal wire"}
The ions stay in the lattice (they only vibrate). The **delocalised electrons** move and carry the current.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the bonds break when a metal is bent"}
The layers slide, but the attraction between the ions and the sea of electrons continues in the new positions. That is why the metal bends rather than breaks.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
A complete description of metallic bonding names three things: **positive ions**, a **sea of delocalised electrons** and the **electrostatic attraction** between them.
:::

:::exam-tip
For malleability, say that the **layers** of ions **slide** over each other, and that the metallic bonding is not broken because the delocalised electrons keep holding the ions together.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A metal is a giant lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.
- Metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons.
- Metals conduct electricity and heat because the delocalised electrons can move through the structure.
- Metals are malleable and ductile because layers of ions can slide over each other without the bonding breaking.
- Most metals have high melting points because the attraction between ions and electrons is strong.
:::
