## Introduction

Diamond, mined at Marange in Manicaland and at Murowa near Zvishavane, is the hardest natural substance known. The "lead" in your pencil is graphite, so soft that it rubs off onto paper. Yet diamond and graphite are both pure carbon. The difference is entirely in how the carbon atoms are joined.

Diamond and graphite are **giant covalent structures**: instead of small molecules, they contain enormous numbers of atoms joined by covalent bonds in a continuous network. In this lesson you will learn their structures, how the structures explain their properties and uses, and (Supplement) why sand, silicon(IV) oxide, behaves like diamond.

You should already know what a covalent bond is and why simple molecular substances have low melting points.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Giant covalent structure"}
A structure in which a very large number of atoms are joined by covalent bonds in a continuous network (lattice) that extends throughout the solid. It is also called a macromolecular structure.
:::

:::definition{term="Delocalised electrons"}
Electrons that are not fixed between two particular atoms and are free to move through a structure.
:::

## Diamond

- Each carbon atom forms **four** strong covalent bonds to four other carbon atoms, arranged tetrahedrally.
- This builds a rigid **three-dimensional** network throughout the crystal.
- All four outer electrons of each carbon atom are used in bonds, so there are **no free electrons**.

| Property | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Extremely hard | Every atom is held in place by four strong covalent bonds in three dimensions |
| Very high melting point | Many strong covalent bonds must be broken to melt it |
| Does not conduct electricity | No delocalised electrons and no ions |
| Insoluble in water | The strong covalent network is not broken up by water |

**Use:** the tips of drills and the edges of saws that cut rock, glass and metal (cutting tools), because it is so hard.

## Graphite

- Each carbon atom forms **three** strong covalent bonds to three other carbon atoms, making flat **layers** of hexagons.
- The layers are held together only by **weak forces**, so they can slide over each other.
- Each carbon atom's fourth outer electron is **delocalised**: free to move along the layers.

| Property | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Soft and slippery | The layers slide over each other because the forces between layers are weak |
| Conducts electricity | Delocalised electrons move along the layers |
| Very high melting point | The strong covalent bonds within the layers must be broken |

**Uses:** as a **lubricant** (the layers slide) and as **electrodes** in electrolysis and in dry cells (it conducts, has a very high melting point and is fairly unreactive). Pencil "lead" is graphite mixed with clay.

:::example{title="Why graphite conducts and diamond does not"}
In diamond, each carbon atom uses all four outer electrons to bond to four neighbours, so every electron is fixed between two atoms. In graphite, each atom uses only three electrons in bonds; the fourth becomes delocalised and moves along the layer when a voltage is applied. The same element, arranged differently, gives a conductor and an insulator.
:::

## Supplement: silicon(IV) oxide

Silicon(IV) oxide, $\ce{SiO2}$, is the main compound in sand and quartz. It has a giant covalent structure very like diamond:

- each silicon atom is covalently bonded to **four** oxygen atoms, arranged tetrahedrally;
- each oxygen atom is bonded to **two** silicon atoms;
- so there is one silicon atom for every two oxygen atoms, which is why the formula is $\ce{SiO2}$.

Like diamond it is **hard**, has a **high melting point** (about $1700\ ^\circ\text{C}$), does **not conduct** electricity and is **insoluble** in water: all because it is a three-dimensional network of strong covalent bonds with no free electrons or ions.

:::example{title="Supplement: carbon dioxide and silicon(IV) oxide"}
Carbon and silicon are in the same group, yet carbon dioxide is a gas and silicon(IV) oxide is a hard solid. Carbon dioxide is made of small $\ce{O=C=O}$ molecules with weak forces between them, so little energy separates them. In silicon(IV) oxide there are no separate molecules: melting it means breaking many strong Si–O covalent bonds, which needs a great deal of energy.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying graphite's layers are joined by covalent bonds"}
The bonds **within** each layer are strong covalent bonds; the forces **between** layers are weak. That is why the layers slide.
:::

:::mistake{title="Explaining a giant structure's melting point with intermolecular forces"}
Diamond, graphite and silicon(IV) oxide have no separate molecules. To melt them, strong **covalent bonds** must be broken.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying graphite conducts because it contains ions"}
Graphite conducts because of **delocalised electrons**, not ions.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Explain" questions about these substances usually need three links: the **structure** (number of bonds per atom, layers or 3D network), the **bonding** (strong covalent bonds, weak forces between layers, delocalised electrons) and the **property** or **use**.
:::

:::exam-tip
To tell a giant covalent substance from data: a very high melting point and **no** electrical conductivity, either as a solid or when molten (graphite is the exception that conducts). An ionic compound also has a high melting point, but it conducts when molten.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Giant covalent structures are continuous networks of atoms joined by strong covalent bonds.
- Diamond: each C bonded to four others in 3D; very hard, very high melting point, no conduction; used in cutting tools.
- Graphite: each C bonded to three others in layers with weak forces between layers and delocalised electrons; soft and slippery, conducts; used as a lubricant and for electrodes.
- Supplement: silicon(IV) oxide has each Si bonded to four O and each O to two Si, giving diamond-like properties: hard, high melting point, non-conducting.
:::
