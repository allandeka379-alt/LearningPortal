## Introduction

Methane from a biogas digester on a farm, the water you drink and the ammonia used to make fertiliser at Sable Chemicals in Kwekwe have something in common: they are all made of small **molecules**, held together by **covalent bonds**. Covalent bonds form between atoms of **non-metals**, which all need to gain electrons. Neither atom can give electrons away to the other, so they **share** them instead.

In this lesson you will learn what a covalent bond is, how to describe the dot-and-cross diagrams of common molecules, and why substances made of small molecules melt and boil at low temperatures.

You should already know the electronic configurations of the first 20 elements.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Covalent bond"}
A pair of electrons shared between two atoms. Sharing lets each atom reach a noble-gas electronic configuration.
:::

:::definition{term="Molecule"}
A group of atoms held together by covalent bonds.
:::

:::definition{term="Single, double and triple bonds"}
One shared pair of electrons is a single bond ($\ce{H-H}$); two shared pairs a double bond ($\ce{O=O}$); three shared pairs a triple bond ($\ce{N#N}$).
:::

## Dot-and-cross diagrams

In a dot-and-cross diagram of a molecule, the outer shells of neighbouring atoms **overlap**, and each shared pair (one dot and one cross) sits in the overlap. Electrons that are not shared are called **non-bonding pairs** (or lone pairs). Count the electrons around each atom, including the shared ones: hydrogen should have 2 and every other atom 8.

![Dot-and-cross diagrams: ammonia NH3 has nitrogen sharing one pair with each of three hydrogen atoms, leaving one non-bonding pair; carbon dioxide has carbon sharing two pairs with each oxygen atom (two double bonds); every atom ends with a full outer shell.](media:diagrams/covalent-dot-cross-nh3-co2.png "Figure: shared pairs in ammonia and carbon dioxide (outer shells only)")

| Molecule | Shared pairs | Non-bonding pairs | What the diagram shows |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrogen, $\ce{H2}$ | 1 | 0 | One pair in the overlap; each H has 2 electrons |
| Chlorine, $\ce{Cl2}$ | 1 | 3 on each Cl | Each Cl has 8 outer electrons |
| Hydrogen chloride, $\ce{HCl}$ | 1 | 3 on Cl | H has 2; Cl has 8 |
| Water, $\ce{H2O}$ | 2 | 2 on O | O shares one pair with each H |
| Methane, $\ce{CH4}$ | 4 | 0 | C shares one pair with each of four H |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$]
1. Nitrogen is 2,5: it needs 3 more electrons. Each hydrogen atom has 1 electron and needs 1 more.
2. Nitrogen shares one pair with each of three hydrogen atoms: **3 shared pairs** (3 single bonds).
3. Nitrogen's remaining two outer electrons form **1 non-bonding pair**.
4. Check: around N there are $3 \times 2 + 2 = 8$ electrons; each H has 2. Total electrons drawn $= 5 + 3 = 8$.
:::

### Supplement: molecules with multiple bonds

| Molecule | Bonds | Non-bonding pairs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oxygen, $\ce{O2}$ | One double bond, $\ce{O=O}$ (2 shared pairs) | 2 on each O |
| Nitrogen, $\ce{N2}$ | One triple bond, $\ce{N#N}$ (3 shared pairs) | 1 on each N |
| Ethene, $\ce{C2H4}$ | One $\ce{C=C}$ double bond and four $\ce{C-H}$ single bonds (6 shared pairs) | None |
| Methanol, $\ce{CH3OH}$ | Three $\ce{C-H}$, one $\ce{C-O}$ and one $\ce{O-H}$ single bonds (5 shared pairs) | 2 on O |

:::worked-solution[Example 2 (Supplement): carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$]
1. Carbon is 2,4 (needs 4 more); oxygen is 2,6 (needs 2 more).
2. Carbon shares **two** pairs with each oxygen atom: two double bonds, $\ce{O=C=O}$.
3. Each oxygen keeps two non-bonding pairs; carbon has none.
4. Check: C has $4 \times 2 = 8$ electrons around it; each O has $2 \times 2 + 2 \times 2 = 8$. Total drawn $= 4 + 6 + 6 = 16$.
:::

## Properties of simple molecular substances

Inside each molecule the covalent bonds are **strong**. Between neighbouring molecules there are only **weak intermolecular forces**.

- **Low melting and boiling points.** Melting or boiling only separates the molecules from each other, which means overcoming the weak forces **between** molecules. Little energy is needed, so many simple molecular substances are gases or liquids at room temperature. The covalent bonds inside the molecules are **not** broken.
- **Poor electrical conductivity.** The molecules are uncharged, and there are no ions or free electrons that can move to carry a current.

:::example{title="Boiling water does not split water molecules"}
When water boils at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$, the steam is still made of $\ce{H2O}$ molecules. Only the weak attractions between molecules were overcome. Splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen needs a chemical reaction, such as electrolysis, because it means breaking the strong $\ce{O-H}$ covalent bonds.
:::

:::note{title="Bigger molecules, higher boiling points"}
Larger molecules generally have stronger forces between them, so their melting and boiling points are higher. Methane, $\ce{CH4}$, boils at about $-162\ ^\circ\text{C}$, while the much larger molecules in candle wax are solid at room temperature.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying covalent bonds break when a molecular substance boils"}
Boiling overcomes the weak forces **between** molecules. The strong covalent bonds **within** the molecules stay intact.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling covalent bonds weak"}
Covalent bonds are strong. Simple molecular substances have low boiling points because the forces between their molecules are weak.
:::

:::mistake{title="Drawing the wrong number of electrons"}
Count the outer electrons of every atom before you start (N 5, H 1 each) and check the total in your finished diagram. Each shared pair has one electron from each atom.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In dot-and-cross diagrams, use dots for one atom's electrons and crosses for the other's, put each shared pair in the overlap of the two shells, and include the non-bonding pairs. Examiners check that every atom other than hydrogen ends up with 8 outer electrons.
:::

:::exam-tip
When **explaining** a low boiling point, write "weak forces between molecules need little energy to overcome". Mentioning broken covalent bonds loses the mark.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two non-metal atoms; sharing gives each atom a noble-gas configuration.
- Single bonds share one pair, double bonds two pairs and triple bonds three pairs ($\ce{O2}$ has a double bond, $\ce{N2}$ a triple bond).
- In dot-and-cross diagrams, shared pairs sit where the shells overlap; hydrogen ends with 2 electrons and other atoms with 8.
- Simple molecular substances have low melting and boiling points because the forces between molecules are weak; the covalent bonds inside the molecules do not break.
- They do not conduct electricity because they have no ions or free electrons.
:::
