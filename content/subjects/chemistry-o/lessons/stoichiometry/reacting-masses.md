## Introduction

A steelworks buys iron ore and coke by the train-load, and a fertiliser plant buys sulfuric acid by the tanker. Ordering too much of one raw material wastes money; ordering too little stops production. Engineers use the balanced equation and the mole to work out exactly how much of each reactant is needed and how much product to expect.

This lesson is Cambridge IGCSE **Supplement** content and ZIMSEC Form 3 content (chemical equations and limiting reagents). You should already be able to balance equations and to use amount $=$ mass $\div$ molar mass.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mole ratio"}
The ratio of the amounts (in moles) of substances that react and form, given by the coefficients of the balanced equation. In $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$ the mole ratio $\ce{Mg} : \ce{O2} : \ce{MgO}$ is $2 : 1 : 2$.
:::

:::definition{term="Limiting reactant"}
The reactant that is completely used up first. It stops the reaction and so decides the maximum amount of product.
:::

:::definition{term="Reactant in excess"}
A reactant present in more than the amount needed; some of it is left over when the reaction stops.
:::

## The four-step method

1. Write the **balanced equation**.
2. Calculate the **amount (mol)** of the substance whose mass you know: $n = m \div M$.
3. Use the **mole ratio** from the equation to find the amount of the substance you want.
4. Convert that amount to a **mass**: $m = n \times M$.

Data for this lesson: C = 12, O = 16, Mg = 24, S = 32, Fe = 56, Cu = 64.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: burning magnesium]
Calculate the mass of magnesium oxide formed when 6.0 g of magnesium burns completely.

1. $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$
2. $n(\ce{Mg}) = 6.0 \div 24 = 0.25$ mol.
3. Mole ratio $\ce{Mg} : \ce{MgO} = 2 : 2 = 1 : 1$, so $n(\ce{MgO}) = 0.25$ mol.
4. $M(\ce{MgO}) = 40$ g/mol, so mass $= 0.25 \times 40 = 10$ g.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: iron from iron ore (a ratio that is not 1 : 1)]
Calculate the mass of iron that can be made from 32 kg of iron(III) oxide.

1. $\ce{Fe2O3 + 3CO -> 2Fe + 3CO2}$
2. $32\ \text{kg} = 32\,000$ g; $M(\ce{Fe2O3}) = 160$ g/mol; $n = 32\,000 \div 160 = 200$ mol.
3. Mole ratio $\ce{Fe2O3} : \ce{Fe} = 1 : 2$, so $n(\ce{Fe}) = 2 \times 200 = 400$ mol.
4. Mass of iron $= 400 \times 56 = 22\,400$ g $= 22.4$ kg.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: the mass of a reactant needed]
Calculate the mass of carbon needed to reduce 8.0 g of copper(II) oxide: $\ce{2CuO + C -> 2Cu + CO2}$.

1. $M(\ce{CuO}) = 64 + 16 = 80$ g/mol; $n(\ce{CuO}) = 8.0 \div 80 = 0.10$ mol.
2. Mole ratio $\ce{CuO} : \ce{C} = 2 : 1$, so $n(\ce{C}) = 0.10 \div 2 = 0.050$ mol.
3. Mass of carbon $= 0.050 \times 12 = 0.60$ g.
:::

## Limiting reactants

When the masses of **both** reactants are given, one of them usually runs out first. To find it:

1. Calculate the amount (mol) of each reactant.
2. Use the mole ratio to see how much of the second reactant the first one needs.
3. If less is available than is needed, the second reactant is limiting; otherwise the first one is.
4. Calculate the product from the **limiting** reactant only.

:::worked-solution[Example 4: iron and sulfur]
11.2 g of iron is heated with 8.0 g of sulfur: $\ce{Fe + S -> FeS}$. Which reactant is limiting, and what mass of iron(II) sulfide forms?

1. $n(\ce{Fe}) = 11.2 \div 56 = 0.20$ mol; $n(\ce{S}) = 8.0 \div 32 = 0.25$ mol.
2. The ratio is $1 : 1$, so 0.20 mol of iron needs only 0.20 mol of sulfur.
3. 0.25 mol of sulfur is available, so sulfur is in excess and **iron is the limiting reactant**.
4. $n(\ce{FeS}) = 0.20$ mol; $M(\ce{FeS}) = 88$ g/mol; mass $= 0.20 \times 88 = 17.6$ g.
5. Sulfur left over: $0.25 - 0.20 = 0.05$ mol, which is $0.05 \times 32 = 1.6$ g.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Moles, not masses, decide"}
The limiting reactant is not necessarily the one with the smaller mass. Compare **amounts in moles**, taking the mole ratio into account.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Skipping the mole ratio"}
In Example 2, using 200 mol of iron instead of 400 mol halves the answer. Always ask: "How many of these react with or form one of those?"
:::

:::mistake{title="Using masses in the ratio"}
The coefficients give a ratio of **moles**, not grams. 2 g of hydrogen does not react with 1 g of oxygen in $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calculating the product from the excess reactant"}
Once you know the limiting reactant, use only its amount to find the product. The excess reactant cannot all react.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing units"}
Convert kg or tonnes to grams before dividing by a molar mass in g/mol, and convert back at the end if the question asks for kg or tonnes.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Set your working out in a line or a small table: equation, moles, ratio, moles, mass. Examiners award marks for each stage, so a slip in the last step still keeps most of the marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked "which reactant is in excess? Show your working", you must show both amounts in moles and the comparison using the mole ratio. A statement without numbers earns nothing.
:::

:::exam-tip
Keep 3 significant figures in the final answer and carry unrounded values through the calculation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Balanced equation → moles of the known substance → mole ratio → moles of the unknown → mass.
- The coefficients in an equation give the mole ratio, not the mass ratio.
- The limiting reactant is used up first and decides the maximum amount of product.
- Find the limiting reactant by comparing amounts in moles with the mole ratio, not by comparing masses.
- Calculate the product, and the excess left over, from the limiting reactant.
:::
