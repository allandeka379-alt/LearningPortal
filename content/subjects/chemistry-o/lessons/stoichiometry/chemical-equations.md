## Introduction

Every gas stove in a Bulawayo kitchen, every bag of fertiliser made in Kwekwe and every tonne of copper refined from Zimbabwean ore depends on reactions that chemists describe with **equations**. An equation is a compact, exact summary of a reaction: which substances react, what they form, in what proportions, and in which physical states.

In this lesson you will write word equations, turn them into balanced symbol equations with state symbols, and deduce an equation from a description. In the Supplement section you will also write ionic equations, which show only the particles that actually change. You should already be able to write the formulae of elements and compounds.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Reactants and products"}
Reactants are the substances present at the start of a reaction; products are the new substances formed. In an equation the reactants are written on the left of the arrow and the products on the right.
:::

:::definition{term="Balanced symbol equation"}
An equation written with formulae in which there is the same number of atoms of each element on both sides. Atoms are not created or destroyed in a reaction, so mass is conserved.
:::

:::definition{term="State symbols"}
Letters in brackets after a formula showing its physical state: $\ce{(s)}$ solid, $\ce{(l)}$ liquid, $\ce{(g)}$ gas and $\ce{(aq)}$ aqueous, meaning dissolved in water.
:::

:::definition{term="Ionic equation (Supplement)"}
An equation that shows only the ions and other particles that take part in a reaction, leaving out the spectator ions.
:::

:::definition{term="Spectator ions (Supplement)"}
Ions that are present in the reaction mixture but are unchanged by the reaction: they are in solution at the start and still in solution at the end.
:::

## Word equations

A word equation names the reactants and products:

$$
\text{magnesium} + \text{oxygen} \rightarrow \text{magnesium oxide}
$$

The arrow means "react to form". A plus sign on the left means "reacts with"; on the right it means "and".

## Balancing symbol equations

Follow the same steps every time.

1. Write the word equation.
2. Write the **correct formula** of every substance. Never change a formula afterwards.
3. Count the atoms of each element on each side.
4. Balance by putting numbers (**coefficients**) in front of formulae. Start with the element that appears in the fewest formulae; leave hydrogen and oxygen to the end when they appear in several places.
5. Count again to check, then add state symbols.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: methane (biogas or natural gas) burning]
1. Word equation: methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.
2. Formulae: $\ce{CH4 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}$.
3. Carbon is balanced (1 each side). Hydrogen: 4 on the left, 2 on the right, so write $\ce{2H2O}$.
4. Oxygen: the right now has $2 + 2 = 4$ atoms, so write $\ce{2O2}$ on the left.
5. Balanced: $\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}$. Check: C 1 = 1, H 4 = 4, O 4 = 4.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: aluminium burning in oxygen]
1. Formulae: $\ce{Al + O2 -> Al2O3}$.
2. Oxygen: 2 on the left, 3 on the right. The lowest common multiple is 6, so write $\ce{3O2}$ and $\ce{2Al2O3}$.
3. Aluminium: the right now has $2 \times 2 = 4$ atoms, so write $\ce{4Al}$.
4. Balanced: $\ce{4Al(s) + 3O2(g) -> 2Al2O3(s)}$. Check: Al 4 = 4, O 6 = 6.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: limestone chips and dilute hydrochloric acid]
1. Word equation: calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid → calcium chloride + water + carbon dioxide.
2. Formulae: $\ce{CaCO3 + HCl -> CaCl2 + H2O + CO2}$.
3. Chlorine: 1 on the left, 2 on the right, so write $\ce{2HCl}$. Hydrogen is then 2 = 2.
4. With states: $\ce{CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}$. Check: Ca 1, C 1, O 3, H 2, Cl 2 on each side.
:::

## Choosing state symbols

- Metals, insoluble salts and **precipitates** are $\ce{(s)}$.
- Water is $\ce{(l)}$, never $\ce{(aq)}$; molten substances are also $\ce{(l)}$.
- Acids, alkalis and dissolved salts are $\ce{(aq)}$.
- Hydrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide, chlorine and other gases are $\ce{(g)}$.

## Deducing an equation from information (Supplement)

Examiners describe a reaction and ask for the equation. Pick out every reactant and product, write their formulae, balance, and use the description to choose the state symbols.

:::example{title="Heating zinc carbonate"}
"When solid zinc carbonate is heated strongly it forms solid zinc oxide and a gas that turns limewater milky."

The gas is carbon dioxide, so the equation is $\ce{ZnCO3(s) -> ZnO(s) + CO2(g)}$. It is already balanced: Zn 1, C 1 and O 3 on each side.
:::

## Ionic equations (Supplement)

Many reactions in solution involve only some of the ions present. To write an ionic equation:

1. Write the balanced equation with state symbols.
2. Split every substance that is present **as ions in solution** into its ions: dissolved ionic compounds such as salts and alkalis, and strong acids such as $\ce{HCl(aq)}$ and $\ce{H2SO4(aq)}$, which are fully ionised in water. Do not split solids, liquids (including water), gases or substances that stay as molecules.
3. Cross out the ions that appear unchanged on both sides (the spectator ions).
4. Check that both the atoms **and the total charges** balance.

:::worked-solution[Example 4: neutralisation of hydrochloric acid by sodium hydroxide]
1. $\ce{HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)}$
2. $\ce{H+(aq) + Cl-(aq) + Na+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq) + H2O(l)}$
3. $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ are spectators.
4. Ionic equation: $\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}$. Charge: $(+1) + (-1) = 0$ on the left and $0$ on the right.
:::

The same ionic equation describes the neutralisation of **any** strong acid (hydrochloric, sulfuric or nitric acid) by any alkali, because only the $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ ions change.

:::example{title="Precipitation and displacement"}
Precipitation (silver nitrate solution added to sodium chloride solution): $\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}$. The nitrate and sodium ions are spectators.

Displacement (zinc added to copper(II) sulfate solution): $\ce{Zn(s) + Cu^2+(aq) -> Zn^2+(aq) + Cu(s)}$. The sulfate ions are spectators; the charge is $2+$ on each side.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Changing a formula to balance an equation"}
Writing $\ce{H2O2}$ instead of $\ce{2H2O}$ changes water into hydrogen peroxide, a different substance. Only change the coefficients in front of formulae.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting diatomic elements"}
Oxygen gas is $\ce{O2}$, hydrogen gas is $\ce{H2}$ and chlorine gas is $\ce{Cl2}$. An equation with $\ce{O}$ as a reactant may look balanced, but it is wrong.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving water the state symbol (aq)"}
Water is the solvent, not a solute. Water formed in a reaction is $\ce{H2O(l)}$, or $\ce{H2O(g)}$ if it is steam.
:::

:::mistake{title="Splitting solids into ions"}
In an ionic equation a precipitate or an undissolved solid keeps its full formula, for example $\ce{AgCl(s)}$ and $\ce{CaCO3(s)}$. Only substances that are present as ions in solution are split.
:::

:::mistake{title="Unbalanced charges"}
$\ce{Fe + Cu^2+ -> Fe^3+ + Cu}$ has the atoms balanced but a charge of $2+$ on the left and $3+$ on the right, so it cannot be right. Check charges as well as atoms.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When a question says "include state symbols", the equation mark usually needs both correct balancing and all the state symbols. Write the states as you write each formula so that you do not forget one.
:::

:::exam-tip
After balancing, count each element once more on each side. One minute of checking protects a mark that is otherwise lost completely.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "deduce the equation" questions, underline every substance named in the stem and every clue to its state ("a white precipitate", "a gas", "solution", "molten").
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A word equation names the reactants and products; a symbol equation uses formulae.
- Balance with coefficients only, never by changing formulae, and check every element.
- State symbols: $\ce{(s)}$, $\ce{(l)}$, $\ce{(g)}$ and $\ce{(aq)}$; water is $\ce{(l)}$ and precipitates are $\ce{(s)}$.
- Ionic equations (Supplement) leave out spectator ions; only substances present as ions in solution (dissolved salts and alkalis, strong acids) are split into ions.
- In an ionic equation both the atoms and the total charges must balance.
:::
