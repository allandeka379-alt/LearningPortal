## Introduction

Gases are awkward to weigh but easy to measure by volume, with a gas syringe in the laboratory or a gas meter on a biogas digester on a farm in Masvingo. It turns out that the volume of a gas tells you directly how many particles it contains, whatever the gas is. This makes gas calculations some of the quickest in chemistry.

This lesson is Cambridge IGCSE **Supplement** content and ZIMSEC Form 4 content. You should already be able to use amount $=$ mass $\div$ molar mass and mole ratios from balanced equations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Molar gas volume"}
The volume occupied by one mole of any gas. At room temperature and pressure it is $24\ \text{dm}^3$ ($24\,000\ \text{cm}^3$).
:::

:::definition{term="Room temperature and pressure (r.t.p.)"}
Ordinary laboratory conditions, about 20 °C and one atmosphere. The value of 24 dm³ per mole applies only at r.t.p.
:::

:::formula{name="Amount of a gas at r.t.p."}
$$
\text{amount (mol)} = \frac{\text{volume (dm}^3)}{24} = \frac{\text{volume (cm}^3)}{24\,000}
$$
:::

:::keyfact{title="Volume units"}
$1\ \text{dm}^3 = 1000\ \text{cm}^3$. A cubic decimetre is the same as a litre.
:::

## Why every gas has the same molar volume

In a gas the particles are very far apart compared with their own size. The volume of a gas therefore depends on how many particles there are, not on how big or heavy each one is. Equal volumes of different gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules, so one mole of hydrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide or methane each occupies 24 dm³ at r.t.p.

![Four equal squares: 1 mole of hydrogen (2 g), oxygen (32 g), carbon dioxide (44 g) and methane (16 g) each occupy the same 24 dm3 at r.t.p., so the volume of a gas depends on the number of particles, not on their mass.](media:diagrams/molar-gas-volume-equal-volumes.png "Figure: equal numbers of gas molecules take the same volume, whatever their mass")

This only applies to **gases**. At r.t.p. water is a liquid: one mole of water (18 g) occupies only about 18 cm³.

## Converting between volume, amount and mass

Data for this lesson: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Ca = 40.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: volume from amount]
What volume does 0.25 mol of oxygen occupy at r.t.p.?

1. Volume $=$ amount $\times 24 = 0.25 \times 24 = 6.0\ \text{dm}^3$.
2. In cm³: $6.0 \times 1000 = 6000\ \text{cm}^3$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: amount and mass from a volume in cm³]
A gas syringe contains $600\ \text{cm}^3$ of carbon dioxide at r.t.p. Find the amount and the mass of the gas.

1. Amount $= 600 \div 24\,000 = 0.025$ mol.
2. $M(\ce{CO2}) = 44$ g/mol, so mass $= 0.025 \times 44 = 1.1$ g.
:::

## Volumes of gases in reactions

Combine the molar gas volume with the mole ratio from the balanced equation.

![Reacting volumes: 50 cm3 of methane burns in 100 cm3 of oxygen to give 50 cm3 of carbon dioxide, a volume ratio of 1 : 2 : 1 matching the equation; liquid water adds no gas volume.](media:diagrams/molar-gas-volume-reacting-volumes.png "Figure: for gases the equation coefficients are the volume ratio (Example 4)")

:::worked-solution[Example 3: gas made from a known mass]
Calculate the volume of carbon dioxide, at r.t.p., produced when 10 g of calcium carbonate reacts with excess hydrochloric acid.

1. $\ce{CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}$
2. $M(\ce{CaCO3}) = 100$ g/mol; $n = 10 \div 100 = 0.10$ mol.
3. Ratio $\ce{CaCO3} : \ce{CO2} = 1 : 1$, so $n(\ce{CO2}) = 0.10$ mol.
4. Volume $= 0.10 \times 24 = 2.4\ \text{dm}^3$ ($2400\ \text{cm}^3$).
:::

When **all** the substances you are comparing are gases, the mole ratio is also the volume ratio, so you do not need to convert to moles at all.

:::worked-solution[Example 4: reacting volumes of gases]
What volume of oxygen is needed to burn $50\ \text{cm}^3$ of methane completely, and what volume of carbon dioxide forms? (All volumes at r.t.p.)

1. $\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}$
2. Volume ratio $\ce{CH4} : \ce{O2} : \ce{CO2} = 1 : 2 : 1$.
3. Oxygen needed $= 2 \times 50 = 100\ \text{cm}^3$; carbon dioxide formed $= 50\ \text{cm}^3$.
4. The water is a liquid at r.t.p., so it adds nothing to the gas volume.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using 24 with a volume in cm³"}
$600\ \text{cm}^3 \div 24 = 25$ mol is wildly wrong. Divide cm³ by $24\,000$, or convert to dm³ first ($0.600\ \text{dm}^3 \div 24 = 0.025$ mol).
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the molar gas volume for solids and liquids"}
24 dm³ per mole applies only to gases. Water at r.t.p., or a solid such as calcium carbonate, does not occupy 24 dm³ per mole.
:::

:::mistake{title="Doubling the volume for diatomic gases"}
One mole of oxygen **molecules** ($\ce{O2}$) occupies 24 dm³, just like one mole of helium atoms. The volume depends on the number of moles of gas particles.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the mole ratio"}
In $\ce{Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2}$ the ratio $\ce{HCl} : \ce{H2}$ is $2 : 1$. Using 1 : 1 doubles the volume of hydrogen.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Check which unit the question wants. "Give your answer in cm³" with an answer in dm³ loses the mark even if the chemistry is right.
:::

:::exam-tip
When a question gives gas volumes and asks for another gas volume, use the coefficients directly as a volume ratio: it saves time and avoids rounding errors.
:::

:::exam-tip
If an answer for an ordinary laboratory experiment comes out at hundreds of dm³, look for a cm³/dm³ slip: a 100 cm³ gas syringe holds only $0.1\ \text{dm}^3$.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- One mole of any gas occupies 24 dm³ (24 000 cm³) at r.t.p.
- Amount of gas = volume in dm³ ÷ 24, or volume in cm³ ÷ 24 000.
- Combine with $n = m \div M$ to convert between the volume and the mass of a gas.
- In a reaction, use the mole ratio to find the amount of gas, then its volume.
- For gases only, the coefficients of the equation give the ratio of their volumes.
:::
