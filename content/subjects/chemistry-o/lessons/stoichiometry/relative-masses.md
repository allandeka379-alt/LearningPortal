## Introduction

A single atom is far too small to weigh: a carbon atom has a mass of about $2 \times 10^{-23}$ g. Yet a fertiliser plant has to know exactly how many tonnes of ammonium nitrate it can make from a tonne of ammonia, and a lime works has to know how much quicklime a kiln-load of limestone will give. Chemists solve this by comparing the masses of atoms with each other on a **relative** scale.

In this lesson you will learn what relative atomic mass means, calculate the relative mass of any molecule or ionic compound from its formula, and use these values to work out reacting masses by simple proportion. You need to be able to read formulae, including brackets. Relative atomic masses are printed in the Periodic Table in your examination paper; this lesson uses H = 1, C = 12, N = 14, O = 16, Mg = 24, S = 32, Ca = 40 and Cu = 64.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Relative atomic mass, Ar"}
The average mass of the atoms of an element (taking account of its isotopes) compared with one-twelfth of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
:::

:::definition{term="Relative molecular mass, Mr"}
The sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in one molecule of a substance.
:::

:::definition{term="Relative formula mass, Mr"}
The same idea for an ionic compound, which has no molecules: the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in its formula.
:::

:::keyfact{title="No units"}
$A_r$ and $M_r$ are ratios: one mass divided by another. They have **no units**.
:::

## Why relative atomic masses are averages

On this scale a carbon-12 atom has a mass of exactly 12. A magnesium atom is about twice as heavy, so $A_r(\ce{Mg}) = 24$. Many elements are mixtures of isotopes, so their $A_r$ is a weighted average of the isotope masses. Chlorine contains about three atoms of chlorine-35 for every atom of chlorine-37, so its $A_r$ is 35.5, not a whole number. Always use the value from the Periodic Table you are given.

## Calculating Mr from a formula

Multiply the $A_r$ of each element by the number of its atoms in the formula, then add.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: carbon dioxide and sulfuric acid]
1. $\ce{CO2}$: one C and two O, so $M_r = 12 + (2 \times 16) = 44$.
2. $\ce{H2SO4}$: two H, one S and four O, so $M_r = (2 \times 1) + 32 + (4 \times 16) = 98$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: formulae with brackets]
1. Calcium nitrate, $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$: one Ca, two N and six O. $M_r = 40 + (2 \times 14) + (6 \times 16) = 40 + 28 + 96 = 164$.
2. Ammonium sulfate, $\ce{(NH4)2SO4}$: two N, eight H, one S and four O. $M_r = 28 + 8 + 32 + 64 = 132$.
:::

A tidy way to set out a long calculation is a small table: element, number of atoms, $A_r$, and their product. Then add the last column.

## Reacting masses by simple proportion

A balanced equation tells you the ratio of the **numbers** of particles that react. Multiplying each formula by its $M_r$ turns that into a ratio of **masses**. Because the ratio stays the same whatever units you use (g, kg or tonnes), you can scale it up or down. This method needs no moles: it is Cambridge Core content.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: quicklime from limestone in a kiln]
Calculate the mass of quicklime (calcium oxide) made from 25 tonnes of pure limestone (calcium carbonate).

1. Equation: $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$.
2. $M_r(\ce{CaCO3}) = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100$ and $M_r(\ce{CaO}) = 40 + 16 = 56$.
3. Mass ratio: 100 tonnes of $\ce{CaCO3}$ give 56 tonnes of $\ce{CaO}$.
4. Scale: $25 \div 100 = 0.25$, so the mass of $\ce{CaO}$ is $0.25 \times 56 = 14$ tonnes.
:::

You can also scale from experimental results, without using $M_r$ at all.

:::worked-solution[Example 4: scaling from an experiment]
In an experiment, 6.4 g of copper formed 8.0 g of copper(II) oxide. What mass of copper(II) oxide would form from 16 g of copper?

1. $16 \div 6.4 = 2.5$, so 2.5 times as much copper is used.
2. Mass of copper(II) oxide $= 2.5 \times 8.0 = 20$ g.
:::

Mass is conserved in every reaction, so the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.

:::example{title="Finding a missing mass"}
2.4 g of magnesium burns to form 4.0 g of magnesium oxide. The mass of oxygen that combined with the magnesium is $4.0 - 2.4 = 1.6$ g.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to multiply the whole bracket"}
For $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$ some students add $40 + 14 + 48 = 102$. The 2 doubles everything in the bracket, giving 164.
:::

:::mistake{title="Including the coefficient in Mr"}
In $\ce{2H2O}$ the 2 in front means two molecules. $M_r(\ce{H2O})$ is still 18; the two molecules together have a relative mass of 36. Use the coefficient only when working out the mass ratio from an equation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving Mr a unit"}
Writing "$M_r = 44$ g" is wrong. $M_r$ has no units. (Molar mass, which you meet with the mole, does have the unit g/mol.)
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the atomic number instead of Ar"}
The Periodic Table shows two numbers for each element. Use the larger one, the relative atomic mass, not the proton number: O = 16, not 8.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Write the full sum, for example $40 + (2 \times 14) + (6 \times 16) = 164$. If you press a wrong key, the working can still earn a method mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
In reacting-mass questions, write the mass ratio in words before scaling: "100 t of $\ce{CaCO3}$ gives 56 t of $\ce{CaO}$". Keep the same unit throughout and include it in the answer.
:::

:::exam-tip
Use exactly the $A_r$ values given in the question or the Periodic Table (Cl = 35.5, Cu = 64). Answers calculated with other values may not match the mark scheme.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- $A_r$ compares the average mass of an element's atoms with one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
- $M_r$ is the sum of the $A_r$ values of all the atoms in a formula; brackets multiply everything inside.
- Relative masses are ratios and have no units.
- A balanced equation and $M_r$ values give a mass ratio that can be scaled in grams, kilograms or tonnes.
- The total mass of products equals the total mass of reactants.
:::
