## Introduction

The equation for making quicklime says that 100 tonnes of calcium carbonate give 56 tonnes of calcium oxide. A real lime works never gets quite that much. The limestone dug from the quarry is not pure calcium carbonate, some limestone in the kiln is not heated enough to decompose, and some powder is lost as dust. Chemists measure these shortfalls with two numbers: **percentage purity** for the raw material and **percentage yield** for the product.

This lesson is Cambridge IGCSE **Supplement** content and ZIMSEC Form 4 content. You should already be able to calculate reacting masses and gas volumes with the mole.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Theoretical yield"}
The maximum mass of product that could be made, calculated from the balanced equation and the mass of the limiting reactant.
:::

:::definition{term="Actual yield"}
The mass of product actually obtained in the experiment or process.
:::

:::formula{name="Percentage yield"}
$$
\text{percentage yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100
$$
:::

:::formula{name="Percentage purity"}
$$
\text{percentage purity} = \frac{\text{mass of the pure substance in the sample}}{\text{mass of the impure sample}} \times 100
$$
:::

## Why actual yields are less than 100%

- The reaction may be **reversible**, so it never goes to completion (the Haber process is an example).
- **Side reactions** may make unwanted products from some of the reactants.
- The reaction may be **incomplete** because it was stopped too early or the reactants were not mixed or heated enough.
- Product is **lost** when it is filtered, transferred between containers, washed or crystallised; some stays dissolved in the solution.
- The reactants may be **impure**.

## Calculating percentage yield

Data for this lesson: H = 1, C = 12, N = 14, O = 16, Ca = 40, Zn = 65.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: quicklime in the laboratory]
50.0 g of pure calcium carbonate is heated strongly and 24.5 g of calcium oxide is obtained. Calculate the percentage yield.

1. $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$; $n(\ce{CaCO3}) = 50.0 \div 100 = 0.500$ mol.
2. Ratio $1 : 1$, so the theoretical yield of $\ce{CaO}$ is $0.500 \times 56 = 28.0$ g.
3. Percentage yield $= \dfrac{24.5}{28.0} \times 100 = 87.5\%$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: ammonium nitrate at a fertiliser plant]
3.40 kg of ammonia gives 15.2 kg of ammonium nitrate: $\ce{NH3 + HNO3 -> NH4NO3}$. Calculate the percentage yield.

1. $n(\ce{NH3}) = 3400 \div 17 = 200$ mol, so up to 200 mol of $\ce{NH4NO3}$ can form.
2. Theoretical yield $= 200 \times 80 = 16\,000$ g $= 16.0$ kg.
3. Percentage yield $= \dfrac{15.2}{16.0} \times 100 = 95.0\%$.
:::

## Calculating percentage purity

Work out, from a product you can measure, how much of the pure substance must have been in the sample. Then compare it with the mass of the whole sample.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: purity of limestone from the mass of gas]
2.50 g of crushed limestone reacts with excess hydrochloric acid and gives 0.880 g of carbon dioxide. The impurities do not react with the acid. Calculate the percentage purity of the limestone.

1. $\ce{CaCO3 + 2HCl -> CaCl2 + H2O + CO2}$
2. $n(\ce{CO2}) = 0.880 \div 44 = 0.0200$ mol, so $n(\ce{CaCO3}) = 0.0200$ mol.
3. Mass of $\ce{CaCO3}$ $= 0.0200 \times 100 = 2.00$ g.
4. Percentage purity $= \dfrac{2.00}{2.50} \times 100 = 80.0\%$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 4: purity of zinc from a gas volume]
1.30 g of impure zinc reacts with excess acid and gives $360\ \text{cm}^3$ of hydrogen at r.t.p.: $\ce{Zn + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2}$. The impurities do not react.

1. $n(\ce{H2}) = 360 \div 24\,000 = 0.0150$ mol, so $n(\ce{Zn}) = 0.0150$ mol.
2. Mass of zinc $= 0.0150 \times 65 = 0.975$ g.
3. Percentage purity $= \dfrac{0.975}{1.30} \times 100 = 75.0\%$.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Turning the fraction upside down"}
Percentage yield is actual ÷ theoretical. Dividing the other way gives a value over 100% for a reaction that clearly lost product.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the impure mass as if it were pure"}
In a purity question, never calculate moles from the mass of the impure sample. Use the product, such as the gas given off, to find how much pure substance reacted.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calculating the theoretical yield from the reactant in excess"}
The theoretical yield always comes from the **limiting** reactant.
:::

:::mistake{title="Accepting a yield above 100%"}
A percentage yield over 100% is impossible for a pure, dry product. It usually means the product was still wet or contained impurities.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Show the theoretical yield as a separate line of working with its unit before calculating the percentage. It often carries its own mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to "suggest why the yield is less than 100%", give a reason that fits the experiment described: losses during filtration for a precipitate, a reversible reaction for the Haber process, incomplete decomposition for heating limestone.
:::

:::exam-tip
Give percentages to 3 significant figures and check they are sensible: purities and yields lie between 0% and 100%.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Theoretical yield is calculated from the equation and the limiting reactant; actual yield is measured.
- Percentage yield = actual yield ÷ theoretical yield × 100.
- Yields fall short because of reversible, incomplete or side reactions, impure reactants and losses during separation.
- Percentage purity = mass of pure substance ÷ mass of sample × 100; find the pure mass from a product such as a gas.
- A result above 100% signals an error, such as a wet product.
:::
