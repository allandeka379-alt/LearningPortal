## Introduction

At a market in Mbare nobody counts grains of rice: rice is sold by the kilogram, and because every grain has roughly the same mass, the mass tells you how many grains you are buying. Chemists face the same problem on a far smaller scale. Atoms are so small that counting them one by one is impossible, so chemists **count particles by weighing**, using a unit called the **mole**.

This lesson is Cambridge IGCSE **Supplement** content and ZIMSEC Form 3 core content. Everything else in quantitative chemistry (reacting masses, gas volumes, concentrations and titrations) is built on it. You should already be able to calculate relative atomic and molecular masses ($A_r$ and $M_r$).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mole (mol)"}
The unit of amount of substance. One mole of a substance contains $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ particles (atoms, molecules or ions).
:::

:::definition{term="Avogadro constant"}
The number of particles in one mole: $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ per mole.
:::

:::definition{term="Molar mass, M"}
The mass of one mole of a substance, in g/mol. It has the same number as the $A_r$ or $M_r$: the molar mass of water ($M_r = 18$) is $18$ g/mol.
:::

:::formula{name="Amount of substance"}
$$
\text{amount (mol)} = \frac{\text{mass (g)}}{\text{molar mass (g/mol)}}
$$

Rearranged: mass = amount $\times$ molar mass, and molar mass = mass $\div$ amount.
:::

:::formula{name="Number of particles"}
$$
\text{number of particles} = \text{amount (mol)} \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}
$$
:::

## Why the mole works

The $A_r$ of magnesium (24) is twice that of carbon (12), so a magnesium atom is, on average, twice as heavy as a carbon atom. That means 24 g of magnesium and 12 g of carbon contain the **same number** of atoms. That number, which is very nearly the number of atoms in 12 g of carbon-12, is the Avogadro constant, $6.02 \times 10^{23}$. So the $A_r$ or $M_r$ of any substance, in grams, always contains one mole of particles.

## Worked examples

Data for this lesson: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Ca = 40.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: amount from mass]
How many moles of water are in 9.0 g of water?

1. Molar mass of $\ce{H2O}$ $= 2 + 16 = 18$ g/mol.
2. Amount $= \dfrac{9.0}{18} = 0.50$ mol.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: mass from amount]
Calculate the mass of 0.25 mol of calcium carbonate, $\ce{CaCO3}$.

1. Molar mass $= 40 + 12 + 48 = 100$ g/mol.
2. Mass $= 0.25 \times 100 = 25$ g.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: counting molecules and atoms]
How many molecules, and how many atoms, are in 0.50 mol of carbon dioxide?

1. Molecules $= 0.50 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 3.01 \times 10^{23}$.
2. Each $\ce{CO2}$ molecule has 3 atoms, so atoms $= 3 \times 3.01 \times 10^{23} = 9.03 \times 10^{23}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 4: finding a molar mass]
A sample of a gas containing 0.20 mol has a mass of 8.8 g. Calculate its molar mass and suggest what the gas might be.

1. Molar mass $= \dfrac{\text{mass}}{\text{amount}} = \dfrac{8.8}{0.20} = 44$ g/mol.
2. $M_r = 44$, which matches carbon dioxide ($12 + 32 = 44$). Other substances also have $M_r = 44$, so further tests would be needed to be sure.
:::

## Atoms, molecules and ions: say which particle

"One mole of oxygen" is ambiguous. One mole of oxygen **molecules**, $\ce{O2}$, has a mass of 32 g and contains two moles of oxygen **atoms**. Always state the particle.

For ionic compounds, count the ions in the formula. One mole of calcium chloride, $\ce{CaCl2}$, contains 1 mol of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ ions and 2 mol of $\ce{Cl-}$ ions: 3 mol of ions altogether.

:::keyfact{title="The mole map"}
mass (g) $\xrightarrow{\ \div\ M\ }$ amount (mol) $\xrightarrow{\ \times\ 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ }$ number of particles. Go backwards by multiplying by $M$ or dividing by $6.02 \times 10^{23}$.
:::

## Using your calculator with standard form

Enter $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ with the $\times 10^x$ (or EXP) key: 6.02, then $\times 10^x$, then 23. Do not type "$\times 10$" before pressing that key: the calculator then works out $6.02 \times 10 \times 10^{23}$, an answer ten times too large. Keep unrounded values in the calculator until the final step.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up atoms and molecules"}
0.50 mol of $\ce{CO2}$ contains $3.01 \times 10^{23}$ molecules but $9.03 \times 10^{23}$ atoms. Read the question: does it ask for molecules, atoms or ions?
:::

:::mistake{title="Dividing the wrong way round"}
Amount is mass **divided by** molar mass. A quick check: amounts of everyday lab samples are usually small numbers such as 0.05 mol, not hundreds of moles.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to convert kilograms to grams"}
Molar mass is in g/mol, so a mass in kg must be multiplied by 1000 first: 2.4 kg of magnesium is 2400 g, which is 100 mol.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the proton number"}
The molar mass of oxygen atoms is 16 g/mol, not 8 g/mol: use the relative atomic mass, not the proton number.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Write the formula you are using, then substitute, then give the answer with its unit: "amount $= 9.0 \div 18 = 0.50$ mol". Method marks are awarded for correct working.
:::

:::exam-tip
Give answers to 3 significant figures unless the question says otherwise, and write very large or very small numbers in standard form, for example $3.01 \times 10^{23}$.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Calculate the number of moles" and "calculate the amount" mean the same thing. Amount is always measured in mol.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- One mole contains $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ particles (the Avogadro constant).
- Molar mass in g/mol has the same number as $A_r$ or $M_r$.
- Amount (mol) = mass (g) ÷ molar mass (g/mol); rearrange it to find a mass or a molar mass.
- Number of particles = amount × $6.02 \times 10^{23}$; multiply by the atoms per molecule (or ions per formula) when asked for atoms or ions.
- Always state which particle (atoms, molecules or ions) you are counting.
:::
