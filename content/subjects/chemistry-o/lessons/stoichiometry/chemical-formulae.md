## Introduction

A bag of fertiliser in a farm store near Chinhoyi is labelled **ammonium nitrate, $\ce{NH4NO3}$**. Those few symbols tell a chemist anywhere in the world exactly which elements are in the bag and in what proportions. A chemical formula is the shorthand of chemistry: every equation, every calculation with moles and every practical report depends on writing formulae correctly.

In this lesson you will learn to read a formula, to build the formula of an ionic compound from the charges (or valencies) of its ions, to name compounds, and to work out a formula from a model or a diagram. You should already know the symbols of the common elements and that metal atoms lose electrons to form positive ions while non-metal atoms gain electrons to form negative ions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Chemical formula"}
A combination of symbols and numbers that shows which elements a substance contains and how many atoms (or ions) of each there are, or the ratio in which they combine.
:::

:::definition{term="Molecular formula"}
The number and type of each atom in one molecule of a substance. The molecular formula of ethene is $\ce{C2H4}$: each molecule has two carbon atoms and four hydrogen atoms.
:::

:::definition{term="Empirical formula (Supplement)"}
The simplest whole-number ratio of the different atoms or ions in a compound. The empirical formula of ethene is $\ce{CH2}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Valency"}
The combining power of an atom or group of atoms. For an ion it equals the size of the charge: the valency of $\ce{Mg^2+}$ is 2 and the valency of $\ce{Cl-}$ is 1.
:::

:::definition{term="Compound ion"}
A group of atoms that carries an overall charge and stays together as one unit in compounds and reactions, for example the sulfate ion $\ce{SO4^2-}$ and the ammonium ion $\ce{NH4+}$.
:::

## Reading a formula

- A small number written after a symbol (a **subscript**) applies only to the atom just before it: $\ce{H2O}$ has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
- A subscript after a bracket multiplies everything inside the bracket.
- A large number in front of a formula (a **coefficient**) multiplies the whole formula: $\ce{3H2O}$ means three water molecules, containing 6 hydrogen atoms and 3 oxygen atoms.

:::example{title="Counting the atoms in calcium nitrate"}
In $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$ the bracket is multiplied by 2, so there is 1 calcium atom, $1 \times 2 = 2$ nitrogen atoms and $3 \times 2 = 6$ oxygen atoms: 9 atoms in one formula unit.
:::

Most metals and the noble gases are written as single atoms: $\ce{Fe}$, $\ce{Cu}$, $\ce{Na}$, $\ce{He}$. Seven common elements exist as **diatomic molecules** and must be written with a 2: $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{N2}$, $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{F2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{Br2}$ and $\ce{I2}$.

## Ion charges and valencies

For the main groups of the Periodic Table the charge of an ion follows from the group number.

| Group | Valency | Typical ions |
| --- | :---: | --- |
| I | 1 | $\ce{Li+}$, $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{K+}$ |
| II | 2 | $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Ba^2+}$ |
| III | 3 | $\ce{Al^3+}$ |
| IV | 4 | usually forms covalent bonds, as in $\ce{CH4}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ |
| V | 3 | $\ce{N^3-}$ (nitride); covalent in $\ce{NH3}$ |
| VI | 2 | $\ce{O^2-}$ (oxide), $\ce{S^2-}$ (sulfide) |
| VII | 1 | $\ce{F-}$, $\ce{Cl-}$, $\ce{Br-}$, $\ce{I-}$ |

Transition metals can form more than one ion, for example $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$, $\ce{Cu+}$ and $\ce{Cu^2+}$; zinc forms only $\ce{Zn^2+}$ and silver only $\ce{Ag+}$.

You must also learn the common **compound ions**:

| Name | Formula | Charge |
| --- | --- | :---: |
| ammonium | $\ce{NH4+}$ | 1+ |
| hydroxide | $\ce{OH-}$ | 1− |
| nitrate | $\ce{NO3-}$ | 1− |
| hydrogencarbonate | $\ce{HCO3-}$ | 1− |
| carbonate | $\ce{CO3^2-}$ | 2− |
| sulfate | $\ce{SO4^2-}$ | 2− |
| phosphate (in fertilisers) | $\ce{PO4^3-}$ | 3− |

## Writing the formula of an ionic compound

*Cambridge Supplement (3.1.6): deducing the formula of any ionic compound from the charges on its ions. Core candidates must still know the formulae of the compounds named in the syllabus, and this method is the easiest way to work them out. ZIMSEC Form 3.*

An ionic compound has no overall charge, so **the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge**. Two methods give the same answer.

- **Balancing charges:** choose the smallest number of each ion that makes the charges cancel.
- **Valency swap (the method many ZIMSEC teachers use):** write the two symbols, write each valency above, swap the valencies to become subscripts, then simplify and leave out any 1s.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: aluminium oxide]
1. The ions are $\ce{Al^3+}$ and $\ce{O^2-}$.
2. The lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6: two $\ce{Al^3+}$ give $+6$ and three $\ce{O^2-}$ give $-6$.
3. The formula is $\ce{Al2O3}$.
4. Valency swap check: Al (valency 3) and O (valency 2) swap to give $\ce{Al2O3}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: calcium nitrate]
1. The ions are $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$.
2. One $\ce{Ca^2+}$ needs two $\ce{NO3-}$ ions to balance its $+2$ charge.
3. Two compound ions are needed, so the nitrate goes in brackets: $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: ammonium sulfate, a common fertiliser]
1. The ions are $\ce{NH4+}$ and $\ce{SO4^2-}$.
2. Two $\ce{NH4+}$ ions balance one $\ce{SO4^2-}$ ion.
3. The formula is $\ce{(NH4)2SO4}$. No bracket is needed around the sulfate because there is only one.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 4: iron(III) hydroxide]
1. The Roman numeral (III) tells you the ion is $\ce{Fe^3+}$; hydroxide is $\ce{OH-}$.
2. Three $\ce{OH-}$ ions balance one $\ce{Fe^3+}$.
3. The formula is $\ce{Fe(OH)3}$.
:::

Simplify where you can. For magnesium oxide the valency swap gives $\ce{Mg2O2}$, which simplifies to $\ce{MgO}$ because the ions are in a $1 : 1$ ratio.

## Naming compounds

- A compound of just two elements ends in **-ide**: sodium chloride, magnesium oxide, calcium sulfide. The metal is named first. (Hydroxides, such as sodium hydroxide, also end in -ide although they contain three elements.)
- A compound ion containing oxygen usually ends in **-ate**: sulfate, nitrate, carbonate, phosphate.
- For a metal that can form more than one ion, a **Roman numeral** gives the charge on the metal ion: copper(I) oxide is $\ce{Cu2O}$, copper(II) oxide is $\ce{CuO}$, and iron(II) sulfate is $\ce{FeSO4}$.

## Formulae from models and diagrams

Examiners often show a model or diagram and ask for the formula. Count each type of atom, or find the ratio of the ions.

:::example{title="From a molecular model"}
A ball-and-stick model shows one carbon atom bonded to four hydrogen atoms: the formula is $\ce{CH4}$. A model of an ethanol molecule has two carbon atoms, six hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom: $\ce{C2H6O}$, usually written $\ce{C2H5OH}$ to show the $\ce{-OH}$ group.
:::

:::example{title="From a diagram of ions (Supplement)"}
A diagram of part of an ionic lattice shows two potassium ions for every oxide ion, so the formula is $\ce{K2O}$. A dot-and-cross diagram showing one calcium ion with the arrangement $[2,8,8]^{2+}$ and two chloride ions with the arrangement $[2,8,8]^{-}$ represents $\ce{CaCl2}$. The formula of an ionic compound always gives the simplest ratio of its ions, so it is an empirical formula.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Leaving out brackets"}
Calcium nitrate is $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$, not $\ce{CaNO32}$ or $\ce{CaNO3}$. Without the bracket, the final 2 would be read as part of the number of oxygen atoms instead of doubling the whole nitrate ion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading a Roman numeral as a number of atoms"}
The numeral is the **charge** on the metal ion. Iron(III) oxide contains $\ce{Fe^3+}$ ions and is $\ce{Fe2O3}$, not $\ce{Fe3O}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing chlorine gas as Cl"}
Chlorine, oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen and the other halogens are diatomic: write $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{N2}$, $\ce{H2}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Showing charges inside a compound's formula"}
Charges appear on the formulae of ions, not on the formula of the compound: write $\ce{NaCl}$, not $\ce{Na+Cl-}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Simplifying a molecular formula"}
Only ionic formulae (and empirical formulae) are simplified. Hydrogen peroxide is $\ce{H2O2}$ and ethene is $\ce{C2H4}$: these are the real numbers of atoms in one molecule.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Capital letters matter. $\ce{Co}$ is cobalt but $\ce{CO}$ is carbon monoxide. Write the second letter of a symbol in lower case and make subscripts clearly smaller and lower.
:::

:::exam-tip
When a question gives you an unfamiliar ion, such as "the chromate ion, $\ce{CrO4^2-}$", use the charge it gives you and balance the charges exactly as you would for a familiar ion.
:::

:::exam-tip
"State the formula" needs only the formula. "Deduce the formula" from a diagram earns the mark only if your formula matches the diagram exactly, so count the atoms or ions twice.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A formula shows the elements in a substance and the number or ratio of their atoms; brackets multiply everything inside them.
- Seven common elements are diatomic: $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{N2}$, $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{F2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{Br2}$ and $\ce{I2}$.
- In an ionic compound the positive and negative charges balance; the valency swap gives the same result.
- Learn the compound ions $\ce{NH4+}$, $\ce{OH-}$, $\ce{NO3-}$, $\ce{HCO3-}$, $\ce{CO3^2-}$, $\ce{SO4^2-}$ and $\ce{PO4^3-}$, and use brackets when more than one is needed.
- A Roman numeral in a name gives the charge on the metal ion; "-ide" usually means just two elements (hydroxide is an exception), and "-ate" usually means a compound ion with oxygen.
:::
