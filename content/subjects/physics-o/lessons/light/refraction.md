## Introduction

A straight stick dipped into a river looks bent at the surface. A swimming pool looks shallower than it really is. A glass of water makes a pencil behind it look shifted. These effects are caused by **refraction**: light changes direction when it passes from one material into another.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Refraction"}
The change in direction of light as it passes at an angle from one medium into another, caused by a change in its speed.
:::

:::definition{term="Angle of refraction, r"}
The angle between the refracted ray and the normal.
:::

:::definition{term="Refractive index, n (Cambridge Supplement; ZIMSEC)"}
The ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum (or air) to its speed in the medium.
:::

:::formula{name="Refractive index"}
$$
n = \frac{\text{speed of light in vacuum}}{\text{speed of light in medium}} = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r}
$$
:::

:::keyfact
**Laws of refraction**: (1) the incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal all lie in the same plane; (2) for two given media, $\dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r}$ is a constant (Snell's law). That constant is the refractive index.
:::

## Which way does light bend?

Light travels at about $3.0 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$ in air but more slowly in glass or water (optically **denser** media).

- Entering a **denser** medium (air to glass), light **slows down** and bends **towards** the normal.
- Leaving into a **less dense** medium (glass to air), light **speeds up** and bends **away from** the normal.
- A ray that meets the boundary **along the normal** ($i = 0$) passes straight through without changing direction (but it still slows down).

### Investigating refraction

Place a rectangular glass block on paper and draw its outline. Shine a ray from a ray box into one side at a measured angle of incidence, mark the ray coming out of the opposite side with crosses, remove the block and join up the path. Draw normals and measure the angles. The ray leaving a parallel-sided block is **parallel** to the ray entering it, but shifted sideways.

## Calculating refractive index

:::worked-solution[Refractive index of a glass block]
A ray enters a glass block from air with an angle of incidence of $40^\circ$; the angle of refraction is $25^\circ$.

1. $n = \dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \dfrac{\sin 40^\circ}{\sin 25^\circ} = \dfrac{0.643}{0.423}$.
2. $n = 1.52$.
:::

:::worked-solution[The speed of light in glass]
Glass has a refractive index of $1.5$.

1. Speed in glass $= \dfrac{\text{speed in vacuum}}{n} = \dfrac{3.0 \times 10^{8}}{1.5}$.
2. Speed in glass $= 2.0 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$.
:::

## Apparent depth (ZIMSEC)

Light from a stone on the bottom of a pool bends **away from** the normal as it leaves the water. Your eye traces the rays back in straight lines, so the stone appears **higher** than it really is: the pool looks shallower. This is **apparent depth**, and it is why a stick dipped in water looks bent at the surface.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Light always bends towards the normal"}
Only when it slows down (entering a denser medium). Leaving glass for air, it bends away from the normal.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using angles from the surface"}
$i$ and $r$ are measured from the normal, not from the surface of the block.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "explain why light refracts" questions, mention the change in **speed** at the boundary; "it bends" alone does not explain.
:::

:::exam-tip
When using $n = \sin i / \sin r$, $i$ is the angle in air (the faster medium). Check your answer: refractive index is always greater than 1.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Refraction is a change of direction caused by a change of speed at a boundary.
- Entering a denser medium, light slows and bends towards the normal; leaving it, light speeds up and bends away.
- Light along the normal is not deviated.
- $n = \dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \dfrac{\text{speed in vacuum}}{\text{speed in medium}}$.
- Refraction makes water look shallower than it is (apparent depth).
:::
