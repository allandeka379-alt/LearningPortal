## Introduction

Look up in a school hall, a shop or a warehouse and you will see beams: long members that carry loads across a gap. The steel joists of a roof, the concrete lintel above a door and the wooden plank of a footbridge over a stream are all beams. The shape of a beam's cross-section decides how strong and how stiff it is, and engineers choose shapes that are strong without wasting material. This lesson covers ZIMSEC topic 5.1.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Beam"}
A long, rigid structural member, usually horizontal, that carries loads across a span between supports.
:::

:::definition{term="Tension"}
A pulling (stretching) force inside a material.
:::

:::definition{term="Compression"}
A pushing (squashing) force inside a material.
:::

:::definition{term="Shear"}
Forces that make one layer of a material slide over the next.
:::

:::definition{term="Buckling"}
The sudden sideways bending of a long, thin member when it is compressed.
:::

## Cross-sections of beams

Beams are described by the shape of their cross-section (the shape you see if you cut through the beam):

| Section | Description |
| --- | --- |
| solid rectangular | a plank or a solid concrete lintel |
| I (or H) section | two flat flanges joined by a thin upright web |
| T, L (angle) and Z sections | rolled steel shapes used in frames and supports |
| box section | a hollow rectangular tube |
| O (tube) or cylindrical | a hollow round pipe, or a solid round bar |

## What happens inside a loaded beam

When a beam supported at both ends carries a load in the middle, it bends (sags) slightly:

![A beam loaded at the centre sags: the top layers are in compression and the bottom layers in tension, with a neutral axis between that is neither stretched nor squashed. Below are cross-sections: a plank on edge bends much less than flat, and I, box and tube sections put material where the stress is greatest.](media:diagrams/beam-stresses-sections.png "Figure: stresses inside a loaded beam, and common beam cross-sections")

- the **top** layers are squashed: they are in **compression**;
- the **bottom** layers are stretched: they are in **tension**;
- a layer in the middle, the **neutral axis** (neutral zone), is neither stretched nor squashed.

The stresses are greatest at the top and bottom surfaces and fall to zero at the neutral axis. Near the supports the beam also experiences **shear**.

:::example{title="Why reinforced concrete has steel near the bottom"}
Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension. In a concrete beam or lintel, steel rods are placed near the **bottom**, where the beam is in tension, so that the steel carries the pull and the concrete carries the push.
:::

## Comparing the strength of beams

### Depth matters most

A plank placed on its edge bends far less under a load than the same plank lying flat. The **depth** of the beam (its height in the direction of the load) matters much more than its width, because a deeper beam puts its top and bottom surfaces further from the neutral axis.

### Putting material where it works hardest

Material near the neutral axis carries little stress, so it adds weight without adding much strength. The **I-section** puts most of its material in the top and bottom flanges, where the stresses are largest, joined by a thin web. Hollow **box** and **tube** sections do the same. These shapes have a high strength for their mass, which is why steel roof beams are usually I-sections.

:::example{title="A classroom test of beam shapes"}
Learners make beams of the same mass per unit length (for example from the same amount of card) folded into different shapes: flat, L, T, box and I. Each is supported at its ends and loaded at its centre until it fails, or its sag is measured for the same load. The flat strip sags most; the box and I-shapes are stiffest.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="The whole beam is in compression"}
A loaded beam is in compression on one side and tension on the other. For a beam supported at both ends, the top is compressed and the bottom stretched.
:::

:::mistake{title="Wider is stronger"}
For a beam carrying a downward load, extra **depth** gives far more strength than extra width.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to compare beams, mention both the shape of the cross-section and how the material is distributed relative to the neutral axis.
:::

:::exam-tip
Use the four key words correctly: tension, compression, shear and buckling. Examiners often ask you to identify which one acts in a given part of a structure.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A beam carries loads across a span; it is described by its cross-section (rectangular, I, T, L, Z, box, tube).
- In a beam loaded in the middle and supported at its ends, the top is in compression, the bottom in tension and the neutral axis is unstressed.
- Depth matters more than width for strength and stiffness.
- I, box and tube sections put material where the stress is largest, giving high strength for their mass.
- Reinforced concrete beams have steel near the tension side.
:::
