## Introduction

A structure is only as strong as its joints. A carpenter making a desk, a welder building a scotch cart, and a technician soldering a radio circuit all choose a joining method to suit the materials and the forces the joint must carry. This lesson covers ZIMSEC topic 5.3.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Joint"}
A place where two or more parts of a structure are fastened together.
:::

:::definition{term="Pinning"}
Joining parts with a fastener that passes through them: nails, screws, bolts with nuts, rivets or dowels.
:::

:::definition{term="Surface-contact joint"}
A joint held together over an area of contact: glued joints, soldered, brazed and welded joints.
:::

## Joining by pins

| Fastener | Typical use | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| nail | wood to wood | quick; can pull out under tension |
| screw | wood, plastic | thread grips better than a nail; can be removed |
| bolt and nut | metal or wood frames | strong; can be taken apart and adjusted |
| rivet | metal sheets (bridges, trailers, aircraft) | permanent; the end is hammered flat |
| dowel | wood furniture | wooden pin, usually glued in |

The strength of a pinned joint depends on:

- the **number** of pins: more pins share the load;
- their **position**: pins spread out (not too close to the edge or to each other) resist turning and splitting better than pins bunched together;
- the **size** and material of each pin.

## Joining by surface contact

### Gluing

Adhesives join wood, plastics, paper and some metals. A glued joint is stronger when the **area of contact** is larger, which is why carpenters use shaped joints such as **tongue-and-groove** and dowelled joints, which add extra glued surfaces.

### Soldering, brazing and welding (metals)

- **Soldering**: a low-melting-point alloy (solder) is melted into the joint; the parts themselves do not melt. Used for electrical connections and thin metal; the weakest of the three.
- **Brazing**: a brass filler with a higher melting point is used; stronger than soldering. The parts still do not melt.
- **Welding**: the edges of the parts themselves are melted (by a gas flame or an electric arc), usually with a filler rod, so they fuse into one piece. The strongest metal joint, used for steel frames, burglar bars and vehicle bodies.

### Plastics

Thermoplastics can be **welded** with heat (the surfaces melt and fuse) or **glued** with a solvent cement that softens the surfaces so they bond.

## Comparing joints

:::example{title="Choosing joints for a scotch cart"}
A blacksmith building a scotch cart **welds** the steel axle brackets to the chassis, because those joints carry the whole load and must never come apart. He **bolts** the wooden floorboards to the frame, so damaged boards can be replaced, and uses several bolts spread along each board so that no single bolt carries all the force.
:::

Joints are compared by testing them: identical test pieces joined by different methods are loaded until each joint fails, and the breaking loads are compared. Typical results: a welded steel joint is stronger than a brazed one, which is stronger than a soldered one; a glued joint with a large contact area is stronger than one with a small area; three spaced-out nails hold better than one.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Soldering and welding are the same"}
In soldering and brazing only the filler melts. In welding the parts themselves melt and fuse, so welded joints are much stronger.
:::

:::mistake{title="More glue makes a stronger joint"}
What matters is the **area of contact** of well-fitting surfaces, not a thick layer of glue.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to choose a joining method, give the method **and** a reason linked to the material and the job: "rivets, because the steel sheets must be joined permanently and the joint carries large forces".
:::

:::exam-tip
"Compare the strength of joints" questions want the factors: contact area for glued joints; the number, size and position of pins for pinned joints.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Materials are joined by pins (nails, screws, bolts, rivets, dowels) or by surface contact (glue, solder, brazing, welding).
- Pinned joints are stronger with more pins, spread out sensibly, of suitable size.
- Glued joints are stronger with a larger contact area (tongue-and-groove, dowels).
- Welding melts the parts themselves and is stronger than brazing, which is stronger than soldering.
- Plastics can be welded with heat or glued with solvent cement.
:::
