## Introduction

Zimbabwe has some famous large structures. The Victoria Falls Bridge (opened in 1905) is a steel **arch** bridge across the Zambezi gorge. The Birchenough Bridge over the Save River is another steel arch bridge. Kariba Dam holds back one of the largest artificial lakes in the world with a curved concrete **arch dam**. This lesson (ZIMSEC topic 5.4) explains how such structures carry enormous loads and why their materials and shapes are chosen.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Compressive strength"}
The largest squashing force per unit area a material can withstand before it fails.
:::

:::definition{term="Tensile strength"}
The largest pulling force per unit area a material can withstand before it breaks.
:::

:::definition{term="Durability"}
How well a material lasts without decaying, corroding or rusting.
:::

## Materials for large structures

| Material | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| stone and brick | very strong in compression; durable | weak in tension; heavy |
| concrete | strong in compression; durable; can be cast into shapes | weak in tension; heavy |
| reinforced concrete | concrete for compression plus steel bars for tension | heavier and costlier than plain concrete |
| steel | strong in both tension and compression; can be made thin | rusts unless painted or galvanised; expensive |
| wood | light; fairly strong along the grain; easy to work | rots, burns and is eaten by termites; less strong than steel |

Engineers choose a material by its compressive and tensile strength, its mass and its durability, and also by its cost, although cost calculations are not required.

## Bridges

### Beam (and pier) bridges

A deck rests on supports at each end, like a plank across a stream. Under a load the deck bends: its top is in compression and its bottom in tension. A beam bridge only spans a short gap, so long beam bridges rest on many **piers**, making a pier bridge.

### Arch bridges

An arch turns the downward load into **compression** along the curve of the arch, which is passed to strong supports (abutments) at each end. Because the arch is in compression, it suits materials that are weak in tension, such as stone and concrete. The ends of an arch push **outwards**, so the abutments, or the rock walls of a gorge as at Victoria Falls, must be strong enough to resist that push.

:::example{title="Why the Victoria Falls Bridge is an arch"}
The bridge spans a deep gorge with walls of strong rock. A steel arch could push its outward thrust into those rock walls, so no tall piers had to be built up from the bottom of the gorge, where the Zambezi rushes past. The arch carries the weight of the deck and trains as compression down to its feet on each side.
:::

### Suspension bridges

The deck hangs from vertical cables attached to two main **cables** draped over tall **towers** and anchored firmly in the ground at each end. The cables are in **tension** and the towers are in **compression**. Suspension bridges have the longest spans of all.

## Arches in large structures

Arches are used above doors, windows and tunnels as well as in bridges, because they carry heavy loads using only compression, so brick, stone or concrete can be used without needing to resist tension.

## Dams

The water pressure on a dam wall **increases with depth**, so every dam wall is thicker at the bottom than at the top.

- **Earth (embankment) dams** are built of compacted earth and clay with a very wide base and a triangular cross-section. Their great weight and width resist the water. They suit wide valleys and are common for farm dams.
- **Concrete gravity dams** are straight walls that rely on their own great weight to stay in place.
- **Arch dams** are thin, curved concrete walls bowing **upstream**. The water pushes on the curved face and the arch carries this force as compression into the rock walls of the valley, as at Kariba. They need a narrow gorge with very strong rock sides, but use far less concrete than a gravity dam.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Arch dams curve the other way"}
An arch dam bows **towards** the water (upstream), so the water pressure squeezes the arch into the valley sides.
:::

:::mistake{title="Steel is always best"}
Steel is strong but rusts and is expensive. Stone and concrete are excellent wherever the structure is in compression.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For bridge questions, state which parts are in tension and which in compression: suspension cables in tension, towers in compression; an arch in compression.
:::

:::exam-tip
To explain the shape of a dam wall, link depth and pressure: "pressure increases with depth, so the base must withstand the greatest force and is made thicker".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Choose construction materials by compressive and tensile strength, mass and durability.
- Stone and concrete are strong in compression but weak in tension; steel is strong in both; reinforced concrete combines them.
- Beam bridges bend (top compressed, bottom stretched); arches carry loads in compression; suspension bridges use cables in tension and towers in compression.
- Dam walls are thicker at the bottom because water pressure increases with depth.
- Earth dams are wide and heavy; arch dams curve upstream and push into strong valley walls.
:::
