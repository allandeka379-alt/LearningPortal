## Introduction

A torch, a cellphone, a car and a solar home system all need a **source of electromotive force (e.m.f.)** to push charge round their circuits. In many Zimbabwean homes and rural clinics, solar panels charge batteries during the day so that lights and phones can be used at night and during load-shedding. This lesson compares the main sources.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cell"}
A device that converts stored chemical energy (or light energy, in a solar cell) into electrical energy, giving an e.m.f. between its terminals.
:::

:::definition{term="Battery"}
Two or more cells connected together.
:::

:::definition{term="Primary cell"}
A cell that cannot be recharged: once its chemicals are used up, it is thrown away.
:::

:::definition{term="Secondary cell (accumulator)"}
A rechargeable cell: passing a current through it in the reverse direction restores its chemicals.
:::

## Primary cells

The common **dry cell** (zinc–carbon or the longer-lasting alkaline type) used in torches, radios and remote controls has an e.m.f. of about $1.5\ \text{V}$. Its chemicals are in a paste, so it does not spill. It is cheap and light, but once it is flat it must be replaced. Primary cells must **never** be recharged: they can overheat, leak or burst.

## Secondary cells

- The **lead–acid accumulator** used in cars, trucks and solar systems has plates of lead and lead(IV) oxide in dilute sulfuric acid. Each cell gives about $2\ \text{V}$; a $12\ \text{V}$ car battery contains six cells in series. It can supply very large currents (for a car's starter motor), but it is heavy and the acid is corrosive.
- **Lithium-ion** cells in phones and laptops, and **nickel–metal hydride** cells, are light rechargeable cells.

To recharge a secondary cell, connect it to a d.c. supply of slightly higher voltage, **positive terminal to positive terminal**, so that current is driven through the cell backwards.

## Photovoltaic (solar) cells

A **solar cell** converts light energy directly into electrical energy. A single cell gives only about half a volt, so many are joined in a **solar panel**. Solar panels have no moving parts and use a free, renewable source, but they give no output at night and less on cloudy days, so they are usually used with rechargeable batteries.

Other sources of e.m.f. include **generators** (in power stations and in petrol or diesel gensets) and **thermocouples**.

## Cells in series

When cells are joined in series, positive terminal to negative terminal, their e.m.f.s **add up**.

:::worked-solution[E.m.f. of cells in series]
1. A torch holds three $1.5\ \text{V}$ cells in series.
2. Total e.m.f. $= 3 \times 1.5 = 4.5\ \text{V}$.
3. If one cell is put in the wrong way round, it opposes the others: $1.5 + 1.5 - 1.5 = 1.5\ \text{V}$, and the torch is dim.
:::

:::worked-solution[How many cells in a battery?]
1. A lead–acid battery has an e.m.f. of $12\ \text{V}$ and each cell gives $2.0\ \text{V}$.
2. Number of cells $= \dfrac{12}{2.0} = 6$, connected in series.
:::

## Caring for batteries and disposing of them

- Do not short-circuit a cell (connect its terminals with a wire): a large current heats it and can damage it or cause a fire.
- Do not recharge primary cells. Charge secondary cells with the correct charger.
- Keep lead–acid batteries topped up with distilled water if they are the refillable type, charge them in a ventilated place (they give off gas), and handle the acid with care.
- Batteries contain toxic metals such as lead, cadmium and mercury. Do not burn them or throw them where they can leak into soil and water; take used car batteries to a recycling point.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Batteries store charge"}
A cell does not store charge to be used up; it stores **chemical energy** and gives energy to the charges that flow through it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Recharging any battery"}
Only secondary cells can be recharged. Trying to recharge a primary dry cell is dangerous.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to compare primary and secondary cells, give a contrast for each point: rechargeable or not, cost, mass, typical uses.
:::

:::exam-tip
For cells in series, add the e.m.f.s, but subtract any cell that is connected the wrong way round.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sources of e.m.f. include primary cells, secondary cells, photovoltaic cells and generators.
- Primary cells (dry cells, about 1.5 V) cannot be recharged; secondary cells (lead–acid about 2 V per cell, lithium-ion) can.
- Solar cells convert light directly into electrical energy and are usually used with rechargeable batteries.
- The e.m.f.s of cells in series add.
- Batteries must be used, charged and disposed of safely because of heating, acid and toxic metals.
:::
