## Introduction

An electrician wiring a new house in Chitungwiza and a technician repairing a radio both work from **circuit diagrams**: drawings that use standard symbols to show how components are connected. A circuit diagram shows the connections clearly, even though the real wires may twist and turn.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Circuit diagram"}
A drawing of an electric circuit that uses standard symbols for the components and straight lines for the connecting wires.
:::

:::definition{term="Complete (closed) circuit"}
An unbroken path from one terminal of the source, through the components, back to the other terminal. Current flows only in a complete circuit.
:::

## Standard symbols

Each symbol is described below. Learn to draw every one neatly.

| Component | What the symbol looks like | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| cell | a long thin line (positive terminal) beside a short thick line (negative terminal) | provides an e.m.f. |
| battery | two or more cell symbols joined in a row | provides a larger e.m.f. |
| power supply | two small circles marked + and − (d.c.), or a ~ sign (a.c.) | provides an e.m.f. from the mains |
| switch (open) | a gap in the line with a short hinged line tilted away from it | breaks or completes the circuit |
| lamp | a circle with a cross inside | gives light |
| fixed resistor | a small rectangle | limits the current |
| variable resistor | a rectangle with a diagonal arrow through it | lets the resistance be changed |
| heater | a rectangle divided into several sections by short lines | transfers energy as thermal energy |
| NTC thermistor | a rectangle with a diagonal line through it that has a short horizontal end, labelled −t° | resistance falls as temperature rises |
| light-dependent resistor (LDR) | a rectangle inside a circle with two arrows pointing in | resistance falls as light gets brighter |
| ammeter | a circle with A inside | measures current (in series) |
| voltmeter | a circle with V inside | measures p.d. (in parallel) |
| motor | a circle with M inside | transfers energy to kinetic energy |
| generator | a circle with G inside | produces an e.m.f. |
| fuse | a small rectangle with a line running through its length | melts to break the circuit if the current is too big |
| relay | a coil (rectangle) beside a switch it operates | a small current switches a separate circuit |
| potential divider | a resistor with a sliding contact (arrow) touching its side | gives a variable p.d. |
| diode (Cambridge Supplement) | a triangle pointing at a short bar across the line | lets current flow in one direction only |
| light-emitting diode (LED) (Cambridge Supplement) | a diode symbol with two small arrows pointing away from it | gives light when current flows forwards |

## Rules for drawing circuit diagrams

- Use a ruler; draw wires as straight lines with square corners.
- Leave no gaps in the wires, except in an open switch.
- Show a junction (where wires join) with a solid dot.
- Connect ammeters **in series** and voltmeters **in parallel** across the component they measure.
- Draw the cell with the long line as the positive terminal; conventional current leaves the positive terminal.

:::example{title="Reading a circuit diagram"}
A diagram shows a battery, a closed switch, a lamp and an ammeter in a single loop, with a voltmeter connected across the lamp. The lamp, switch and ammeter are in **series**, so the same current passes through each and the ammeter measures it. The voltmeter is in **parallel** with the lamp, so it measures the p.d. across the lamp. Opening the switch breaks the only path, so the lamp goes out and the ammeter reads zero.
:::

:::example{title="A circuit for testing a thermistor"}
To see how a thermistor behaves, connect it in series with a battery and an ammeter, and place it in a beaker of water with a thermometer. As the water is heated, the ammeter reading rises, because the thermistor's resistance falls as its temperature rises.
:::

## Diodes and LEDs (Cambridge Supplement)

A **diode** conducts only when it is **forward biased**: the triangle in its symbol points in the direction of conventional current. Connected the other way (reverse biased), it has a very high resistance and almost no current flows. An **LED** behaves in the same way and gives out light when current flows forwards; it needs a resistor in series to limit the current.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Gaps and loose ends"}
A circuit with a gap anywhere in the loop is incomplete and no current flows. Check every line joins up.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing thermistor and LDR"}
A thermistor responds to **temperature**; an LDR responds to **light**. Learn which symbol has the arrows.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Examiners deduct marks for untidy symbols. Draw each symbol clearly, and do not draw a line straight through a lamp or a meter.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to add a meter to a circuit, decide first whether it measures current (in series) or p.d. (in parallel).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Circuit diagrams use standard symbols joined by straight lines.
- Current flows only in a complete circuit.
- Ammeters go in series; voltmeters go in parallel.
- Thermistors respond to temperature, LDRs to light; variable resistors and potential dividers allow adjustment.
- A diode (and an LED) lets current flow in one direction only (Cambridge Supplement).
:::
