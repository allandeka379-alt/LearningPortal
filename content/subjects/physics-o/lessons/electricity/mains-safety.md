## Introduction

The mains supply in Zimbabwean homes is about $220$–$240\ \text{V}$ a.c. at $50\ \text{Hz}$: enough to kill. Yet we plug in kettles, irons and phone chargers safely every day, because circuits are protected by fuses, trip switches, earth wires and good insulation. Knowing how these work, and what makes electricity dangerous, keeps you and your family safe.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Live wire"}
The wire that carries the alternating high voltage from the supply (brown insulation).
:::

:::definition{term="Neutral wire"}
The wire that completes the circuit back to the supply; it stays at or near zero volts (blue insulation).
:::

:::definition{term="Earth wire"}
A safety wire connected to the ground that normally carries no current (green-and-yellow insulation).
:::

:::definition{term="Fuse"}
A thin piece of wire in the live line that melts and breaks the circuit if the current becomes larger than its rating.
:::

## Hazards of mains electricity

- **Damaged insulation**: exposed live wires can give an electric shock or cause a short circuit and fire.
- **Overheating cables**: a cable carrying more current than it is designed for heats up and can melt its insulation and start a fire.
- **Damp conditions**: water conducts, so wet hands or appliances used near water (bathrooms, outdoors in the rain) make shocks more likely.
- **Overloading** plugs, extension leads and sockets: running several high-power appliances from one socket draws a large current, which overheats the wiring.

## The three wires and the switch

The live wire alternates between large positive and negative voltages; the neutral stays near $0\ \text{V}$. Switches and fuses are always placed in the **live** wire, so that when a switch is off or a fuse has blown, the appliance is **disconnected from the high voltage**. If the switch were in the neutral wire, the appliance would still be connected to the live wire even when switched off, and touching a fault inside it could be fatal.

## Fuses and trip switches

A fuse melts when the current exceeds its rating, breaking the circuit before the cable overheats. Choose the fuse rating **just above** the normal working current of the appliance; common plug fuses are $3\ \text{A}$, $5\ \text{A}$ and $13\ \text{A}$.

:::worked-solution[Choosing a fuse]
An electric kettle is rated $2.0\ \text{kW}$, $230\ \text{V}$. Fuses of $3\ \text{A}$, $5\ \text{A}$ and $13\ \text{A}$ are available.

1. Normal current: $I = \dfrac{P}{V} = \dfrac{2000}{230} = 8.7\ \text{A}$.
2. A $3\ \text{A}$ or $5\ \text{A}$ fuse would melt in normal use, so choose the $13\ \text{A}$ fuse, the smallest rating above $8.7\ \text{A}$.
:::

A **trip switch** (circuit breaker) does the same job: an electromagnet or a heat-sensitive strip opens a switch when the current is too large. Unlike a fuse, it can be **reset** with a button or lever once the fault is fixed, and it acts faster. Its trip setting is chosen in the same way, just above the normal current of the circuit. Many homes also have an **earth-leakage** trip (residual current device), which switches off if any current leaks to earth, for example through a person.

## Earthing and double insulation

If a fault makes the live wire touch the **metal casing** of an appliance, anyone touching the casing could get a shock. To prevent this:

- **Earthing**: the metal casing is connected to the earth wire. If the live wire touches the casing, a large current flows through the low-resistance earth path, the fuse melts (or the trip switch opens), and the appliance is disconnected.
- **Double insulation**: the appliance has an outer casing made of an insulator (plastic), and the live parts are insulated twice, so the casing cannot become live. Such appliances (hair dryers, many phone chargers) need no earth wire and carry a symbol of one square inside another. Their **fuse still protects the cable and circuit** from overheating.

## Wiring a three-pin plug (ZIMSEC)

With the back of the plug removed, the pins are arranged with the **earth** pin at the top:

- **Brown (live)** → the right-hand terminal, through the **fuse**.
- **Blue (neutral)** → the left-hand terminal.
- **Green-and-yellow (earth)** → the top terminal, connected to the large earth pin.

Strip only enough insulation to fit each terminal, tighten the screws so no loose strands remain, and clamp the **outer sheath** of the cable firmly in the **cable grip** so that a pull on the cable does not tug the wires out of their terminals.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Choosing a huge fuse to be safe"}
A fuse rated far above the normal current may not melt before the cable overheats. Choose the smallest rating above the normal current.
:::

:::mistake{title="The earth wire carries the current"}
In normal use the earth wire carries no current. It only carries current when there is a fault.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "why is the switch in the live wire?", say that when the switch is open the appliance is no longer connected to the high voltage, so it is safe to touch.
:::

:::exam-tip
To explain earthing, describe the chain: fault → large current to earth → fuse melts or breaker trips → circuit broken → casing safe.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Hazards: damaged insulation, overheating cables, damp conditions, overloaded sockets and leads.
- Live (brown) carries the high voltage, neutral (blue) completes the circuit, earth (green-and-yellow) is a safety wire.
- Switches and fuses go in the live wire; a fuse rating is chosen just above the normal current.
- Trip switches break the circuit when the current is too large and can be reset.
- Metal casings are earthed; double-insulated appliances need no earth wire, but their fuse still protects the cable.
:::
