## Introduction

In the dry winter months, pulling off a jersey can make your hair stand on end with a crackle of tiny sparks. A plastic comb run through dry hair picks up small pieces of paper. In the summer rainy season, lightning flashes between storm clouds and the ground. All of these are effects of **electric charge** that has built up and stays in one place: **static electricity**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Electric charge"}
A property of matter that causes electric forces. There are two kinds: **positive** and **negative**.
:::

:::definition{term="Conductor"}
A material that allows charge to flow through it easily, such as copper, aluminium or graphite.
:::

:::definition{term="Insulator"}
A material that does not allow charge to flow through it easily, such as plastic, rubber, glass or dry wood.
:::

:::definition{term="Electric field (Cambridge Supplement)"}
A region in which an electric charge experiences a force.
:::

## Atoms and charge

Every atom has a nucleus containing positive **protons**, surrounded by negative **electrons**. A neutral object has equal numbers of protons and electrons. Protons are locked inside nuclei, but some electrons can be moved from one object to another.

- An object that **gains** electrons becomes **negatively** charged.
- An object that **loses** electrons becomes **positively** charged.

Charge is measured in **coulombs** (C) (Cambridge Supplement). One electron carries a tiny charge, so even a small static charge involves billions of electrons moving.

## Forces between charges

:::keyfact
**Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.** A charged object also attracts small neutral objects, such as bits of paper, by pulling opposite charge to their near side.
:::

## Charging by friction

When two different insulators are rubbed together, **electrons** are transferred from one to the other. Only negative charge moves; the positive charges stay in the nuclei.

:::example{title="Rubbing a polythene rod"}
A polythene rod is rubbed with a woollen duster. Electrons move from the wool to the polythene. The rod becomes **negatively** charged and the duster is left with an equal **positive** charge. An acetate or perspex rod rubbed with a duster does the opposite: it loses electrons and becomes positive.
:::

### Detecting charge

- Hang a charged rod in a paper stirrup from a thread. Bring a second charged rod near: if they repel, the charges are the same; if they attract, they are opposite. (**Repulsion** is the only sure test for charge, because a charged rod also attracts an uncharged object.)
- A **gold-leaf electroscope** has a metal cap connected to a metal rod with a thin gold leaf hinged on it. When charge reaches the cap, it spreads to both the rod and the leaf; they repel, so the leaf rises. The more charge, the higher the leaf.

## Conductors and insulators

In metals, some electrons are **free electrons**: they are not bound to any one atom and can drift through the metal. That is why metals conduct. In insulators, all the electrons are held tightly by their atoms, so charge cannot flow, and charge placed on an insulator stays where it is put.

To test a material, connect it in a circuit with a cell and a lamp (or an ammeter): the lamp lights only for a conductor. Alternatively, touch the cap of a charged electroscope with the material: a conductor lets the charge flow away to earth through your hand, so the leaf falls; an insulator does not.

## Electric fields (Cambridge Supplement)

The direction of an electric field at a point is the direction of the force on a small **positive** charge placed there. Field lines show the patterns:

- **Point charge or charged sphere**: radial lines. They point **outwards** from a positive charge and **inwards** towards a negative charge.
- **Two oppositely charged parallel plates**: evenly spaced, parallel lines from the positive plate to the negative plate (a uniform field), curving slightly at the edges.

## Charging by induction (ZIMSEC)

A conductor can be charged without touching it:

1. Hold a negatively charged rod near a metal sphere on an insulating stand. Free electrons in the sphere are repelled to the far side, leaving the near side positive.
2. Touch the far side with a finger (connecting it to earth): the repelled electrons flow away to earth.
3. Remove the finger **first**, then remove the rod.
4. The sphere is left with a **positive** charge, opposite to the charge on the rod.

## Lightning and lightning conductors (ZIMSEC)

In a thundercloud, collisions between ice particles separate charge; the base of the cloud usually becomes negative. This induces positive charge on the ground and on tall buildings below. When the charge is large enough, the air conducts and a huge spark, **lightning**, carries charge between cloud and ground, heating the air so fast that it expands with a bang: thunder.

A **lightning conductor** is a pointed metal rod fixed above the top of a building and joined by a thick copper strip to a metal plate buried in the ground. It gives the charge an easy, low-resistance path to earth, so a strike passes safely to the ground instead of through the building.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Positive charge moves when rubbing"}
Only electrons (negative charge) move during charging by friction. A rod becomes positive by **losing** electrons, not by gaining positive charge.
:::

:::mistake{title="Attraction proves an object is charged"}
A charged rod attracts uncharged objects too. Only repulsion shows for certain that an object is charged.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In "explain" questions about charging, always say which way the **electrons** move and which object gains or loses them.
:::

:::exam-tip
When drawing field lines, add arrows: away from positive, towards negative.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- There are positive and negative charges; like charges repel and unlike charges attract.
- Charging by friction transfers electrons: the object gaining electrons becomes negative.
- Metals conduct because they contain free electrons; in insulators electrons are held in place.
- Charge is measured in coulombs; an electric field is a region where a charge feels a force, directed as the force on a positive charge (Cambridge Supplement).
- Conductors can be charged by induction; lightning conductors give charge a safe path to earth (ZIMSEC).
:::
