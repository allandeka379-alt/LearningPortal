## Introduction

Almost all the electricity we use, from the turbines at Kariba to a bicycle dynamo, is produced by **electromagnetic induction**: moving a magnet and a coil relative to each other generates an e.m.f. Michael Faraday discovered the effect in 1831, and it underlies generators, transformers and microphones.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Electromagnetic induction"}
The production of an e.m.f. in a conductor when it moves across a magnetic field, or when the magnetic field linking it changes.
:::

:::definition{term="Lenz's law"}
The induced e.m.f. (and current) is always in a direction that opposes the change producing it.
:::

## Demonstrating induction

Connect a coil of wire to a sensitive **centre-zero meter** (galvanometer).

- Push a bar magnet **into** the coil: the needle flicks one way.
- Hold the magnet **still** inside the coil: the needle reads **zero**.
- Pull the magnet **out**: the needle flicks the **other** way.
- Move the magnet **faster**: the deflection is **larger**.

An e.m.f. is induced only while there is **relative movement** (or a changing field). The same happens if the coil is moved and the magnet held still, or if a straight wire is moved quickly up or down between the poles of a U-shaped magnet. Moving the wire **along** the field lines induces nothing: the wire must **cut** the field lines.

## Factors affecting the size of the induced e.m.f.

The induced e.m.f. is larger when:

- the relative movement is **faster** (the field changes more quickly);
- the magnet is **stronger**;
- the coil has **more turns** (and a larger area).

## The direction of the induced current

### Lenz's law (Cambridge Supplement; ZIMSEC)

The induced current always **opposes the change** that produces it.

:::worked-solution[A magnet approaching a coil]
The north pole of a magnet is pushed towards one end of a coil connected in a closed circuit.

1. The change is a north pole approaching.
2. To oppose it, the induced current makes the near end of the coil a **north** pole, which **repels** the approaching magnet.
3. When the magnet is pulled away, the near end becomes a **south** pole, which **attracts** it and opposes its removal.
4. Using the solenoid rule: a north pole at that end means the current flows **anticlockwise** as seen from the magnet.
:::

Lenz's law follows from the conservation of energy. Because the coil always opposes the motion, you must do work to move the magnet; that work becomes the electrical energy of the induced current. If the induced current helped the motion, energy would be created from nothing.

### Fleming's right-hand rule (Cambridge Supplement; ZIMSEC)

For a straight wire moving through a field, hold the thumb and first two fingers of your **right** hand at right angles:

- th**u**mb: **motion** of the wire;
- **F**irst finger: **F**ield (N to S);
- se**C**ond finger: induced **C**urrent.

:::worked-solution[A wire moving down through a field]
A horizontal wire is moved downwards between a north pole on the left and a south pole on the right.

1. Thumb down (motion), first finger to the right (field from N to S).
2. The second finger points **out of the page**: the induced current flows towards you.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="A strong magnet at rest induces a current"}
A stationary magnet, however strong, induces nothing. There must be relative motion or a changing field.
:::

:::mistake{title="Left or right?"}
Motors (force on a current): left hand. Generators (induced current): right hand.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An e.m.f. is induced when a conductor cuts magnetic field lines or the field linking a coil changes.
- No relative movement means no induced e.m.f.
- The e.m.f. is larger for faster movement, a stronger magnet and more turns.
- Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change producing it (a consequence of energy conservation).
- Fleming's right-hand rule: thuMb motion, First finger Field, seCond finger induced Current.
:::
