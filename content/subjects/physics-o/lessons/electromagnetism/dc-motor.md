## Introduction

A cordless drill, a car's windscreen wipers, a toy car and a solar-powered borehole pump all contain **d.c. motors**. Each one is a coil of wire that keeps turning in a magnetic field, using the motor effect and a clever switch called a **commutator**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Split-ring commutator"}
A metal ring cut into two halves, fixed to the coil's axle, that reverses the direction of the current in the coil every half turn.
:::

:::definition{term="Brushes"}
Springy carbon (or metal) contacts that press against the commutator and carry the current into and out of the rotating coil.
:::

## The turning effect on a coil

A rectangular coil carrying a current sits between the poles of a magnet, with its plane along the field.

![End view of a d.c. motor coil with its plane along the magnetic field between a north pole on the left and a south pole on the right. Current goes into the page on the left side and out on the right side, so Fleming's left-hand rule gives a downward force on the left and an upward force on the right, turning the coil anticlockwise.](media:diagrams/dc-motor-coil-forces.png "Figure: the forces on opposite sides of the coil give a turning effect")

- The current flows **one way** along one side of the coil and **the opposite way** along the other side.
- By Fleming's left-hand rule, one side is pushed **up** and the other side is pushed **down**.
- These two forces make a **turning effect** (a moment) about the axle, so the coil rotates.

The turning effect is increased by:

- **more turns** of wire on the coil;
- a **larger current**;
- a **stronger magnetic field**.

(A larger coil also helps, because the forces act further from the axle.)

## Keeping the coil turning

Without a commutator, the coil would turn until its plane was at right angles to the field (vertical). There the forces on its sides point straight up and down, through the axle, and have no turning effect; the coil would rock back and settle there.

The **split-ring commutator** solves this:

1. The current enters and leaves the coil through the brushes, which press on the two halves of the commutator.
2. As the coil passes the vertical position, the gaps in the commutator pass the brushes and each half of the ring swaps to the other brush.
3. This **reverses the current in the coil** every half turn.
4. The force on each side of the coil reverses too, just as that side moves from one half of the field to the other, so the coil keeps turning in the **same direction**. The coil's momentum carries it past the vertical position, where there is briefly no turning force.

:::example{title="Reversing a motor"}
A d.c. motor turns clockwise. Swapping the connections to the battery reverses the current, so it turns anticlockwise. Turning the magnets round (reversing the field) also reverses the rotation. Doing both at once leaves it turning clockwise.
:::

## Practical motors

Real motors have many coils wound on a **soft-iron armature**, curved pole pieces and often electromagnets instead of permanent magnets. These give a smoother, larger turning effect and a steady speed.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="The commutator reverses the battery"}
The commutator reverses the current **in the coil**, not the current from the battery. The battery's current stays in one direction (d.c.).
:::

:::mistake{title="Maximum force at the vertical"}
The turning effect is **largest** when the coil's plane is **parallel** to the field and **zero** when it is perpendicular (vertical).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In "explain how the motor works" answers, mention: forces on opposite sides in opposite directions (left-hand rule) → turning effect → commutator reverses the current every half turn → rotation continues the same way.
:::

:::exam-tip
"State two ways to make the motor turn faster": more current, more turns, stronger magnet.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A current-carrying coil in a magnetic field feels equal and opposite forces on its sides, giving a turning effect.
- The turning effect increases with more turns, more current and a stronger field.
- A split-ring commutator reverses the current in the coil every half turn, so the coil keeps turning the same way.
- Brushes carry current to the rotating commutator.
- Reversing either the current or the field reverses the direction of rotation.
:::
