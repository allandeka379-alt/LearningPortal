## Introduction

In 1820, Hans Christian Ørsted noticed a compass needle twitch whenever he switched on a current in a nearby wire. Every electric current produces a **magnetic field**. This discovery links electricity and magnetism, and it is the basis of electromagnets, relays, electric bells, loudspeakers and motors.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Solenoid"}
A long coil of wire. When a current flows in it, it produces a magnetic field like that of a bar magnet.
:::

:::definition{term="Right-hand grip rule"}
A rule for finding the direction of the magnetic field produced by a current (described below).
:::

## The field around a straight wire

Pass a vertical wire through a horizontal card and switch on a large current. Iron filings sprinkled on the card form **concentric circles** around the wire; small plotting compasses placed on the card point along the circles and show their direction.

- The field lines are circles centred on the wire.
- **Right-hand grip rule**: grip the wire with your right hand with the **thumb pointing along the conventional current**; your **fingers curl in the direction of the field**.
- Reversing the current reverses the direction of the field (the compasses turn round).
- (Cambridge Supplement) The field is **strongest close to the wire** and gets weaker further away, so the circles are drawn further apart as the distance increases. A **larger current** gives a **stronger** field.

:::example{title="Which way round?"}
A vertical wire carries a current upwards. Looking down on the card from above, the current comes towards you, so by the right-hand grip rule the field lines run **anticlockwise**. If the current is reversed (flowing downwards), the field lines run clockwise.
:::

## The field of a solenoid

Thread a coil of wire through a card and sprinkle iron filings around it.

- Outside the solenoid, the field has the same shape as that of a **bar magnet**: one end acts as a north pole and the other as a south pole.
- Inside the solenoid, the field lines are **straight, parallel and close together**: the field is strong and almost uniform.
- **Finding the poles**: grip the solenoid with your right hand so your **fingers curl the way the current flows** around the turns; your **thumb points to the north pole**. Equivalently, looking at one end, if the current flows **anticlockwise** that end is a **north** pole; if **clockwise**, a **south** pole.
- Reversing the current swaps the poles.

The field of a solenoid is made stronger by:

- a **larger current**;
- **more turns** of wire (closer together);
- a **soft-iron core** inside the coil, which becomes strongly magnetised: this makes an **electromagnet**.

## Uses of the magnetic effect

### Relays

A relay uses a small current in a coil to operate a switch in a separate circuit. Current in the coil magnetises a soft-iron core, which attracts an iron armature; the armature pushes a pair of contacts together, completing the second circuit. When the coil current stops, a spring pulls the armature back and the contacts open. Relays let a small, safe current (for example from a sensor or a car key switch) control a large current or the mains.

### Electric bells

An electromagnet attracts a soft-iron armature carrying a hammer, which strikes the gong. Moving the armature breaks the circuit at a contact screw, so the electromagnet switches off and a spring pulls the armature back, remaking the contact. This repeats rapidly while the bell push is pressed.

### Loudspeakers

A loudspeaker has a light coil attached to a paper or plastic cone, sitting in the strong field of a permanent magnet. An alternating current from an amplifier flows in the coil. The magnetic field of the current interacts with the magnet's field, so the coil is pushed back and forth, in step with the current. The cone vibrates and produces sound waves with the same frequency as the current.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Left hand for the grip rule"}
The grip rule uses the **right** hand. (The left hand is used for Fleming's left-hand rule for forces, which comes later.)
:::

:::mistake{title="Field lines from end to end of a wire"}
Around a straight wire the field lines are circles round the wire, not lines along it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When drawing the field of a wire seen end-on, show circles that get further apart moving outwards, with arrows given by the grip rule. A dot means current out of the page; a cross means current into the page.
:::

:::exam-tip
To state how to make an electromagnet stronger, give three distinct changes: more current, more turns, a soft-iron core.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A current produces a magnetic field: circles around a straight wire; a bar-magnet pattern around a solenoid.
- The right-hand grip rule gives the field direction (thumb along the current, fingers show the field).
- The field is strongest near the wire and inside the solenoid; it grows with the current, and reversing the current reverses it (Cambridge Supplement).
- A solenoid's field is increased by more current, more turns and a soft-iron core.
- Relays, electric bells and loudspeakers all use the magnetic effect of a current.
:::
