## Introduction

Without friction you could not walk, a bus could not brake and a nail would slide out of the wood. With too much friction, engines wear out and bicycles are hard to pedal. Friction is a force that we sometimes want and sometimes fight.

You should already know Newton's first and second laws.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Friction"}
A force between two surfaces in contact that opposes (impedes) their relative motion, and produces heating.
:::

:::definition{term="Drag"}
The friction force on an object moving through a fluid (a liquid or a gas). Drag in air is called **air resistance**.
:::

## Solid friction

When two surfaces rub, tiny bumps on each surface catch on each other. Friction:

- acts **along** the surfaces, **opposite** to the direction of motion (or attempted motion);
- **heats** the surfaces: rubbing your hands together warms them, and brake pads get hot;
- causes **wear**: tyres, shoe soles and brake pads slowly wear away.

:::worked-solution[Pushing a crate at constant speed]
A worker pushes a crate across a warehouse floor at a constant speed with a horizontal force of $150\ \text{N}$.

1. The speed is constant, so the resultant force is zero (Newton's first law).
2. So the friction force on the crate is $150\ \text{N}$, opposite to the push.
:::

:::worked-solution[When the push is larger than friction]
The worker now pushes the $25\ \text{kg}$ crate with $200\ \text{N}$, and friction stays at $150\ \text{N}$.

1. Resultant force $= 200 - 150 = 50\ \text{N}$ forwards.
2. $a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{50}{25} = 2.0\ \text{m/s}^2$.
:::

## Drag in liquids and gases

An object moving through water or air pushes the fluid out of its way, and the fluid pushes back. Drag:

- **increases with speed**: double the speed of a car and the air resistance roughly quadruples;
- depends on **shape and size**: a streamlined (smooth, pointed) shape has less drag than a flat, wide one;
- is much larger in liquids than in gases.

This is why falling objects reach a terminal velocity, and why fast vehicles, birds and fish are streamlined.

## Friction: useful or a nuisance?

| Useful friction | Unwanted friction |
| --- | --- |
| grip between shoes and the ground when walking | wear of moving engine parts |
| tyres gripping the road; brakes stopping a vehicle | heating that wastes energy |
| holding nails and screws in wood | extra fuel needed to overcome air resistance |
| striking a match | noise from rubbing parts |

### Reducing friction (ZIMSEC 3.3)

- **Lubrication** with oil or grease between moving parts.
- **Ball bearings** and roller bearings, so surfaces roll instead of slide.
- Making surfaces **smooth** and polished.
- **Streamlining** to reduce drag.
- Separating surfaces with a cushion of air, as in a hovercraft or an air track.

### Increasing friction

Tread on tyres and shoes, rough surfaces such as sand on slippery roads, and brake pads made of high-friction materials.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Friction stops all motion"}
Friction opposes motion; it does not always stop it. If the driving force equals the friction, the object moves at constant speed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Drag is constant"}
Air resistance grows with speed. That is why a cyclist needs much more force to ride fast than slowly.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Describe friction" earns marks for: a force between surfaces, opposing motion, and causing heating (and wear).
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked how to reduce friction in a machine, name the method **and** say how it works, e.g. "oil fills the gaps between the surfaces so they slide past each other easily".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Friction acts between surfaces, opposes motion, and causes heating and wear.
- Drag is friction in a fluid; in air it is air resistance.
- Drag increases with speed and depends on shape: streamlining reduces it.
- Friction is useful for walking, gripping and braking, but wastes energy in machines.
- Lubrication, bearings, smooth surfaces and streamlining reduce friction.
:::
