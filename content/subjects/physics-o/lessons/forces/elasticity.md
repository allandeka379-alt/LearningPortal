## Introduction

Forces do not only make things move; they also change their **shape and size**. The steel springs of a scotch cart stretch and squash as it bumps along a dirt road; the rubber of a catapult stretches when a herd boy pulls it back. Springs are useful because, up to a point, they stretch by an amount that is exactly proportional to the force: this is **Hooke's law**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Load"}
The force (often a weight) that stretches a spring or wire.
:::

:::definition{term="Extension"}
The increase in length of a spring or wire: extension = stretched length − original length.
:::

:::definition{term="Spring constant, k"}
The force per unit extension of a spring (Cambridge Supplement): $k = \dfrac{F}{x}$, in N/m or N/cm.
:::

:::definition{term="Limit of proportionality"}
The point on a load-extension graph beyond which the extension is no longer proportional to the load (the graph stops being a straight line).
:::

:::formula{name="Hooke's law"}
$$
F = kx
$$

The extension $x$ is proportional to the load $F$, up to the limit of proportionality.
:::

## Tension and compression

A force that **stretches** an object puts it in **tension**; a force that **squashes** it puts it in **compression**. A rope holding up a bucket is in tension; the legs of a stool are in compression.

## The load-extension experiment

1. Hang a spring from a clamp next to a vertical metre rule. Record the position of the bottom of the spring with no load: this is the original length.
2. Hang a $100\ \text{g}$ mass hanger (load $0.98\ \text{N}$, about $1\ \text{N}$) and record the new position.
3. Add masses one at a time, recording the position each time. Read the rule at eye level to avoid parallax.
4. Calculate each extension (position − original position) and plot a graph of extension against load.
5. Remove the masses one at a time to check the spring returns to its original length.

For a spring obeying Hooke's law, the graph is a **straight line through the origin**. At larger loads the line curves: that point is the **limit of proportionality**. Stretched too far, the spring will not return to its original length.

![Load-extension graph for a spring: a straight line through the origin up to 6 N (a spring with 4.0 N giving 4.0 cm, so k = 100 N per m), then the line curves upwards beyond the limit of proportionality, where Hooke's law no longer applies.](media:diagrams/hookes-law-load-extension.png "Figure: extension is proportional to load up to the limit of proportionality")

:::worked-solution[Finding the spring constant]
A spring is $8.0\ \text{cm}$ long. With a load of $4.0\ \text{N}$ it is $12.0\ \text{cm}$ long.

1. Extension $= 12.0 - 8.0 = 4.0\ \text{cm} = 0.040\ \text{m}$.
2. $k = \dfrac{F}{x} = \dfrac{4.0\ \text{N}}{0.040\ \text{m}} = 100\ \text{N/m}$ (or $1.0\ \text{N/cm}$).
:::

:::worked-solution[Predicting an extension]
A spring has $k = 40\ \text{N/m}$. What is its extension with a load of $2.4\ \text{N}$?

1. $x = \dfrac{F}{k} = \dfrac{2.4}{40} = 0.060\ \text{m} = 6.0\ \text{cm}$.
2. This is valid only if $2.4\ \text{N}$ is below the limit of proportionality.
:::

A stiffer spring has a **larger** spring constant: it needs more force for each centimetre of stretch, so its load-extension graph is less steep when extension is on the vertical axis.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using the length instead of the extension"}
Hooke's law uses the **extension** (the increase in length), not the total length. Subtract the original length first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing cm and m"}
If $x$ is in cm, $k$ comes out in N/cm. Convert to metres for N/m: $1\ \text{N/cm} = 100\ \text{N/m}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Applying Hooke's law beyond the limit"}
Once the graph curves, $F = kx$ no longer holds, so you cannot use it to predict extensions there.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In the experiment description, name the measuring instrument (metre rule), say how the extension is calculated, mention reading at eye level, and describe the graph you would plot.
:::

:::exam-tip
To identify the limit of proportionality on a graph, find where the straight line first starts to curve.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Forces can change the size and shape of an object: stretching (tension) or squashing (compression).
- Extension = stretched length − original length.
- Hooke's law: $F = kx$, where $k$ is the spring constant (N/m).
- A load-extension graph is a straight line through the origin up to the limit of proportionality.
- A stiffer spring has a larger spring constant.
:::
