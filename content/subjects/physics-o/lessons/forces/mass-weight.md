## Introduction

At a market in Mbare a vendor says a bag of potatoes "weighs 10 kg". In everyday speech mass and weight mean the same thing, but in physics they are different quantities with different units. An astronaut's mass is the same on the Moon as on the Earth, yet she weighs far less there.

You should already know that a force is a push or a pull measured in newtons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mass"}
A measure of the quantity of matter in an object (at rest relative to the observer). Measured in kilograms (kg). It does not depend on where the object is.
:::

:::definition{term="Weight"}
The gravitational force on an object that has mass. Measured in newtons (N). It acts towards the centre of the planet.
:::

:::definition{term="Gravitational field strength, g"}
The gravitational force per unit mass at a place. Measured in newtons per kilogram (N/kg). Near the Earth's surface $g \approx 9.8\ \text{N/kg}$.
:::

:::formula{name="Weight"}
$$
W = mg \qquad \text{so} \qquad g = \frac{W}{m}
$$
:::

## Mass and weight compared

| | Mass | Weight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | quantity of matter | gravitational force |
| Unit | kilogram (kg) | newton (N) |
| Scalar or vector | scalar | vector (acts downwards) |
| Changes with place? | no | yes, with $g$ |
| Measured with | a balance | a force meter (spring balance) |

### g has two meanings

The same $g$ appears in two places. As **gravitational field strength** it is $9.8\ \text{N/kg}$: every kilogram is pulled with $9.8\ \text{N}$. As the **acceleration of free fall** it is $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. The two units are equivalent, because $1\ \text{N/kg} = 1\ \text{m/s}^2$.

:::worked-solution[The weight of a bag of cement]
1. A bag of cement has a mass of $50\ \text{kg}$; take $g = 9.8\ \text{N/kg}$.
2. $W = mg = 50 \times 9.8 = 490\ \text{N}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Weight on the Moon]
On the Moon $g = 1.6\ \text{N/kg}$. An astronaut with her suit has a mass of $120\ \text{kg}$.

1. Her mass on the Moon is still $120\ \text{kg}$.
2. Her weight on the Moon $= 120 \times 1.6 = 192\ \text{N}$.
3. On the Earth her weight would be $120 \times 9.8 = 1176\ \text{N}$: about six times more.
:::

## Comparing masses with a balance

A beam balance compares the weights of two objects in the same gravitational field. Because both pans are pulled by the same $g$, balancing the weights also balances the masses. That is why a beam balance would give the same reading for the mass of a potato on the Moon as on the Earth, while a spring balance (force meter) would show a smaller weight there.

For Cambridge Extended candidates: weight is the **effect of a gravitational field on a mass**. Anywhere there is a gravitational field, a mass experiences a weight equal to $mg$ in the direction of the field.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Weighing in kilograms"}
Weight is a force, measured in newtons. "My weight is 60 kg" is really a statement about mass.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mass changes on the Moon"}
The amount of matter does not change when you travel. Only the weight changes, because $g$ is different.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using grams in W = mg"}
Convert grams to kilograms first: $250\ \text{g} = 0.25\ \text{kg}$.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Define weight" scores with "the gravitational force on an object (that has mass)". "How heavy something is" does not score.
:::

:::exam-tip
State the unit of $g$ that fits the context: N/kg when finding a weight, m/s² when describing a fall.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mass is the quantity of matter (kg); it is the same everywhere.
- Weight is the gravitational force on a mass (N); it depends on $g$.
- $W = mg$, with $g \approx 9.8\ \text{N/kg}$ on the Earth's surface.
- Gravitational field strength (N/kg) equals the acceleration of free fall (m/s²).
- A balance compares masses; a force meter measures weight.
:::
