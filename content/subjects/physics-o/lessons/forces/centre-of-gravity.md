## Introduction

A woman carrying a heavy bucket of water on her head walks upright and steady. A minibus with luggage piled high on its roof rack is more likely to roll over on a sharp bend. Racing cars are built low and wide. All of this is about where an object's weight acts: its **centre of gravity**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Centre of gravity"}
The point through which the whole weight of an object can be taken to act.
:::

:::definition{term="Centre of mass"}
The point at which the whole mass of an object can be taken to be concentrated. In a uniform gravitational field it is at the same place as the centre of gravity (ZIMSEC uses both terms).
:::

:::definition{term="Stability"}
How hard it is to topple an object. A stable object returns to its original position after being tilted slightly.
:::

## Where is the centre of gravity?

For a **regular**, uniform object it is at the geometric centre: the middle of a metre rule, the centre of a uniform disc or rectangle. It does not have to be inside the material: the centre of gravity of a ring is at the empty centre.

### Finding it for an irregular lamina

1. Make a small hole near the edge of the cardboard shape and hang it freely from a pin held in a clamp, so it can swing.
2. Hang a **plumb line** (a thread with a small weight) from the same pin.
3. When everything is still, mark the line of the thread on the card.
4. Repeat from a second (and a third) hole well away from the first.
5. The centre of gravity is where the lines cross. The third line is a check: all three should meet at one point.

This works because a freely hanging object comes to rest with its centre of gravity **directly below** the point of suspension.

## Stability and toppling

An object topples when the vertical line through its centre of gravity falls **outside its base**. Then the weight produces a moment that turns the object further over.

:::example{title="Tilting a bottle"}
Tilt a full bottle a little and let go: the line of its weight still passes through the base, so the weight's moment turns it back upright. Tilt it further, until the line of the weight passes outside the edge of the base, and the moment now turns it over: it topples.
:::

An object is **more stable** when it has:

- a **low** centre of gravity, and
- a **wide** base.

That is why racing cars are low and wide, why buses carry heavy luggage underneath rather than on the roof, and why a tall load on a scotch cart is dangerous.

### Three kinds of equilibrium (ZIMSEC)

- **Stable**: a slight tilt raises the centre of gravity, and the object returns (a cone standing on its base).
- **Unstable**: a slight tilt lowers the centre of gravity, and the object falls over (a cone balanced on its point).
- **Neutral**: a tilt neither raises nor lowers the centre of gravity, so the object stays where it is put (a cone lying on its side, a ball on a flat floor).

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="The centre of gravity must be inside the object"}
For shapes like a ring, a boomerang or a horseshoe it lies in the empty space.
:::

:::mistake{title="Heavy means stable"}
Stability depends on the height of the centre of gravity and the width of the base, not just on the weight.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When describing the plumb-line experiment, say that the lamina must hang **freely**, that you mark the vertical line of the plumb line, repeat from **at least two** points, and that the centre of gravity is where the lines **intersect**.
:::

:::exam-tip
To explain toppling, use the words "the line of action of the weight falls outside the base, so the weight has a moment about the edge that turns the object over".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The centre of gravity is the point where the whole weight seems to act.
- For a regular uniform object it is at the geometric centre; it can lie outside the material.
- For an irregular lamina, hang it freely and use a plumb line from two or more points; the lines cross at the centre of gravity.
- An object topples when its weight acts outside its base.
- A low centre of gravity and a wide base make an object more stable.
:::
