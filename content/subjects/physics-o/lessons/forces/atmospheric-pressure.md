## Introduction

We live at the bottom of an ocean of air. The weight of the atmosphere above us presses on everything with a pressure of about $100\,000\ \text{Pa}$ at sea level: roughly the weight of a small car on every square metre. We do not notice it because it pushes equally from all sides and our bodies push back. This ZIMSEC lesson (topic 3.6) explores atmospheric pressure and some machines that use fluid pressure.

You should already know $p = F/A$ and $p = \rho g h$.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Atmospheric pressure"}
The pressure exerted by the weight of the air above a surface. About $1.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$ at sea level; it decreases with altitude.
:::

:::definition{term="Barometer"}
An instrument that measures atmospheric pressure.
:::

:::definition{term="Manometer"}
A U-shaped tube of liquid used to measure the pressure of a gas compared with atmospheric pressure.
:::

## Demonstrating atmospheric pressure

- **Collapsing can**: a little water is boiled in a metal can, which is then sealed and cooled. The steam condenses, the pressure inside drops, and the outside air crushes the can.
- **Drinking straw**: sucking lowers the pressure in the straw; atmospheric pressure on the drink pushes it up.
- **Rubber sucker**: pressing it squeezes out air; the atmosphere outside holds it against the wall.
- **Magdeburg hemispheres**: two metal bowls with the air pumped out between them cannot easily be pulled apart.

## The mercury barometer

A long glass tube is filled with mercury and turned upside down in a dish of mercury. The mercury falls until the column is about $760\ \text{mm}$ high at sea level, leaving a vacuum at the top. The atmosphere pushing on the mercury in the dish holds the column up, so the **vertical height** of the column measures the atmospheric pressure. Tilting the tube or using a wider tube does not change the vertical height.

:::worked-solution[Atmospheric pressure from a barometer]
On a high plateau the mercury column in a barometer is $0.70\ \text{m}$ high. The density of mercury is $13\,600\ \text{kg/m}^3$; take $g = 9.8\ \text{N/kg}$.

1. $p = \rho g h = 13\,600 \times 9.8 \times 0.70$.
2. $p = 93\,296\ \text{Pa} \approx 93\ \text{kPa}$, less than at sea level because there is less air above.
:::

An **aneroid barometer** uses a sealed metal box that squashes or expands as the pressure changes, moving a pointer: it has no liquid, so it is portable.

## The manometer

One side of a U-tube containing water is connected to a gas supply; the other is open to the air. The gas pushes the water down on its side and up on the open side. The difference in levels $h$ shows how much the gas pressure exceeds atmospheric pressure: excess pressure $= \rho g h$.

:::worked-solution[Gas pressure with a manometer]
1. The water level on the open side stands $12\ \text{cm}$ higher than on the gas side.
2. Excess pressure $= 1000 \times 9.8 \times 0.12 = 1176\ \text{Pa}$ above atmospheric.
3. If the atmospheric pressure is $100\,000\ \text{Pa}$, the gas pressure is $101\,176\ \text{Pa}$.
:::

## Pressure and weather

Weather maps show **isobars**, lines joining places of equal pressure.

- **High pressure**: sinking air, usually clear skies and dry, settled weather.
- **Low pressure**: rising air that cools to form clouds, often rain and unsettled weather.
- **Wind** blows from high to low pressure; isobars **close together** mean a steep pressure change and **strong winds**.

A falling barometer reading warns that rain may be coming.

## Applications of fluid pressure

### Hydraulic systems

Pressure applied to an enclosed liquid is passed on equally to every part of it. A small force on a small piston creates a pressure that pushes on a large piston with a much larger force: $\dfrac{F_1}{A_1} = \dfrac{F_2}{A_2}$. Hydraulic jacks, car brakes and the arms of earth-moving machines use this.

:::worked-solution[A hydraulic jack]
1. A force of $50\ \text{N}$ acts on a small piston of area $4.0\ \text{cm}^2$, so the pressure is $\dfrac{50}{4.0} = 12.5\ \text{N/cm}^2$.
2. The large piston has an area of $200\ \text{cm}^2$, so the force on it is $12.5 \times 200 = 2500\ \text{N}$, enough to lift part of a car.
:::

### Pressure cooker

The sealed lid traps steam, raising the pressure inside. At higher pressure water boils at a **higher temperature** (above $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$), so food cooks faster. At high altitude, where air pressure is lower, water boils below $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and food in an open pot takes longer to cook.

### Water reticulation

Town water is pumped into tanks on towers or hills. The height of water above a tap gives pressure $\rho g h$, so water flows out of taps throughout the town. Taps far below the tank have the greatest pressure.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Measuring along a tilted tube"}
Pressure depends on the **vertical** height of the liquid column, not on the length of liquid in a sloping tube.
:::

:::mistake{title="The manometer reads the gas pressure directly"}
The level difference gives the gas pressure **above atmospheric**. Add atmospheric pressure for the total.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When explaining a demonstration, name the cause of the pressure difference (air removed, steam condensed) and say which way the larger pressure pushes.
:::

:::exam-tip
For hydraulic machines, state that pressure is transmitted equally through the liquid and that liquids are (almost) incompressible.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Atmospheric pressure (about $10^5\ \text{Pa}$ at sea level) comes from the weight of the air; it falls with altitude.
- A mercury barometer measures it by the vertical height of a mercury column: $p = \rho g h$.
- A manometer measures gas pressure above atmospheric from the difference in liquid levels.
- High pressure usually brings fine weather, low pressure clouds and rain; wind blows from high to low.
- Hydraulic systems pass pressure through a liquid to multiply force; pressure cookers raise the boiling point.
:::
