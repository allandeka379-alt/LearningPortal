## Introduction

A slow-moving loaded haulage truck is far harder to stop than a fast cyclist. What matters is not just the speed or the mass, but both together: the **momentum**.

**Board note:** ZIMSEC (topic 3.2) requires the definition of momentum and the calculation $p = mv$ only; conservation of momentum is not required for ZIMSEC. For Cambridge all of this lesson is Supplement content.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Momentum"}
Mass × velocity. A vector, in the direction of the velocity.
:::

:::formula{name="Momentum"}
$$
p = mv
$$

Unit: kg m/s (which is the same as N s).
:::

:::definition{term="Impulse"}
Force × the time for which it acts. It equals the change in momentum it produces.
:::

:::formula{name="Impulse"}
$$
\text{impulse} = F\Delta t = \Delta(mv)
$$
:::

## Calculating momentum

:::worked-solution[The momentum of a runner]
1. A $60\ \text{kg}$ athlete runs at $7.5\ \text{m/s}$.
2. $p = mv = 60 \times 7.5 = 450\ \text{kg m/s}$ in the direction she is running.
:::

Because momentum is a vector, direction matters: if one direction is positive, momentum in the opposite direction is negative.

## Impulse and force

Rearranging the impulse equation gives Newton's second law in terms of momentum: the resultant force equals the **rate of change of momentum**,

$$
F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}
$$

:::worked-solution[Kicking a football]
A $0.45\ \text{kg}$ ball at rest is kicked and leaves the boot at $24\ \text{m/s}$. The boot is in contact with it for $0.010\ \text{s}$.

1. Change in momentum $= 0.45 \times 24 - 0 = 10.8\ \text{kg m/s}$, so the impulse is $10.8\ \text{N s}$.
2. Average force $= \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{10.8}{0.010} = 1080\ \text{N}$.
:::

### Safety: making the stopping time longer

In a crash a passenger's momentum must drop to zero. The change in momentum is fixed, so **increasing the time** over which it happens **decreases the force**. Crumple zones in the front of a car, stretchy seat belts, airbags, cushioned helmets and the sand pit in long jump all work this way.

## Conservation of momentum (Cambridge)

When objects collide or push apart, and no external force acts, the **total momentum before = total momentum after**.

:::worked-solution[Two trolleys that stick together]
A $2.0\ \text{kg}$ trolley moving at $3.0\ \text{m/s}$ hits a stationary $1.0\ \text{kg}$ trolley and they move off together.

1. Momentum before $= 2.0 \times 3.0 + 1.0 \times 0 = 6.0\ \text{kg m/s}$.
2. Momentum after $= (2.0 + 1.0)v = 3.0v$.
3. $3.0v = 6.0$, so $v = 2.0\ \text{m/s}$ in the original direction.
:::

:::worked-solution[Recoil]
A $50\ \text{kg}$ girl standing on a skateboard throws a $2.0\ \text{kg}$ bag forwards at $5.0\ \text{m/s}$. The total momentum before is zero.

1. Momentum of the bag $= 2.0 \times 5.0 = 10\ \text{kg m/s}$ forwards.
2. The girl must have $10\ \text{kg m/s}$ backwards: $v = \dfrac{10}{50} = 0.20\ \text{m/s}$ backwards (ignoring the board's mass).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Ignoring direction"}
In a head-on collision the momenta are in opposite directions, so one is negative. Adding them as if both were positive gives the wrong total.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the speed after for both objects"}
Objects that stick together share one final velocity; objects that bounce apart usually have different final velocities.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
State the principle before using it: "total momentum before = total momentum after, since no external force acts". Then show the momentum of each object.
:::

:::exam-tip
For safety questions use the full chain: same change in momentum, longer time, smaller rate of change of momentum, so smaller force on the passenger.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Momentum $p = mv$ (kg m/s), a vector.
- Impulse $= F\Delta t$, which equals the change in momentum (N s).
- Resultant force = rate of change of momentum, $F = \Delta p / \Delta t$.
- With no external force, total momentum is conserved in collisions and explosions (Cambridge).
- Crumple zones, seat belts and airbags lengthen the stopping time and so reduce the force.
:::
