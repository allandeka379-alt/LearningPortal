## Introduction

A child whirls a stone on a string, a car follows a curve on the Masvingo road, the Moon goes round the Earth. In each case the object moves in a circle, and in each case something must be **pulling it towards the centre**. Without that force, the object would carry straight on.

For Cambridge this is Supplement content; ZIMSEC covers it in topic 3.3. You should know Newton's first law and that velocity is a vector.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Centripetal force"}
The resultant force on an object moving in a circle. It acts towards the centre of the circle, at right angles to the object's motion.
:::

:::definition{term="Centripetal acceleration"}
The acceleration of an object moving in a circle, directed towards the centre. It exists even at constant speed, because the direction of the velocity keeps changing.
:::

## Why a force is needed

By Newton's first law, an object moves in a **straight line** at constant speed unless a resultant force acts. Moving in a circle means the direction changes all the time, so the velocity changes, so there must be a resultant force. That force acts **towards the centre**, at right angles to the motion: it changes the direction but not the speed.

:::example{title="What provides the centripetal force?"}
- A stone whirled on a string: the **tension** in the string.
- A car going round a bend: **friction** between the tyres and the road.
- The Moon orbiting the Earth: **gravitational attraction** of the Earth.
- An electron in the simple model of an atom: **electrostatic attraction** to the nucleus.
:::

If the force suddenly disappears (the string breaks, or the road is covered in oil so there is no grip), the object moves off in a **straight line along the tangent** to the circle at that point. It does not fly outwards along the radius.

## How the force depends on speed, radius and mass

For an object moving in a circle, the size of the force needed changes in these ways:

| Change (other things kept the same) | Force needed |
| --- | --- |
| larger **speed** (mass and radius constant) | larger |
| smaller **radius**, a tighter circle (mass and speed constant) | larger |
| larger **mass** (speed and radius constant) | larger |

Turning the table round: if the force increases while mass and radius stay the same, the speed increases; if the force increases while mass and speed stay the same, the radius decreases.

:::example{title="A car on a bend"}
A loaded truck needs more friction to take a bend than an empty one (larger mass). Taking the bend faster needs more friction (larger speed), and a sharper bend needs more still (smaller radius). On a wet or gravel road the maximum friction is smaller, so the truck must slow down: if the friction needed is more than the road can give, the truck skids outwards along a straight line.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Centrifugal force pushes outwards"}
The only real force in the horizontal direction for a car on a bend is friction, acting **inwards**. You feel thrown outwards because your body tends to keep going straight (inertia) while the car turns.
:::

:::mistake{title="Constant speed means no acceleration"}
In circular motion the direction changes all the time, so there is an acceleration towards the centre even at constant speed.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip

:::

:::exam-tip

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Circular motion needs a resultant force towards the centre, at right angles to the motion.
- The force changes the direction of motion, not the speed; there is an acceleration towards the centre.
- Tension, friction and gravity can each provide this force.
- More force is needed for a larger speed, a smaller radius or a larger mass.
- If the force is removed, the object moves off along a tangent.
:::
