## Introduction

A compass needle swings round to point north, a fridge magnet holds a shopping list in place, and a magnet pulls nails out of a pile of sawdust but ignores the brass screws. Magnets attract only certain materials, and every magnet is surrounded by an invisible **magnetic field** that we can map.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Magnetic poles"}
The regions near the ends of a magnet where its magnetic force is strongest: the north-seeking (N) pole and the south-seeking (S) pole.
:::

:::definition{term="Magnetic material"}
A material that is attracted by a magnet and can be magnetised: iron, steel, nickel and cobalt.
:::

:::definition{term="Magnetic field"}
A region in which a magnetic pole experiences a force.
:::

## Properties of magnets

- A freely suspended bar magnet comes to rest pointing **north–south**. The end that points north is the **north-seeking pole**.
- **Like poles repel; unlike poles attract.**
- A magnet attracts **magnetic materials** (iron, steel, nickel, cobalt) whichever pole is used. **Non-magnetic materials**, such as copper, aluminium, brass, wood, plastic and glass, are not attracted.
- The force is strongest at the poles.

:::example{title="Is this bar a magnet?"}
Bring each end of an unknown iron-coloured bar near the N pole of a compass. If one end **repels** the N pole, the bar is a magnet. Attraction alone proves nothing, because a magnet attracts unmagnetised iron as well. **Repulsion is the only sure test** for a magnet.
:::

## Induced magnetism

When a piece of magnetic material is placed near or touching a magnet, it becomes a magnet itself: this is **induced magnetism**. The end nearest the magnet's N pole becomes an S pole, so the two attract. A chain of steel paper clips hanging from a magnet shows this: each clip is magnetised by the one above.

## Soft iron and steel

| | Soft iron | Steel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| magnetising | easily magnetised | harder to magnetise |
| keeping magnetism | loses it easily when removed from the field | keeps it |
| type of magnet | **temporary** magnet | **permanent** magnet |
| typical use | cores of electromagnets, relays and transformers | compass needles, loudspeaker and fridge magnets |

(ZIMSEC) Inside a magnetic material, tiny regions called **domains** each behave like a small magnet. In an unmagnetised bar they point in random directions; magnetising lines them up.

## Magnetic field lines

Field lines show the shape and direction of a magnetic field.

- Outside a magnet, field lines run from the **N pole to the S pole**.
- The direction of the field at a point is the direction of the force on a **north pole** placed there, which is the way a compass needle points.
- Lines never cross. Around a bar magnet they loop out from the N pole and curve round into the S pole.
- (Cambridge Supplement) The **closer** the lines, the **stronger** the field, so the field is strongest near the poles.

![Magnetic field of a bar magnet: field lines loop out of the north pole and curve round into the south pole, never crossing, and are closest together near the poles where the field is strongest.](media:diagrams/magnet-field-lines.png "Figure: the magnetic field of a bar magnet")

Between two magnets with **unlike** poles facing, the lines run straight across the gap. With **like** poles facing, the lines bend away from each other and there is a **neutral point** midway where the fields cancel.

### Plotting the field

- **Plotting compasses**: place the magnet on paper and put a small compass near the N pole. Mark a dot at the end the compass needle points to, move the compass so its tail is over that dot, and repeat until you reach the S pole. Join the dots into a line with an arrow, then start again from a different point.
- **Iron filings**: lay paper over the magnet, sprinkle iron filings and tap the paper gently. The filings line up along the field lines, showing the **pattern** quickly, but not the direction.

## Magnetic forces as interacting fields (Cambridge Supplement)

When two magnets are close, their fields combine. The force between them is an interaction between the two magnetic fields: where the combined field lines join up from one magnet to the other (unlike poles), the magnets attract; where the lines push apart (like poles), they repel.

## Magnetising and demagnetising (ZIMSEC)

Ways to **magnetise** a steel bar:

- **Stroking**: stroke the bar from end to end many times with one pole of a magnet, always in the same direction, lifting the magnet well clear between strokes. The end where each stroke finishes becomes the **opposite** pole to the stroking pole.
- **Electrical method** (best): place the bar inside a **solenoid** (a long coil of wire) and pass a large **direct current** through it for a moment.

Ways to **demagnetise** a magnet:

- **Heating** it strongly, or **hammering** it (especially while it lies east–west), which jumbles the domains.
- **Electrical method**: place it in a solenoid carrying **alternating current** and slowly pull it out to a distance while the current still flows.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="All metals are magnetic"}
Only iron, steel, nickel and cobalt (and alloys containing them) are magnetic. Copper, aluminium, brass, gold and silver are not.
:::

:::mistake{title="Field lines go from S to N"}
Outside a magnet, field lines go from N to S: they show the way a north pole would be pushed.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In field diagrams, draw smooth lines that do not cross, with arrows from N to S, and closer lines near the poles.
:::

:::exam-tip
In "explain induced magnetism" answers, name the pole induced at the near end (the opposite one) and conclude that unlike poles attract.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Like poles repel, unlike poles attract; magnets attract iron, steel, nickel and cobalt.
- Magnetic materials near a magnet become magnets themselves (induced magnetism).
- Soft iron makes temporary magnets; steel makes permanent magnets.
- Field lines run from N to S, show the force on a north pole, and are closer where the field is stronger.
- Fields are plotted with compasses (direction) or iron filings (pattern); steel is magnetised by stroking or with a d.c. solenoid and demagnetised by heating, hammering or an a.c. solenoid (ZIMSEC).
:::
