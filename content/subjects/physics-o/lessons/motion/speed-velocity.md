## Introduction

Speed is everywhere in daily life: the speedometer of a commuter omnibus, the timing of an athlete at the National Sports Stadium, the speed limit signs on the Harare–Bulawayo road. In physics we describe motion precisely, and we separate **how fast** something moves (speed) from **how fast and in which direction** (velocity).

You should be comfortable rearranging simple formulas and converting units.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Distance"}
The total length of the path travelled (a scalar).
:::

:::definition{term="Displacement"}
The straight-line distance from the starting point to the finishing point, in a stated direction (a vector).
:::

:::definition{term="Speed"}
The distance travelled per unit time.
:::

:::definition{term="Velocity"}
The speed in a given direction (the rate of change of displacement).
:::

:::formula{name="Speed"}
$$
v = \frac{s}{t}
$$

where $s$ is the distance in metres and $t$ the time in seconds, giving $v$ in $\text{m/s}$.
:::

## Average speed

Most journeys are not at a steady speed: a bus slows down at towns and speeds up on open road. The **average speed** treats the whole journey together.

:::formula{name="Average speed"}
$$
\text{average speed} = \frac{\text{total distance travelled}}{\text{total time taken}}
$$
:::

:::worked-solution[An athlete's lap]
1. A runner completes one $400\ \text{m}$ lap in $64\ \text{s}$.
2. Average speed $= \dfrac{400}{64} = 6.25\ \text{m/s}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Average speed over two stages]
A car travels $30\ \text{km}$ at $60\ \text{km/h}$ and then another $30\ \text{km}$ at $40\ \text{km/h}$.

1. Time for stage 1 $= \dfrac{30}{60} = 0.50\ \text{h}$.
2. Time for stage 2 $= \dfrac{30}{40} = 0.75\ \text{h}$.
3. Total distance $= 60\ \text{km}$; total time $= 1.25\ \text{h}$.
4. Average speed $= \dfrac{60}{1.25} = 48\ \text{km/h}$, **not** the average of 60 and 40.
:::

## Converting units

$1\ \text{km/h} = \dfrac{1000\ \text{m}}{3600\ \text{s}}$, so to convert km/h to m/s **divide by 3.6**, and to convert m/s to km/h **multiply by 3.6**. For example, the $80\ \text{km/h}$ speed limit through many towns is $80 \div 3.6 = 22\ \text{m/s}$ (to 2 s.f.).

## Distance and displacement, speed and velocity

:::example{title="Walking round a school block"}
A learner walks $120\ \text{m}$ east along one side of a school block and then $50\ \text{m}$ north. The **distance** walked is $170\ \text{m}$. The **displacement** is the straight line from start to finish: $\sqrt{120^2 + 50^2} = 130\ \text{m}$, in a north-easterly direction.
:::

An object moving at a steady speed can still change its **velocity** by changing direction: a bicycle going round a circular track at a constant speed has a constantly changing velocity.

### Measuring speed in the laboratory

A **ticker-tape timer** makes 50 dots each second on a paper tape pulled by a trolley, so the time between dots is $0.02\ \text{s}$. Widely spaced dots mean a fast trolley. Measuring the length of a 5-space section and dividing by $5 \times 0.02 = 0.1\ \text{s}$ gives the average speed for that section. **Light gates** connected to a timer or data logger are an alternative.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Averaging the speeds"}
The average speed is total distance ÷ total time. Averaging the two stage speeds is only correct if the stages take equal **times**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing units"}
Kilometres with seconds gives km/s, not m/s. Convert first: kilometres to metres, minutes or hours to seconds.
:::

:::mistake{title="Speed and velocity are not the same"}
Velocity needs a direction. "25 m/s" is a speed; "25 m/s due north" is a velocity.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Write the equation, substitute with units, then give the answer with its unit: $v = \dfrac{s}{t} = \dfrac{150\ \text{m}}{12\ \text{s}} = 12.5\ \text{m/s}$. The unit is often a separate mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
In "describe how you would measure the speed of …" questions, name the instruments (tape measure, stop-watch or light gates), say what you measure, and give the calculation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Speed = distance ÷ time ($v = s/t$); the SI unit is m/s.
- Average speed = total distance ÷ total time.
- Divide km/h by 3.6 to get m/s; multiply m/s by 3.6 to get km/h.
- Distance and speed are scalars; displacement and velocity are vectors.
- A change of direction changes the velocity even if the speed stays the same.
:::
