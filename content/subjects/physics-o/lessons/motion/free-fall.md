## Introduction

Drop a stone and it speeds up as it falls. Drop a sheet of paper and it flutters down slowly. Skydivers above Victoria Falls fall fast at first, then at a steady speed, and land gently once the parachute opens. All of this is explained by two forces: the **weight** of the object pulling it down and **air resistance** pushing up against the motion.

You should already know what acceleration is and how to read a speed-time graph.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Free fall"}
Motion under gravity alone, with no air resistance or other force acting.
:::

:::definition{term="Acceleration of free fall, g"}
The acceleration of an object falling freely near the Earth's surface: roughly constant at about $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
:::

:::definition{term="Terminal velocity"}
The constant, maximum velocity reached by a falling object when the air (or liquid) resistance on it equals its weight.
:::

## Falling without air resistance

In a vacuum all objects fall with the **same** acceleration, whatever their mass: a feather and a coin dropped together in an evacuated tube hit the bottom together. Near the Earth's surface this acceleration is about $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, so a freely falling object gains about $9.8\ \text{m/s}$ of speed every second. (Some ZIMSEC questions use $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$; always use the value the question gives.)

:::worked-solution[The speed of a falling stone]
A stone is dropped from rest from a bridge over the Mazowe River. Ignore air resistance and take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.

1. After $2.5\ \text{s}$, $v = u + at = 0 + 9.8 \times 2.5 = 24.5\ \text{m/s}$.
2. The distance fallen is the area under the speed-time graph: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 2.5 \times 24.5 = 30.6\ \text{m}$ (about $31\ \text{m}$).
:::

## Falling through air: terminal velocity

As an object falls faster, the air resistance on it increases. This changes the **resultant force**, and so the acceleration:

1. **Just after release**: the speed is small, so air resistance is small. The resultant force is almost the full weight, and the acceleration is about $g$.
2. **Speeding up**: air resistance grows, so the resultant force (weight − air resistance) gets smaller and the acceleration decreases.
3. **Terminal velocity**: air resistance equals the weight. The resultant force is zero, the acceleration is zero and the speed stays constant.

On a speed-time graph this is a curve that starts with a gradient of about $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ and levels off to a horizontal line at the terminal velocity.

:::example{title="A skydiver opens a parachute"}
At terminal velocity a skydiver falls at about $55\ \text{m/s}$. Opening the parachute suddenly increases the air resistance so that it is **greater** than the weight: the resultant force is upwards and the skydiver **decelerates**. As the speed falls, the air resistance decreases until it equals the weight again, at a new, much lower terminal velocity of about $5\ \text{m/s}$, safe for landing.
:::

The same happens in liquids: a steel ball dropped into a tall tube of thick oil quickly reaches a small terminal velocity, because the drag in a liquid is much larger than in air.

## Measuring g

Drop a small, heavy ball (so that air resistance is small) through a known height $h$ and time the fall, using light gates or an electronic timer to avoid reaction-time errors. ZIMSEC learners then use $h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$, so $g = \dfrac{2h}{t^2}$.

:::worked-solution[Finding g from a timed drop]
1. A steel ball falls $1.80\ \text{m}$ from rest in $0.61\ \text{s}$, timed electronically.
2. $g = \dfrac{2h}{t^2} = \dfrac{2 \times 1.80}{0.61^2} = \dfrac{3.60}{0.372} = 9.7\ \text{m/s}^2$.
3. Repeating for several heights and averaging improves the result.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Heavier objects fall faster"}
Without air resistance every object has the same acceleration $g$. With air resistance, shape and size matter more than mass: a crumpled sheet of paper falls faster than a flat one of the same mass.
:::

:::mistake{title="Zero acceleration means stopped"}
At terminal velocity the acceleration is zero but the object is still moving fast, at a constant speed.
:::

:::mistake{title="The parachute makes the diver go up"}
When the parachute opens, the resultant force is upwards, so the diver **slows down**; the diver still moves downwards.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Terminal-velocity explanations score marks in a chain: speed increases → air resistance increases → resultant force decreases → acceleration decreases → air resistance equals weight → resultant force zero → constant (terminal) velocity.
:::

:::exam-tip
State $g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ near the Earth's surface (Cambridge) and use the value printed in the question.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Near the Earth's surface, a freely falling object accelerates at $g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, whatever its mass.
- Air resistance increases with speed, so the resultant force and the acceleration of a falling object decrease.
- Terminal velocity is reached when air resistance equals weight: resultant force zero, constant speed.
- Opening a parachute makes air resistance exceed the weight, so the diver decelerates to a lower terminal velocity.
- $g$ can be measured by timing a heavy ball falling a known height, using light gates.
:::
