## Introduction

In 1896 Henri Becquerel found that uranium salts fogged photographic plates wrapped in black paper. The uranium was giving out invisible radiation all by itself. We now know there are three main kinds of nuclear radiation, **alpha**, **beta** and **gamma**, and that they differ greatly in how far they travel and how much damage they do.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Radioactivity"}
The spontaneous emission of radiation (alpha particles, beta particles or gamma rays) from the unstable nuclei of certain atoms.
:::

:::definition{term="Ionising radiation"}
Radiation that can knock electrons out of atoms, turning them into ions.
:::

## Spontaneous and random

Radioactive emission is **spontaneous**: it is not affected by temperature, pressure or chemical reactions. It is also **random**: we cannot predict when a particular nucleus will decay, or in which direction the radiation will go.

## The three types of radiation

| | Alpha (α) | Beta (β) | Gamma (γ) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nature | helium nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons | fast-moving electron (from the nucleus) | electromagnetic wave of very high frequency |
| relative charge | $+2$ | $-1$ | $0$ |
| relative mass | 4 | about $\dfrac{1}{1840}$ | 0 |
| ionising effect | strong | moderate (weaker) | weak |
| penetrating ability | low: stopped by a sheet of paper or a few centimetres of air | medium: stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium | high: only reduced by thick lead or concrete |

The more strongly a radiation ionises, the more quickly it loses its energy and the less penetrating it is.

:::example{title="Identifying a radiation"}
A detector near a source records a high count rate. A sheet of paper placed in front of the source makes no difference, but 5 mm of aluminium reduces the count rate to the background level. The radiation cannot be alpha (paper would have stopped it) or gamma (aluminium would hardly reduce it), so it is **beta** radiation.
:::

## Why their ionising effects differ (Cambridge Supplement)

- **Alpha** particles have a large charge ($+2$) and a large mass; they move relatively slowly but carry a lot of kinetic energy. They interact strongly with almost every atom they pass, producing very many ions along a short path, so they lose their energy quickly.
- **Beta** particles have a smaller charge ($-1$) and much less mass and travel much faster, so they interact less often and produce fewer ions per centimetre.
- **Gamma** rays have **no charge**, so they rarely interact with atoms; they produce few ions and pass through a lot of material.

## Deflection in electric and magnetic fields (Cambridge Supplement)

- **Electric field** (between charged plates): alpha particles bend towards the **negative** plate; beta particles bend towards the **positive** plate, and **more** strongly, because their mass is so much smaller; gamma rays go **straight on**.
- **Magnetic field**: alpha and beta particles are deflected in **opposite directions** (they have opposite charges), at right angles to the field, as given by Fleming's left-hand rule. Beta particles curve much more sharply. Gamma rays are **not deflected**, because they are uncharged.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Gamma is the most dangerous because it is most penetrating"}
Gamma is the most **penetrating** but the least **ionising**. Inside the body, alpha emitters are the most damaging, because all their energy is deposited in a small volume of tissue.
:::

:::mistake{title="Beta particles are orbiting electrons"}
A beta particle is an electron created and emitted by the **nucleus** when a neutron changes into a proton; it is not one of the atom's orbiting electrons.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Learn the absorber for each type: alpha by paper, beta by a few millimetres of aluminium, gamma reduced by thick lead.
:::

:::exam-tip
In deflection diagrams, show alpha and beta curving in opposite directions, beta more sharply, and gamma going straight on.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Radioactivity is the spontaneous, random emission of radiation from unstable nuclei.
- Alpha: helium nucleus, charge $+2$, strongly ionising, stopped by paper.
- Beta: fast electron, charge $-1$, moderately ionising, stopped by a few mm of aluminium.
- Gamma: electromagnetic wave, no charge, weakly ionising, reduced only by thick lead or concrete.
- Alpha and beta are deflected in opposite directions by electric and magnetic fields (beta more); gamma is not deflected (Cambridge Supplement).
:::
