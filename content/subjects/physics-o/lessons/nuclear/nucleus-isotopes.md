## Introduction

Carbon in the maize you eat, in a lump of coal from Hwange and in your own body is mostly carbon-12, but a tiny fraction is carbon-14, which is radioactive and lets scientists date ancient wood and bone. Both are carbon, yet their nuclei differ. To see how, we need to look inside the nucleus.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nucleon"}
A particle in the nucleus: a proton or a neutron.
:::

:::definition{term="Proton number (atomic number), Z"}
The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.
:::

:::definition{term="Nucleon number (mass number), A"}
The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.
:::

:::definition{term="Isotopes"}
Atoms of the same element (the same proton number) with different numbers of neutrons, and so different nucleon numbers.
:::

## Particles in the atom

| Particle | Where it is | Relative charge | Relative mass |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| proton | nucleus | $+1$ | 1 |
| neutron | nucleus | $0$ | 1 |
| electron | orbiting the nucleus | $-1$ | about $\dfrac{1}{1840}$ (almost zero) |

The number of **protons** decides which **element** an atom is: every carbon atom has 6 protons, every uranium atom 92.

## Nuclide notation

A nucleus (a **nuclide**) is written as ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$, where X is the chemical symbol, $A$ the nucleon number (top) and $Z$ the proton number (bottom).

:::formula{name="Number of neutrons"}
$$
N = A - Z
$$
:::

:::worked-solution[Inside a uranium nucleus]
A uranium nucleus is written ${}^{238}_{92}\text{U}$.

1. Protons: $Z = 92$.
2. Nucleons: $A = 238$.
3. Neutrons: $N = 238 - 92 = 146$.
4. A neutral atom also has 92 electrons, equal to the number of protons.
:::

## Isotopes

Isotopes of an element have the **same number of protons** but **different numbers of neutrons**. Because they have the same number of electrons, they behave the same chemically.

:::example{title="Isotopes of hydrogen and carbon"}
- Hydrogen: ${}^{1}_{1}\text{H}$ (no neutrons), deuterium ${}^{2}_{1}\text{H}$ (1 neutron) and tritium ${}^{3}_{1}\text{H}$ (2 neutrons).
- Carbon: ${}^{12}_{6}\text{C}$ (6 neutrons) and ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C}$ (8 neutrons).

Some isotopes are stable; others, such as tritium and carbon-14, are **radioactive**.
:::

## Charge and mass of a nucleus (Cambridge Supplement)

- The **relative charge** of a nucleus equals its **proton number**: a nucleus with $Z = 8$ has a charge of $+8$ (in units of the proton's charge).
- The **relative mass** of a nucleus is approximately its **nucleon number**, since protons and neutrons each have a relative mass of about 1. Electrons add almost nothing.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up A and Z"}
The **larger** number (top) is the nucleon number $A$; the **smaller** (bottom) is the proton number $Z$. Neutrons $= A - Z$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Isotopes have different numbers of protons"}
Different numbers of protons would make a **different element**. Isotopes differ only in their number of neutrons.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked for the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in a neutral atom, remember that electrons equal protons.
:::

:::exam-tip
Define isotopes with both parts: same number of **protons**, different number of **neutrons**.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The nucleus contains protons (charge $+1$) and neutrons (charge $0$); electrons (charge $-1$) orbit it.
- Proton number $Z$ = number of protons; nucleon number $A$ = protons + neutrons; neutrons $= A - Z$.
- Nuclide notation: ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$.
- Isotopes have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
- A nucleus's relative charge is $Z$ and its relative mass is about $A$ (Cambridge Supplement).
:::
