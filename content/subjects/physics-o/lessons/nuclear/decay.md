## Introduction

Radium glows faintly in the dark, and over thousands of years it slowly turns into radon gas. When an unstable nucleus gives out alpha or beta radiation, it does not just lose energy: it changes into the nucleus of a **different element**. Decay equations show exactly what each nucleus becomes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Radioactive decay"}
A random, spontaneous change in an unstable nucleus, in which it emits an alpha particle or a beta particle and/or gamma radiation.
:::

:::definition{term="Parent and daughter nuclei"}
The **parent** is the nucleus before the decay; the **daughter** is the nucleus formed by the decay.
:::

## Why are some nuclei unstable? (Cambridge Supplement)

A nucleus may be unstable because:

- it has **too many neutrons** compared with protons (such nuclei often decay by **beta** emission); or
- it is **too heavy**, with too many nucleons altogether (such nuclei often decay by **alpha** emission).

After an alpha or beta decay, the new nucleus is often left with extra energy, which it gives out as a **gamma ray**.

## How each emission changes the nucleus

| Emission | Particle (nuclide notation) | Change in nucleon number $A$ | Change in proton number $Z$ | New element? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| alpha | ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ (or ${}^{4}_{2}\alpha$) | decreases by 4 | decreases by 2 | yes |
| beta | ${}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$ (or ${}^{0}_{-1}\beta$) | no change | increases by 1 | yes |
| gamma | ${}^{0}_{0}\gamma$ | no change | no change | no: same nucleus, less energy |

**Beta decay** (Cambridge Supplement): inside the nucleus, a **neutron changes into a proton and an electron**. The proton stays in the nucleus (so $Z$ goes up by 1 while $A$ is unchanged) and the electron is emitted at high speed as the beta particle.

## Writing decay equations

In every decay equation, the nucleon numbers (top) and the proton numbers (bottom) must balance on the two sides.

:::worked-solution[Alpha decay of radium]
Radium-226, ${}^{226}_{88}\text{Ra}$, decays by alpha emission.

1. The daughter has $A = 226 - 4 = 222$ and $Z = 88 - 2 = 86$.
2. The element with $Z = 86$ is radon, so the equation is
$$
{}^{226}_{88}\text{Ra} \rightarrow {}^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}
$$
3. Check: $226 = 222 + 4$ and $88 = 86 + 2$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Beta decay of carbon-14]
Carbon-14, ${}^{14}_{6}\text{C}$, decays by beta emission.

1. The daughter has $A = 14$ (unchanged) and $Z = 6 + 1 = 7$: nitrogen.
2. The equation is
$$
{}^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}
$$
3. Check: $14 = 14 + 0$ and $6 = 7 + (-1)$.
:::

## Gamma emission

Gamma emission does not change $A$ or $Z$; the nucleus simply moves to a lower energy state. For example, cobalt-60 decays by beta emission into an excited nickel-60 nucleus, which then gives out gamma rays and is still nickel-60 afterwards.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Beta decay lowers the proton number"}
In beta decay a neutron becomes a proton, so the proton number goes **up** by 1. Remember that the beta particle has $Z = -1$, so $Z$ must increase on the other side to balance.
:::

:::mistake{title="Gamma decay changes the element"}
Gamma emission changes only the energy of the nucleus, not its composition.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When completing a decay equation, find the missing $A$ and $Z$ by balancing, then use $Z$ to name the element (you may be given a short list of elements).
:::

:::exam-tip
Write the alpha particle as ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ and the beta particle as ${}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$, with both numbers shown.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Radioactive decay is a random, spontaneous change in an unstable nucleus.
- Alpha decay: $A$ falls by 4 and $Z$ by 2. Beta decay: $A$ unchanged, $Z$ rises by 1 (a neutron becomes a proton and an electron).
- Gamma emission changes neither $A$ nor $Z$.
- Alpha and beta decay produce a different element.
- Nucleon numbers and proton numbers balance in decay equations (Cambridge Supplement).
:::
