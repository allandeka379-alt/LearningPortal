## Introduction

Everything around you, from the granite of a kopje to the air you breathe, is made of atoms far too small to see. Just over a hundred years ago, physicists fired tiny particles at gold foil and discovered something astonishing: an atom is almost entirely **empty space**, with nearly all its mass packed into a minute **nucleus** at its centre.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Atom"}
The smallest particle of an element, made of a positively charged nucleus with negatively charged electrons orbiting it.
:::

:::definition{term="Nucleus"}
The tiny, dense centre of an atom, which contains almost all its mass and all its positive charge.
:::

:::definition{term="Ion"}
An atom (or group of atoms) that has gained or lost electrons, so it has an overall charge.
:::

## The nuclear model

- At the centre is the **nucleus**, which is **positively** charged.
- **Electrons**, which are **negatively** charged, orbit the nucleus at relatively large distances.
- A neutral atom has equal amounts of positive and negative charge.
- The nucleus is about $10^{-15}\ \text{m}$ across, while the whole atom is about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$: roughly a hundred thousand times larger. If an atom were the size of a football stadium, its nucleus would be about the size of a pea on the centre spot.

## Forming ions

Electrons can be removed from or added to atoms (for example by friction, heat or radiation):

- an atom that **loses** one or more electrons becomes a **positive ion**;
- an atom that **gains** one or more electrons becomes a **negative ion**.

The nucleus is unchanged in both cases. Radiation that knocks electrons out of atoms is called **ionising radiation**.

## Evidence for the nucleus: alpha-particle scattering (Cambridge Supplement)

Under Ernest Rutherford's direction in 1909, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden fired a beam of **alpha particles** (small, fast, positively charged particles) at a very thin sheet of **gold foil** in a vacuum, and counted where they went using a detector they could move around the foil.

:::example{title="What they observed and what it means"}
| Observation | Conclusion |
| --- | --- |
| Most alpha particles went **straight through** the foil. | The atom is mostly **empty space**. |
| A few were **deflected** through small angles. | The alpha particles passed close to a concentrated **positive** charge, which repelled them. |
| A very small number (about 1 in 8000) **bounced back** towards the source. | The positive charge and most of the **mass** are concentrated in a **tiny, dense nucleus**; only a head-on approach could reverse an alpha particle. |
:::

These results replaced the earlier "plum pudding" model, in which the positive charge was thought to be spread evenly through the atom with electrons dotted in it. A spread-out charge could not have turned any alpha particles back.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Positive ions gain protons"}
Atoms become positive ions by **losing electrons**. The number of protons in the nucleus does not change.
:::

:::mistake{title="Most alphas bounced back"}
Only a tiny fraction bounced back. Most passed straight through, which is the evidence that atoms are mostly empty space.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For the scattering experiment, link each observation to its conclusion: straight through → empty space; small deflections → positive charge; bounced back → tiny, dense, massive nucleus.
:::

:::exam-tip
When explaining ions, always say whether electrons are gained or lost.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An atom is a tiny, positively charged nucleus with negatively charged electrons orbiting it.
- The nucleus holds almost all the mass but is about 100 000 times smaller than the atom.
- Losing electrons makes a positive ion; gaining electrons makes a negative ion.
- Alpha-particle scattering showed that atoms are mostly empty space with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus (Cambridge Supplement).
:::
