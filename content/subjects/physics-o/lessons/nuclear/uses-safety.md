## Introduction

Radioactive sources sterilise surgical gloves, keep smoke alarms working, check the thickness of paper in a mill and help doctors see how well a patient's organs work. Yet the same radiation can damage living cells. Using radioactivity safely means choosing the right isotope for each job and protecting people from unnecessary exposure.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Tracer"}
A small amount of a radioactive isotope added to a substance so that its path can be followed by detecting the radiation it gives out.
:::

:::definition{term="Irradiation"}
Exposing an object to radiation. Irradiated objects do **not** become radioactive.
:::

## Choosing an isotope (Cambridge Supplement)

Two properties decide whether an isotope suits a use: the **type of radiation** (how far it penetrates and how strongly it ionises) and the **half-life** (how long the source stays active).

| Use | Radiation needed | Half-life needed | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| smoke alarm (americium-241) | alpha | long (hundreds of years) | alpha particles ionise the air between two plates so a small current flows; smoke absorbs the alpha particles, the current drops and the alarm sounds. Alpha cannot escape the alarm casing. A long half-life means the source lasts the life of the alarm. |
| sterilising medical equipment and irradiating food (cobalt-60) | gamma | fairly long (years) | gamma penetrates sealed packaging and kills bacteria; a long half-life means the source need not be replaced often. |
| measuring the thickness of paper or aluminium foil | beta | long | beta is partly absorbed by thin sheets: if the sheet gets thicker, the count rate falls and the rollers are adjusted. Alpha would be stopped completely; gamma would pass through almost unchanged. |
| medical tracer (technetium-99m) | gamma | short (hours) | gamma escapes the body to reach a detector; a short half-life means the patient's exposure is brief. |
| finding leaks in underground pipes | gamma | short (hours or days) | gamma passes through the soil to a detector above; a short half-life means little radioactivity is left behind. |

:::example{title="Choosing a tracer for a kidney scan"}
A hospital can choose between three isotopes: X emits alpha particles and has a half-life of 6 hours; Y emits gamma rays and has a half-life of 6 hours; Z emits gamma rays and has a half-life of 30 years.

- X is unsuitable: alpha particles cannot get out of the body to reach the detector, and they are the most damaging inside it.
- Z is unsuitable: it would keep irradiating the patient for many years.
- **Y** is the best choice: its gamma rays pass out of the body to the detector, and its activity falls to a very low level within a few days.
:::

## Effects of ionising radiation on living things

Ionising radiation can damage the molecules in living cells, including DNA. The effects include:

- **cell death** (high doses can cause radiation burns and radiation sickness);
- **mutations**: changes to the DNA, which may be passed on;
- **cancer**, when damaged cells divide out of control.

Outside the body, alpha particles are stopped by the dead outer layer of skin, so beta and gamma sources are the greater hazard. Inside the body (if swallowed or breathed in), alpha emitters are the most dangerous, because all their energy is deposited in a small volume of tissue.

## Handling and storing sources safely

- Handle sources with **long tongs**, never with bare hands, and never point a source at anyone.
- Keep sources in **lead-lined containers** in a locked, labelled store when not in use.
- Use the source for as **short a time** as possible and keep as **far** from it as possible.
- People who work with radiation wear **dosimeters** (film badges or electronic meters) to monitor their dose, and work behind **shielding** such as lead screens or thick concrete.

In Zimbabwe, the Radiation Protection Authority of Zimbabwe licenses and inspects hospitals, mines and industries that use radioactive sources.

### Time, distance and shielding (Cambridge Supplement)

Exposure is kept low in three ways:

1. **Time**: the dose received is proportional to the time spent near the source, so work quickly.
2. **Distance**: the radiation spreads out and weakens with distance (and alpha and beta are absorbed by air), so stay as far away as possible, using tongs.
3. **Shielding**: put absorbing material such as lead or concrete between the source and people.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Irradiated food is radioactive"}
Food irradiated with gamma rays does **not** become radioactive; the radiation passes through and kills microbes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Long half-life tracers"}
A tracer used in the body needs a **short** half-life, so it does not keep irradiating the patient.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The type of radiation and the half-life decide which isotope suits each use (Cambridge Supplement).
- Smoke alarms use alpha; sterilisation uses gamma; thickness gauges for paper use beta; tracers use short-half-life gamma emitters.
- Ionising radiation can cause cell death, mutations and cancer.
- Sources are handled with tongs and stored in lead-lined containers; workers wear dosimeters.
- Exposure is reduced by limiting time, increasing distance and using shielding.
:::
