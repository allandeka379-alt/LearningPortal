## Introduction

When a pot of beans cooks in a kitchen, the smell soon reaches the next room. When a drop of ink falls into a glass of water, the colour slowly spreads without stirring. Both happen because all matter is made of tiny **particles** (atoms, molecules or ions) that are always moving. The **kinetic particle model** uses these particles to explain the properties of solids, liquids and gases.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Kinetic particle model"}
The idea that matter is made of tiny particles in constant motion, with forces between them.
:::

:::definition{term="Absolute zero"}
The lowest possible temperature, $-273\ ^\circ\text{C}$ ($0\ \text{K}$), at which the particles have the least possible kinetic energy.
:::

:::definition{term="Brownian motion"}
The random, jerky movement of microscopic particles (such as smoke or pollen grains) in a fluid, caused by collisions with the fast-moving molecules of the fluid.
:::

:::definition{term="Diffusion"}
The spreading out of particles from a region of high concentration to a region of low concentration because of their random motion.
:::

## Particles in the three states

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| arrangement | regular pattern (lattice) | irregular, touching | random |
| separation | very close | close | far apart |
| motion | vibrate about fixed positions | slide past each other | move fast in all directions |
| forces between particles | strong | weaker | very weak |

In particle diagrams, solids are drawn as packed rows of circles, liquids as touching circles in no pattern, and gases as a few widely spaced circles.

## Temperature and particle motion

The higher the temperature of a substance, the faster its particles move on average: temperature is related to the **average kinetic energy** of the particles. Cooling slows them down. At **absolute zero**, $-273\ ^\circ\text{C}$, the particles would have the least possible kinetic energy, and nothing can be colder.

## Evidence for moving particles: Brownian motion

Look at smoke trapped in a small glass cell through a microscope, with light shining from the side: the smoke particles appear as bright specks that dance about in a random, zig-zag way. The smoke particles are hit unevenly from all sides by **air molecules**, which are far too small to see but are moving very fast. Pollen grains in water show the same motion.

For Cambridge Extended candidates: the light, fast-moving air molecules can move the much more massive smoke particles because there are huge numbers of collisions, and at any moment more hit one side than the other. Note the distinction: the **microscopic particles** (smoke, pollen) can be seen; the **atoms or molecules** of the gas or liquid cannot.

## Diffusion (ZIMSEC)

A crystal of potassium permanganate dropped into still water slowly colours all the water purple. A smell spreads across a room. The particles spread out because of their random motion, from where they are concentrated to where they are not. Diffusion is faster in gases than in liquids, and faster at higher temperatures.

:::example{title="Explaining properties with particles"}
A gas can be compressed because its particles are far apart, with plenty of empty space between them. A solid keeps its shape because strong forces hold its particles in fixed positions; they can only vibrate.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Particles expand when heated"}
Heating makes particles move faster and further apart; the particles themselves do not get bigger.
:::

:::mistake{title="The smoke particles are the molecules"}
In Brownian motion, the visible specks are smoke particles; the air molecules that hit them are invisible.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Describe particles in three ways every time: arrangement, separation (how close) and motion.
:::

:::exam-tip
For Brownian motion, say what is seen (random, jerky movement of the smoke specks), and what causes it (collisions with fast-moving air molecules, unequal from different sides).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Matter is made of particles in constant motion, with forces between them.
- Solids: close, regular, vibrating. Liquids: close, irregular, sliding. Gases: far apart, random, fast.
- Higher temperature means a higher average kinetic energy of the particles.
- Absolute zero ($-273\ ^\circ\text{C}$) is the lowest possible temperature.
- Brownian motion and diffusion are evidence that particles move randomly.
:::
