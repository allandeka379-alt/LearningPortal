## Introduction

A nurse at a clinic checks a child for fever, a farmer monitors the temperature in a tobacco barn, and an engineer measures the temperature inside a furnace. Each uses a different kind of **thermometer**, chosen for its range, sensitivity and speed. This lesson covers the thermometer part of ZIMSEC topic 7.2.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Thermometric property"}
A physical property that changes steadily with temperature and can be measured, for example the length of a liquid column, electrical resistance or the voltage of a thermocouple.
:::

:::definition{term="Fixed points"}
Temperatures that can be reproduced exactly and are used to mark a thermometer's scale: the **ice point** (pure melting ice, $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$) and the **steam point** (steam above pure boiling water at standard atmospheric pressure, $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$).
:::

## Making a temperature scale

To calibrate a liquid-in-glass thermometer, place it in melting ice and mark the liquid level as $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$; place it in steam above boiling water and mark $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$; divide the length between the marks into 100 equal parts.

![Calibration line for an unmarked thermometer: the liquid column is 2.0 cm at the ice point (0 degrees C) and 22.0 cm at the steam point (100 degrees C); a column of 16.5 cm in the tea reads 72.5 degrees C.](media:diagrams/thermometer-calibration-line.png "Figure: calibration line of the unmarked thermometer, with the tea reading")

:::worked-solution[Reading an unmarked thermometer]
An unmarked thermometer's liquid column is $2.0\ \text{cm}$ long at the ice point and $22.0\ \text{cm}$ at the steam point. In a cup of tea the column is $16.5\ \text{cm}$ long.

1. $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ corresponds to $22.0 - 2.0 = 20.0\ \text{cm}$, so $1\ \text{cm}$ corresponds to $5\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
2. The column has risen $16.5 - 2.0 = 14.5\ \text{cm}$ above the ice point.
3. Temperature $= 14.5 \times 5 = 72.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
:::

## Qualities of a thermometer

- **Sensitivity**: how much the reading changes for each degree. A liquid-in-glass thermometer is more sensitive with a **larger bulb** and a **narrower bore** (tube).
- **Range**: the lowest and highest temperatures it can measure.
- **Linearity**: whether equal temperature changes give equal changes of the property, so the scale can have evenly spaced marks.
- **Responsiveness**: how quickly it reaches the temperature being measured (thin glass and a small bulb help).

## Types of thermometer

### Liquid-in-glass (laboratory)

Mercury or coloured alcohol expands up a narrow tube. Mercury is easy to see and expands evenly, but it freezes at $-39\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and is poisonous. Alcohol freezes at a much lower temperature (about $-115\ ^\circ\text{C}$), so it suits very cold places, but it boils at about $78\ ^\circ\text{C}$, so it cannot measure boiling water.

### Clinical thermometer

Used to measure body temperature. It has a **short range** (about $35$ to $42\ ^\circ\text{C}$), a very **narrow bore** for high sensitivity, and a **constriction** (kink) just above the bulb so the liquid thread does not fall back when the thermometer is removed from the patient; it is shaken down afterwards. Digital clinical thermometers are now common.

### Thermocouple

Two wires of different metals are joined at their ends; a temperature difference between the two junctions produces a small voltage, read on a voltmeter. Thermocouples have a very **wide range** (furnace temperatures), respond **quickly** because the junction is tiny, and can give readings far from the place being measured, but they need a meter and are usually not linear.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Bigger thermometers are more sensitive"}
Sensitivity depends on a large bulb **and** a narrow bore, not on the overall size.
:::

:::mistake{title="The constriction stops expansion"}
The constriction does not stop the liquid expanding; it breaks the thread when the liquid contracts, so the maximum reading stays on the scale.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When describing calibration, name both fixed points, how each is produced (pure melting ice; steam at standard pressure) and how the scale is divided.
:::

:::exam-tip
Choose a thermometer by matching its range and speed to the job: clinical for the body, alcohol for very low temperatures, thermocouple for furnaces or rapidly changing temperatures.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A thermometer uses a thermometric property that changes with temperature.
- Fixed points: ice point $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and steam point $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ at standard pressure.
- Sensitivity: large bulb, narrow bore. Also consider range, linearity and speed of response.
- A clinical thermometer has a short range, a narrow bore and a constriction.
- A thermocouple has a wide range and quick response but needs a voltmeter.
:::
