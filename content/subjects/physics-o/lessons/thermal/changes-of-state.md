## Introduction

Many households keep drinking water cool in an unglazed clay pot: water seeps through the clay and evaporates from the outside, cooling the water inside. A pot of water on a stove stays at its boiling point however long it boils. These are two features of **changes of state**: they take in or give out energy **without** changing the temperature, and evaporation causes cooling.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Melting point"}
The temperature at which a solid melts. For pure water (ice) it is $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Boiling point"}
The temperature at which a liquid boils. For pure water at standard atmospheric pressure it is $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Evaporation"}
The escape of the more energetic particles from the surface of a liquid.
:::

## Melting and boiling

While a substance melts or boils, energy is supplied but the **temperature does not rise**. The energy is used to **break the bonds** (overcome the forces) between particles, not to make them move faster. A **heating curve** (temperature against time for steady heating) therefore has two flat sections: one at the melting point and one at the boiling point.

**Condensation** and **solidification** are the reverse: as a gas condenses, its particles move closer and form bonds again; as a liquid freezes, its particles settle into fixed positions. Energy is **given out** in both, again at constant temperature.

## Evaporation and cooling

Particles in a liquid move at a range of speeds. Some fast particles near the surface have enough energy to escape. Because the **most energetic** particles leave, the **average kinetic energy** of those left behind falls: the liquid **cools**. This is why sweating cools you and why a clay pot keeps water cool.

:::example{title="The clay-pot cooler"}
Water soaks through the porous clay and evaporates from the outer surface. The evaporating water takes energy from the pot and the water inside, so the drinking water stays several degrees below the air temperature, especially in a dry, breezy place.
:::

### Factors affecting the rate of evaporation (Cambridge Supplement)

Evaporation is faster when:

- the **temperature** is higher (more particles have enough energy to escape);
- the **surface area** is larger (more particles are at the surface);
- **air moves** over the surface (escaped particles are blown away, so fewer return).

An object in contact with an evaporating liquid is cooled too, because the liquid takes energy from it to replace what the escaping particles carried away.

### Boiling versus evaporation (Cambridge Supplement)

| Boiling | Evaporation |
| --- | --- |
| happens at one temperature, the boiling point | happens at any temperature |
| throughout the liquid (bubbles form inside) | only at the surface |
| needs a continuous energy supply | the liquid cools as it evaporates |

## Impurities and pressure (ZIMSEC)

- **Impurities** lower the melting point and raise the boiling point: salt spread on icy roads makes the ice melt below $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$; salty water boils above $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
- **Pressure** changes the boiling point: higher pressure raises it (a pressure cooker cooks above $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$), and lower pressure lowers it (at high altitude water boils below $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$).

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Boiling water keeps getting hotter"}
Turning up the stove makes water boil faster, not hotter: it stays at its boiling point.
:::

:::mistake{title="Evaporation only happens when boiling"}
Puddles dry up and washing dries at ordinary temperatures, far below $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
To explain cooling by evaporation, use the chain: the most energetic particles escape → the average kinetic energy of the remaining particles falls → the temperature falls.
:::

:::exam-tip
To explain a flat section on a heating curve: energy is still being supplied but it breaks the bonds between particles, so their kinetic energy (and the temperature) does not increase.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Melting and boiling need energy but happen at constant temperature: the energy breaks bonds between particles.
- Pure water melts at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and boils at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ at standard pressure.
- Evaporation is the escape of the most energetic surface particles, so it cools the liquid.
- Evaporation is faster with higher temperature, larger surface area and moving air.
- Impurities lower the melting point and raise the boiling point; higher pressure raises the boiling point.
:::
