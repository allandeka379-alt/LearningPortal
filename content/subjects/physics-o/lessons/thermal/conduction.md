## Introduction

A metal spoon left in a pot of boiling sadza soon becomes too hot to hold, while a wooden cooking stick stays cool at the top. Pots are made of metal but have plastic or wooden handles. The difference is **thermal conduction**: how well energy passes through a material by heating.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Conduction"}
The transfer of thermal energy through a material from a hotter region to a colder region, without the material itself moving.
:::

:::definition{term="Thermal conductor"}
A material that conducts thermal energy well, such as a metal.
:::

:::definition{term="Thermal insulator"}
A material that conducts thermal energy badly, such as wood, plastic, wool or air.
:::

## Comparing conductors

:::example{title="Rods with wax"}
Rods of copper, aluminium, iron and glass of the same length and thickness are fixed into the side of a metal trough of hot water. Each has a small blob of wax (or a pin stuck on with wax) at its far end. The wax melts first on the copper rod, then aluminium, then iron; on the glass rod it may not melt at all. Copper is the best conductor of these. For a fair test the rods must be the same size and start at the same temperature.
:::

Metals are good conductors. Non-metals such as wood, plastic and glass are poor conductors, and gases are very poor conductors. Many solids, such as glass and brick, conduct better than true insulators but far worse than metals.

## How conduction happens (Cambridge Supplement)

### In all solids: lattice vibrations

The particles in a solid vibrate about fixed positions. When one end is heated, its particles vibrate more strongly and bump into their neighbours, passing on some energy, which passes it on again along the material. This is slow.

### In metals: free electrons

Metals also contain **free (delocalised) electrons** that can move through the whole metal. At the hot end they gain kinetic energy, travel quickly to colder parts and collide with particles there, transferring energy rapidly. This is why metals are such good thermal conductors (and also good electrical conductors).

### Why gases and liquids conduct badly

In a gas the particles are far apart, so collisions that pass energy along are rare. In most liquids the particles are close but there are no free electrons, so conduction is still poor.

## Using insulators

Air is an excellent insulator **if it is trapped** so it cannot move (otherwise it carries energy by convection). Wool, feathers, fur, polystyrene and fibreglass all work by trapping air in small pockets. Thatched roofs keep huts cool by day and warm at night for the same reason.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Metals feel cold because they are colder"}
A metal bench and a wooden bench in the same room are at the same temperature. The metal feels colder because it conducts energy away from your hand faster.
:::

:::mistake{title="Conduction moves the material"}
In conduction the material stays where it is; only energy moves through it. Movement of a fluid carrying energy is convection.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For the free-electron explanation, mention: free electrons gain kinetic energy at the hot end, move through the metal and transfer energy in collisions elsewhere.
:::

:::exam-tip
In a conduction experiment, state the control variables: same length and thickness of rod, same starting temperature, same heat source.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Conduction transfers thermal energy through a material without the material moving.
- Metals are good conductors; non-metals and gases are poor conductors (insulators).
- In all solids, energy passes by lattice vibrations; in metals, free electrons carry energy quickly.
- Gases conduct badly because their particles are far apart.
- Trapped air makes materials such as wool and polystyrene good insulators.
:::
