## Introduction

Firewood and charcoal cook meals in many rural and urban homes; diesel moves trucks and buses; coal powers industry; paraffin and gas light and heat homes during power cuts. Every fuel has costs beyond its price: to forests, to the air, to health and to the economy. This lesson covers the "social and economic considerations of using fuels" in ZIMSEC topic 8.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fuel"}
A substance that releases useful energy, usually as heat, when it is burnt.
:::

:::definition{term="Renewable fuel"}
A fuel that can be replaced within a short time, for example wood from replanted trees, biogas and ethanol from crops.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-renewable fuel"}
A fuel that took millions of years to form and will run out, for example coal, petroleum (petrol, diesel, paraffin) and natural gas.
:::

## Renewable and non-renewable fuels

| Renewable | Non-renewable |
| --- | --- |
| wood and charcoal (if trees are replanted) | coal |
| biogas from animal dung or plant waste | petrol, diesel, paraffin (from petroleum) |
| ethanol made from sugar cane or maize | natural gas and LPG |

Ethanol made from sugar cane is blended with petrol in Zimbabwe; biogas digesters on farms turn cow dung into a cooking gas and leave a useful fertiliser.

## Environmental effects

- **Deforestation**: cutting trees for firewood, charcoal and tobacco curing faster than they regrow destroys woodland, causes soil erosion and silts up rivers and dams.
- **Air pollution**: burning fuels releases carbon dioxide (a greenhouse gas linked to global warming), smoke and soot; coal also releases sulfur dioxide, which causes acid rain.
- **Carbon monoxide**: incomplete burning, with too little air, produces this colourless, odourless, poisonous gas.

## Social and economic effects

- Petroleum fuels are **imported**, so using less of them (for example by blending with locally made ethanol) saves foreign currency.
- Fuel industries create **jobs** in mining, farming (sugar cane), transport and distribution.
- Rising fuel prices raise the cost of transport and food.
- Collecting firewood takes many hours each week in rural areas, often by women and children, and smoke from indoor cooking fires harms health.

:::example{title="A charcoal brazier in a closed room"}
Every winter there are reports of families harmed while sleeping in a closed room with a burning charcoal brazier (mbaula). With little fresh air, the charcoal burns incompletely and fills the room with carbon monoxide, which cannot be seen or smelled. Braziers must only be used where there is a good flow of fresh air, and never left burning in a bedroom.
:::

## Safe handling of fuels

- Store petrol, paraffin and diesel in **approved, labelled, sealed containers**, never in drinks bottles where children may mistake them for something to drink.
- Keep fuels **away from flames** and heat, and do not smoke near them.
- Store them in a cool, well-ventilated place, out of reach of children.
- Check gas (LPG) cylinders and pipes for leaks; never look for a leak with a flame.
- Keep a fire extinguisher or sand nearby where fuels are stored or dispensed.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Wood is always renewable"}
Wood is renewable only if trees are replanted at least as fast as they are cut.
:::

:::mistake{title="If you cannot smell it, it is safe"}
Carbon monoxide has no smell. Good ventilation, not your nose, is the protection.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Renewable fuels (wood with replanting, biogas, ethanol) can be replaced; non-renewable fuels (coal, petroleum, gas) will run out.
- Using fuels can cause deforestation, air pollution, acid rain and global warming.
- Incomplete combustion makes poisonous carbon monoxide: always ventilate.
- Fuel choices affect jobs, imports, prices and health.
- Store fuels in labelled, approved containers, away from flames and children.
:::
