## Introduction

On a clear night far from town lights, you can see Venus shining brightly after sunset, Jupiter as a steady bright point, and perhaps Mars glowing orange. They are part of our **Solar System**: the Sun and everything held in orbit around it by its gravity.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Solar System"}
The Sun and all the objects that orbit it: the eight planets and their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids and comets.
:::

:::definition{term="Minor planets"}
Bodies orbiting the Sun that are smaller than the planets, including **dwarf planets** (such as Pluto and Ceres) and **asteroids** (rocky bodies, most found in a belt between Mars and Jupiter).
:::

:::definition{term="Comet"}
A small body of ice and dust in a very elongated orbit; near the Sun it heats up and forms a glowing head and a tail that points away from the Sun.
:::

## The members of the Solar System

The eight planets in order from the Sun are:

**Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune**

(a memory aid: **M**y **V**ery **E**asy **M**ethod **J**ust **S**peeds **U**p **N**aming).

- The **four inner planets** are small and **rocky**.
- The **four outer planets** are large and **gaseous**, made mostly of hydrogen and helium (with ices in Uranus and Neptune).
- **Moons** (natural satellites) orbit most of the planets; the asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter; dwarf planets such as Pluto orbit beyond Neptune.

## How the Solar System formed: the accretion model

About 4.6 billion years ago, a huge cloud of gas and dust (a nebula) began to collapse under its own **gravity**. As it collapsed, it spun faster and flattened into a rotating disc.

1. Most of the mass fell to the centre, became very hot and dense, and formed the **Sun**.
2. In the disc, dust and gas particles collided and stuck together (**accretion**), building up larger and larger bodies that became the planets.
3. Close to the hot young Sun, only rocky and metallic materials could stay solid, so the **inner planets** are small and rocky.
4. Further out, it was cold enough for ices to form as well; these bodies grew large enough to pull in huge amounts of hydrogen and helium gas, forming the **gas giants**.

## Gravity in the Solar System

- The Sun contains more than 99% of the Solar System's mass, so its gravitational pull dominates: the planets, asteroids and comets all **orbit the Sun**.
- The Sun's **gravitational attraction** provides the force that keeps each body moving in its orbit instead of flying off in a straight line.
- The gravitational field strength at a planet's surface depends on the planet's **mass** (and size): it is about $9.8\ \text{N/kg}$ on the Earth, but only about $1.6\ \text{N/kg}$ on the much less massive Moon.
- Gravitational field strength **decreases with distance** from a planet or the Sun.

## Light travel times

Distances in the Solar System are so large that even light takes a noticeable time to cross them.

:::formula{name="Time taken by light"}
$$
t = \frac{d}{c} \qquad c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}
$$
:::

:::worked-solution[Light from the Moon and from Neptune]
1. Moon: $t = \dfrac{3.84 \times 10^{8}}{3.0 \times 10^{8}} = 1.3\ \text{s}$.
2. Neptune is about $4.5 \times 10^{12}\ \text{m}$ from the Sun: $t = \dfrac{4.5 \times 10^{12}}{3.0 \times 10^{8}} = 1.5 \times 10^{4}\ \text{s}$, about 4 hours.
:::

## Orbits and planetary data (Cambridge Supplement)

Planets, minor planets and comets move in **elliptical** orbits. The Sun is at one focus of the ellipse, not at the centre, unless the orbit is very nearly circular (as most planetary orbits are). Comets have very elongated ellipses.

A body in an elliptical orbit moves **fastest when it is nearest the Sun**. This follows from the **conservation of energy**: as it moves closer to the Sun it loses gravitational potential energy and gains the same amount of kinetic energy; as it moves away it slows down again.

The Sun's gravitational field weakens with distance, so planets further out move more slowly in their orbits and take much longer to complete each one.

:::example{title="Patterns in the data"}
- The orbital period increases with distance from the Sun.
- The inner, rocky planets are much denser (about 4–5.5 g/cm³) than the outer, gaseous planets (about 0.7–1.6 g/cm³).
- Temperatures generally fall with distance from the Sun. Venus is the exception: its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps energy, making it hotter than Mercury.
- Surface gravitational field strength does not simply follow distance: it depends on each planet's mass and size.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Planets orbit the Earth"}
All the planets, including the Earth, orbit the Sun. Only the Moon (and artificial satellites) orbit the Earth.
:::

:::mistake{title="The Sun at the centre of every orbit"}
In an elliptical orbit the Sun is at a focus, off-centre. That is why comets come very close to the Sun and then travel very far away.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In data questions, describe a trend with both quantities ("as the distance from the Sun increases, the orbital period increases") and quote values from the table.
:::

:::exam-tip
For light travel times, keep distances in metres and use $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$; convert large times into minutes or hours.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Solar System is the Sun, eight planets, their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids and comets.
- Inner planets are small and rocky; outer planets are large and gaseous, explained by the accretion model.
- The Sun holds most of the mass; its gravity keeps everything in orbit; field strength depends on mass and falls with distance.
- Light travel time: $t = \dfrac{d}{c}$.
- Orbits are elliptical; bodies move fastest nearest the Sun (energy conservation); outer planets move more slowly (Cambridge Supplement).
:::
