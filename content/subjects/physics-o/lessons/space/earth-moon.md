## Introduction

Every morning the Sun rises in the east over the eastern highlands and sets in the west; every year the hot, wet summer gives way to the cool, dry winter; every month the Moon swells from a thin crescent to a full disc and back again. All three cycles come from the way the Earth and the Moon move. This lesson is Cambridge content (topic 6).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Axis"}
The imaginary line through the North and South Poles about which the Earth spins. It is tilted (by about 23.5°) relative to the plane of the Earth's orbit.
:::

:::definition{term="Orbit"}
The curved path of one body around another, such as the Earth around the Sun or the Moon around the Earth.
:::

:::definition{term="Phases of the Moon"}
The changing shapes of the lit part of the Moon seen from the Earth during each month.
:::

## Day and night

The Earth **spins on its tilted axis** once in about **24 hours**. The half of the Earth facing the Sun has **day**; the other half has **night**. Because the Earth spins from west to east, the Sun **appears** to rise in the east, move across the sky and set in the west, even though it is the Earth that is turning.

## The year and the seasons

The Earth **orbits the Sun** once in about **365 days** (one year). Its axis stays tilted in the same direction in space throughout the orbit, so:

- for part of the year the **southern hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun**: the Sun climbs higher in the sky, its rays strike the ground more directly (more energy on each square metre) and the days are longer, so it is **summer** in Zimbabwe (around December);
- six months later the southern hemisphere is tilted **away** from the Sun: the Sun is lower, its energy is spread over a larger area and the days are shorter, so it is **winter** (around June), while the northern hemisphere has summer.

The seasons are **not** caused by changes in the Earth's distance from the Sun; the Earth is actually slightly closer to the Sun in January than in July.

## The phases of the Moon

The Moon orbits the Earth about once a **month**. It gives out no light of its own; we see sunlight **reflected** from it. Half of the Moon is always lit by the Sun, but as it orbits, we see different amounts of that lit half:

1. **New moon**: the Moon is between the Earth and the Sun; its lit side faces away, so we see almost nothing.
2. **Waxing crescent**, then **first quarter** (half the disc lit), then **waxing gibbous**.
3. **Full moon**: the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon, and we see the whole lit face.
4. **Waning gibbous**, **last quarter** and **waning crescent**, back to new moon.

The whole cycle of phases takes about 29.5 days.

## Average orbital speed (Cambridge Supplement)

For a nearly circular orbit of radius $r$ completed in a time $T$, the distance travelled in one orbit is the circumference $2\pi r$.

:::formula{name="Average orbital speed"}
$$
v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}
$$
:::

:::worked-solution[The Moon's orbital speed]
The Moon orbits at an average distance of $3.84 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m}$ from the Earth's centre, taking $27.3$ days.

1. $T = 27.3 \times 24 \times 3600 = 2.36 \times 10^{6}\ \text{s}$.
2. $v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T} = \dfrac{2\pi \times 3.84 \times 10^{8}}{2.36 \times 10^{6}} = 1.02 \times 10^{3}\ \text{m/s}$, about $1\ \text{km/s}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[The Earth's orbital speed]
The Earth orbits the Sun at a distance of $1.5 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}$ in 365 days.

1. $T = 365 \times 24 \times 3600 = 3.15 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s}$.
2. $v = \dfrac{2\pi \times 1.5 \times 10^{11}}{3.15 \times 10^{7}} = 3.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{m/s}$, about $30\ \text{km/s}$.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Seasons come from distance"}
Summer happens when your hemisphere is tilted **towards** the Sun, not when the Earth is nearer the Sun.
:::

:::mistake{title="The Earth's shadow causes the phases"}
The phases come from seeing different amounts of the Moon's sunlit half. The Earth's shadow falls on the Moon only during a lunar eclipse.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In orbital speed calculations, convert the period into **seconds** and the radius into **metres** before using $v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T}$.
:::

:::exam-tip
When explaining the seasons, mention both the height of the Sun (energy more concentrated) and the length of the day.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Earth spins on its tilted axis once in about 24 hours, giving day and night and the Sun's apparent daily motion.
- The Earth orbits the Sun in about 365 days; the tilt of its axis causes the seasons.
- The Moon orbits the Earth in about a month; its phases come from seeing different amounts of its sunlit half.
- Average orbital speed: $v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ (Cambridge Supplement).
:::
