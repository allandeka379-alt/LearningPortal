## Introduction

On a moonless night in the Hwange bush, a pale band of light stretches across the sky: the **Milky Way**, our view along the disc of the galaxy we live in. Almost every star you can see with your eyes belongs to that galaxy, and each one is born, shines and dies over millions or billions of years.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Galaxy"}
An enormous group of many billions of stars, with gas and dust, held together by gravity.
:::

:::definition{term="Light-year"}
The distance travelled by light through space in one year. It is a unit of **distance**, not of time.
:::

## Galaxies and the Milky Way

- Galaxies contain **many billions of stars**.
- The Sun is one star in the **Milky Way** galaxy.
- Other stars are **much further away** than the Sun: even the nearest, Proxima Centauri, is about 4.2 light-years away, while the Sun is only about 8 light-minutes away.
- Because these distances are so large, astronomers measure them in **light-years**.

## How long is a light-year? (Cambridge Supplement)

:::worked-solution[One light-year in metres]
1. Seconds in a year: $365 \times 24 \times 3600 = 3.15 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s}$.
2. Distance $= vt = 3.0 \times 10^{8} \times 3.15 \times 10^{7} = 9.5 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[The distance to Proxima Centauri]
1. Distance $= 4.2 \times 9.5 \times 10^{15} = 4.0 \times 10^{16}\ \text{m}$.
2. Light leaving Proxima Centauri tonight will reach the Earth in about 4.2 years.
:::

## The life cycle of a star (Cambridge Supplement)

All stars begin the same way:

1. **Interstellar cloud**: a cloud of gas (mostly hydrogen) and dust begins to collapse under its own gravity.
2. **Protostar**: as the cloud collapses, it heats up and glows.
3. **Stable star**: when the core is hot enough, **hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium**. The outward pressure from the energy released balances the inward pull of gravity, and the star stays stable for most of its life.

What happens when the hydrogen in the core runs out depends on the star's **mass**:

| | Stars like the Sun (low mass) | Massive stars (much more than the Sun) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| next stage | expands into a **red giant** | expands into a **red supergiant** |
| what follows | throws off its outer layers as a planetary nebula; the core shrinks to a hot, dense **white dwarf**, which slowly cools | explodes as a **supernova** |
| what is left | a white dwarf | a **neutron star**, or for the most massive stars a **black hole** |

A supernova scatters elements made inside the star, including heavy elements, into space. That material forms new **nebulae**, from which new stars and planets can form. The iron in your blood and the calcium in your bones were made inside stars that died before the Sun formed.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="A light-year is a time"}
A light-year is the **distance** light travels in a year, about $9.5 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Every star becomes a black hole"}
Only the most massive stars become black holes. Stars like the Sun end as white dwarfs.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Learn the life-cycle stages in order and the branch point: after the red giant or supergiant, the path depends on the star's mass.
:::

:::exam-tip
When converting light-years to metres, multiply by $9.5 \times 10^{15}$ and give the answer in standard form.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Galaxies contain billions of stars; the Sun is in the Milky Way.
- Other stars are much further away than the Sun; distances are measured in light-years.
- One light-year is about $9.5 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m}$ (Cambridge Supplement).
- Stars form from collapsing clouds, become protostars, then stable stars fusing hydrogen into helium.
- Low-mass stars become red giants and then white dwarfs; massive stars become red supergiants, explode as supernovae and leave neutron stars or black holes (Cambridge Supplement).
:::
