## Introduction

Every physics result starts with a measurement. A carpenter in Mutare checking that a door frame is square, a nurse timing a patient's pulse and a learner timing a pendulum in the laboratory all need the same skills: choose the right instrument, read it correctly and reduce the uncertainty in the reading.

In this lesson you will learn how to measure lengths, volumes and times, and a trick that every examiner expects you to know: **measure many, then divide**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Precision"}
The smallest change an instrument can show, set by its smallest scale division. A metre rule marked in millimetres reads to the nearest $1\ \text{mm}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Zero error"}
A reading that an instrument shows when the true value is zero. It must be subtracted from (or added to) every reading.
:::

:::definition{term="Period"}
The time taken for one complete oscillation, for example one full swing of a pendulum there and back.
:::

## Measuring length

| Instrument | Typical range | Reads to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Metre rule | up to $1\ \text{m}$ | $1\ \text{mm}$ |
| Tape measure | several metres | $1\ \text{mm}$ |
| Vernier callipers | up to about $15\ \text{cm}$ | $0.1\ \text{mm}$ |
| Micrometer screw gauge | up to about $25\ \text{mm}$ | $0.01\ \text{mm}$ |

When you use a rule, put your eye directly above the mark. Looking from the side causes **parallax error**. Measure from a clear mark rather than the worn end of the rule.

### Vernier callipers and the micrometer (ZIMSEC)

A **Vernier calliper** has a main scale in millimetres and a sliding Vernier scale with ten divisions. Read the main scale just before the Vernier zero, then find the Vernier mark that lines up exactly with a main-scale mark: that gives the next decimal place.

![Vernier callipers: the Vernier zero is just past 4.1 cm on the main scale and the 6th Vernier mark lines up with a main-scale mark, so the length is 4.1 plus 0.06, which is 4.16 cm.](media:diagrams/vernier-reading-416.png "Figure: reading Vernier callipers, 4.16 cm")

A **micrometer screw gauge** has a sleeve scale in half-millimetres and a thimble with 50 divisions, each $0.01\ \text{mm}$. Add the sleeve reading to the thimble reading. Close the jaws gently using the ratchet so that the object is not squashed.

![Micrometer screw gauge: the sleeve shows 3.5 mm and the 42nd thimble division is on the datum line, each division being 0.01 mm, so the diameter is 3.5 plus 0.42, which is 3.92 mm.](media:diagrams/micrometer-reading-392.png "Figure: reading a micrometer screw gauge, 3.92 mm")

:::worked-solution[Reading a Vernier and a micrometer]
1. Vernier: the Vernier zero is just past $4.1\ \text{cm}$ on the main scale, and the 6th Vernier mark lines up with a main-scale mark.
2. So the length is $4.1 + 0.06 = 4.16\ \text{cm}$.
3. Micrometer: the sleeve shows $3.5\ \text{mm}$ and the thimble line at 42.
4. So the diameter is $3.5 + 0.42 = 3.92\ \text{mm}$.
:::

## Measuring volume

A **measuring cylinder** measures the volume of a liquid. Stand it on a level bench and read the bottom of the **meniscus** (the curved surface) with your eye level with it. Volumes are usually in $\text{cm}^3$ (millilitres): $1\ \text{cm}^3 = 1\ \text{ml}$ and $1000\ \text{cm}^3 = 1$ litre.

The volume of a **regular** solid comes from its measurements, for example a cuboid: length × width × height. The volume of an **irregular** solid that sinks is found by **displacement**: the rise in the water level in a measuring cylinder equals the volume of the object. You will use this in the density lesson.

## Measuring time

Stop-watches, stop-clocks and digital timers measure time intervals. A digital stop-watch may display hundredths of a second, but a person's **reaction time** (about $0.2\ \text{s}$ to $0.3\ \text{s}$) at the start and the end is far larger than that. Light gates connected to a timer remove reaction time altogether.

## Measure many, then divide

When something is too small or too quick to measure well on its own, measure many of them together and divide. The reaction-time error, or the rule's $1\ \text{mm}$ precision, is then shared out over many items, so the result is far more reliable.

:::worked-solution[The thickness of one page]
1. A stack of 400 pages from a textbook measures $2.8\ \text{cm}$ with a ruler.
2. Thickness of one page $= \dfrac{2.8\ \text{cm}}{400} = 0.007\ \text{cm} = 0.07\ \text{mm}$.
3. Measuring one page directly with the ruler would be impossible: it reads only to $1\ \text{mm}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[The period of a pendulum]
1. Start the stop-watch as the bob passes the centre of its swing, and count 20 complete oscillations.
2. The time for 20 oscillations is $28.6\ \text{s}$.
3. Period $T = \dfrac{28.6\ \text{s}}{20} = 1.43\ \text{s}$.
4. Repeat the timing and average the results; a reaction-time error of $0.2\ \text{s}$ is now only $0.01\ \text{s}$ per oscillation.
:::

Timing from the centre of the swing is better than timing from the end, because the bob moves fastest there and its position is easiest to judge.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Counting oscillations wrongly"}
Saying "one" as you start the watch gives one oscillation too many. Say "zero" at the start, then count each complete return to the same point in the same direction.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading the top of the meniscus"}
For water, read the bottom of the curved surface, with your eye level with it. Looking down from above makes the reading too high.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting a zero error"}
If a micrometer reads $0.02\ \text{mm}$ with its jaws closed, every reading is $0.02\ \text{mm}$ too big. Subtract it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Describe how to measure" questions earn marks for: the instrument, how it is read (eye level, meniscus, zero error), measuring many multiples, repeating and averaging, and the calculation (divide by the number of items).
:::

:::exam-tip
Quote readings to the precision of the instrument: a metre-rule reading is written as $63.0\ \text{cm}$, not $63\ \text{cm}$, when it is read to the nearest millimetre.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Choose an instrument whose precision suits the size of the measurement: rule ($1\ \text{mm}$), Vernier callipers ($0.1\ \text{mm}$), micrometer ($0.01\ \text{mm}$).
- Avoid parallax by reading scales with your eye directly in line; read the bottom of a meniscus at eye level.
- Check for and correct zero errors.
- Human reaction time limits stop-watch timing; light gates avoid it.
- For small lengths or short times, measure many multiples and divide; repeat and average.
:::
