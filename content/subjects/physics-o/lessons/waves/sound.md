## Introduction

The beat of a marimba band, the hum of a borehole pump, the crack of thunder during a summer storm: all sounds begin with something **vibrating**. The vibration pushes on the air around it and sends a wave through the air to your ears.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sound"}
A longitudinal wave produced by a vibrating source and passed on by the vibrations of the particles of a medium.
:::

:::definition{term="Compression"}
A region of a sound wave where the particles are closer together than normal (higher pressure).
:::

:::definition{term="Rarefaction"}
A region of a sound wave where the particles are further apart than normal (lower pressure).
:::

## How sound travels

A vibrating object, such as a drum skin or a loudspeaker cone, pushes the air particles next to it together (a **compression**) as it moves out and lets them spread apart (a **rarefaction**) as it moves back. These compressions and rarefactions travel outwards: sound is a **longitudinal** wave, because the particles vibrate along the direction the sound travels.

Sound needs a **medium** (a solid, liquid or gas). In the classic experiment, an electric bell rings inside a glass bell jar; as a vacuum pump removes the air, the sound fades away although the hammer can still be seen striking the bell. Sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

## Range and speed of sound

- Humans can hear frequencies from about **20 Hz to 20 000 Hz** (20 kHz).
- The speed of sound in air is about **330 to 350 m/s** (it is faster in warm air).
- In general sound travels **fastest in solids**, slower in liquids and slowest in gases (Cambridge Supplement), because the particles are closer together and more strongly linked.

## Measuring the speed of sound in air

Two learners stand a measured distance apart, for example $400\ \text{m}$ across a sports field, measured with a long tape. One makes a sudden sound that can also be seen, such as clapping two wooden blocks together above her head. The other starts a stop-watch when he **sees** the blocks hit (light arrives almost instantly) and stops it when he **hears** the sound. Repeat several times, average, and then calculate speed = distance ÷ time.

:::worked-solution[Calculating the speed of sound]
1. Distance $= 500\ \text{m}$; average time between seeing and hearing $= 1.5\ \text{s}$.
2. Speed $= \dfrac{500}{1.5} = 333\ \text{m/s}$.
:::

The same idea explains why you see lightning before you hear thunder. Light arrives almost at once, while sound covers about $1\ \text{km}$ every $3\ \text{s}$; counting the seconds between flash and bang gives the distance to the storm.

## Pitch, loudness and quality

- **Pitch** depends on **frequency**: a higher frequency gives a higher-pitched note.
- **Loudness** depends on **amplitude**: a larger amplitude gives a louder sound.
- **Quality** (timbre, ZIMSEC) is what makes the same note sound different on a guitar and a flute; it depends on the shape of the waveform.

On a cathode-ray oscilloscope (CRO) a microphone signal shows these clearly: more waves across the screen for a higher pitch, taller waves for a louder sound.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Sound travels through space"}
There is no medium in space, so sound cannot travel there. Explosions in space films would be silent.
:::

:::mistake{title="Louder means higher pitch"}
Loudness depends on amplitude; pitch depends on frequency. A loud note can be low-pitched.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In the speed-of-sound experiment, mention a large distance (to make the time measurable), timing from seeing to hearing, repeating and averaging, and the calculation.
:::

:::exam-tip
Describe sound with the words "longitudinal", "compressions" and "rarefactions"; examiners look for them.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sound is produced by vibrations and travels as a longitudinal wave of compressions and rarefactions.
- Sound needs a medium; it cannot travel through a vacuum.
- Human hearing: about 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz. Speed in air: about 330–350 m/s; faster in liquids and solids.
- The speed of sound is measured from a known distance and the time between seeing and hearing a sound.
- Higher frequency gives higher pitch; larger amplitude gives louder sound.
:::
