## Introduction

Ocean waves bend as they approach a beach and line up with the shore. A radio in a valley can still pick up a signal from behind a hill. A voice can be heard around the corner of a building. These are examples of three things all waves do: **reflect**, **refract** and **diffract**. A **ripple tank** lets us see them with water waves.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Reflection"}
The bouncing back of a wave from a surface. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
:::

:::definition{term="Refraction"}
The change in direction of a wave when it enters a different medium (or region) at an angle, caused by a change in its speed.
:::

:::definition{term="Diffraction"}
The spreading out of a wave as it passes through a gap or around the edge of an obstacle.
:::

## The ripple tank

A shallow glass-bottomed tray of water is lit from above so the shadows of the waves appear on a screen or the floor below. A vibrating bar makes straight (plane) wavefronts; a small dipper makes circular ones.

### Reflection at a plane surface

When plane wavefronts hit a straight barrier, they reflect: the reflected waves make the same angle with the barrier as the incoming ones. The wavelength, frequency and speed do not change.

### Refraction from a change of depth

Water waves travel **more slowly in shallow water**. A glass plate placed in the tank makes a shallow region. As waves pass from deep to shallow water:

![Water waves crossing from deep to shallow water at an angle: the wavefronts are closer together in the shallow region (wavelength 4.0 cm against 6.0 cm) and bend towards the normal, because the waves slow from 0.30 to 0.20 m/s while the frequency stays 5.0 Hz.](media:diagrams/wave-refraction-ripple-tank.png "Figure: waves slow down in shallow water, so the wavelength shortens and they bend")

- their **speed decreases**;
- their **wavelength decreases** (the wavefronts get closer together);
- their **frequency stays the same** (it is set by the source);
- if they arrive at an angle, their **direction changes**, bending towards the normal.

:::worked-solution[Waves slowing in shallow water]
Waves of frequency $5.0\ \text{Hz}$ travel at $0.30\ \text{m/s}$ in deep water and $0.20\ \text{m/s}$ in shallow water.

1. Deep water: $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f} = \dfrac{0.30}{5.0} = 0.060\ \text{m} = 6.0\ \text{cm}$.
2. Shallow water: $\lambda = \dfrac{0.20}{5.0} = 0.040\ \text{m} = 4.0\ \text{cm}$.
3. The frequency stays $5.0\ \text{Hz}$; the slower waves have a shorter wavelength.
:::

### Diffraction through a gap and around an edge

When plane waves pass through a gap in a barrier, they spread out beyond it into curved wavefronts. Waves also bend around the **edge** of an obstacle into the region behind it. The wavelength does not change.

## How much do waves diffract? (Cambridge Supplement)

- Diffraction through a gap is **greatest when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength**. A gap much wider than the wavelength lets the waves through almost straight, with only slight spreading at the edges.
- At an edge, **longer wavelengths diffract more**.

![Two ripple-tank sketches of plane waves meeting a barrier with a gap. When the gap is about one wavelength wide the waves spread out into almost semicircular wavefronts; when the gap is much wider than the wavelength the waves go through nearly straight, with slight bending only at the edges. The wavelength is unchanged.](media:diagrams/wave-diffraction-gap-size.png "Figure: diffraction is greatest when the gap is about one wavelength")

:::example{title="Hearing round corners"}
Sound waves have wavelengths of about a metre, similar to the width of a doorway, so sound diffracts strongly through doors and around buildings. Light has a wavelength of under a millionth of a metre, far smaller than a doorway, so light passes through almost straight: you can hear a person around a corner but not see them.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Frequency changes in refraction"}
The frequency is fixed by the source and never changes when a wave crosses into a new medium; the speed and wavelength change.
:::

:::mistake{title="Diffraction changes the wavelength"}
Diffraction changes the direction and shape of the wavefronts, not their wavelength.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When drawing refraction, keep the frequency the same by making the wavefronts closer together in the slower region, and bend them towards the normal when they slow down.
:::

:::exam-tip
When drawing diffraction, keep the wavelength (the spacing of the wavefronts) the same after the gap.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Waves reflect from a barrier with the angle of incidence equal to the angle of reflection.
- Waves refract when their speed changes: in shallow water they slow down, their wavelength shortens and they bend towards the normal.
- The frequency of a wave never changes when it moves into a new medium.
- Waves diffract (spread out) through gaps and around edges.
- Diffraction is greatest when the gap is similar to the wavelength; longer wavelengths diffract more at edges (Cambridge Supplement).
:::
