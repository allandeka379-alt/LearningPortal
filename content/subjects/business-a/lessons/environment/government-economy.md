## Introduction

Chipo Foods (a fictional processor in Bulawayo) borrowed US\$80,000 at a variable rate to buy a new packing line. Six months later the central bank raised interest rates, the government announced a training grant for firms that hire apprentices, and the rand weakened against the dollar. The first made the loan more costly, the second reduced Chipo's labour costs, and the third made imported packaging cheaper. The same firm, with no change in its own behaviour, found its position altered three times by decisions of government.

Business decisions are made against the background of the economy and government policy. The topic is tested in Cambridge Paper 3 and Paper 4 (A Level) and in ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2. It is not a Business Enterprise Skills topic. You should already know the main stakeholders and the political and legal influences on business.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Market failure"}
A situation in which free markets do not allocate resources efficiently, so that society's welfare is not maximised. The main causes are **externalities** (costs or benefits that fall on people outside the transaction, such as pollution), **public goods** (goods such as street lighting that the market will not supply because people cannot be charged) and **monopoly power**.
:::

:::definition{term="Macroeconomic objectives"}
The government's aims for the whole economy: **low unemployment**, **low and stable inflation** (a sustained rise in the general level of prices) and **economic growth** (a rise in the total output of goods and services).
:::

:::definition{term="Fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy"}
**Fiscal policy** changes government spending and taxation. **Monetary policy** changes interest rates or the supply of money. **Supply-side policy** aims to raise the productive capacity of the economy, for example through education, training and infrastructure.
:::

:::definition{term="Exchange rate"}
The price of one currency in terms of another, for example the number of rand received for one US dollar. A **weak** (depreciated) domestic currency makes exports cheaper and imports dearer for the home country; a **strong** (appreciated) currency does the opposite.
:::

## How a government helps business

- **Grants and subsidies** to start up or to produce specific goods.
- **Tax breaks** such as lower tax for new firms or for investment in a priority region.
- **Training and education** programmes that supply skilled labour.
- **Infrastructure** such as roads, power and water, which cut firms' costs.
- **Advice and credit schemes** for small enterprises, and **government contracts**.

## How a government constrains business

- **Taxes** on profit, sales and employment raise costs and reduce spending.
- **Regulation** of safety, the environment and labour.
- **Price controls** that cap or set prices.
- **Licensing and quotas** that limit entry or amounts produced or traded.

## Market failure in outline

When a market fails, governments intervene. For **externalities** (pollution) they may tax the polluter or regulate; for **public goods** the state supplies the good itself; for **monopoly power** they use competition law and may regulate prices. These actions change the costs and opportunities of the businesses concerned.

## The economy and business

| Condition | Typical effect on business |
| --- | --- |
| Economic growth | Rising incomes, demand and profits; more investment |
| Recession | Falling demand; pressure on survival; redundancies |
| High unemployment | Plenty of labour but low spending |
| High inflation | Rising costs and uncertainty; difficulty planning prices and wages |

## Policies and their effects

| Policy | Example of change | Likely effect on business |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monetary | Higher interest rates | Costlier loans, lower borrowing and spending, lower demand for houses, cars and machinery |
| Fiscal | Higher income tax or VAT | Lower spending, lower sales; higher costs if the tax falls on firms |
| Fiscal | More spending on roads | More contracts for builders; lower transport costs for others |
| Supply-side | Funding for training | A more skilled workforce; lower recruitment costs |
| Exchange rate | The domestic currency weakens | Exports cheaper abroad (higher sales), imports dearer (higher costs for importers) |

The size of the effect depends on how dependent the firm is on borrowing, imports or exports, the type of product (luxuries are more sensitive than necessities) and the time period, because some effects are delayed.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: interest rates and exchange rates]
Chipo Foods has a variable-rate loan of US\$80,000, and the rate rises from 9% to 11%. Separately, the firm imports a packaging machine priced at R36,000 (rand, an assumed price). The rand weakens from R18 to R20 for US\$1. (All rates are assumed for practice.)

| Bank loan of US\$80,000 | Interest rate (%) | Yearly interest (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Before the rise | 9 | 7,200 |
| After the rise | 11 | 8,800 |
| Increase | 2 | 1,600 |

| Imported machine priced at R36,000 | Rand per US\$1 | Cost in US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Before | 18 | 2,000 |
| After the rand weakens | 20 | 1,800 |
| Change | 2 | (200) |

**Working.** Yearly interest = loan × rate: 80,000 × 9% = US\$7,200 and 80,000 × 11% = US\$8,800, a rise of US\$1,600 (22.2% more interest). The machine's cost in dollars is the rand price ÷ the rate: 36,000 ÷ 18 = US\$2,000, and 36,000 ÷ 20 = US\$1,800, a saving of US\$200.

**Interpretation.** The interest-rate rise increases the firm's costs, while the weaker rand lowers the cost of imports. A firm that sells goods to South Africa at a dollar price would see the opposite: an item priced at US\$500 costs R9,000 at R18 but R10,000 at R20, so it becomes dearer for South African buyers and sales may fall. In an evaluation, state which change matters more to this firm and why.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, a government subsidy and tax change]
**Context.** The government of a fictional country gives a subsidy for every tonne of fertiliser produced and at the same time raises the sales tax on imported tractors. Rudo's Fertiliser (a fictional firm in Gweru) and Dube Farm Machinery (an importer in Mutare) are both affected.

**Point.** The subsidy helps Rudo's, while the tax constrains Dube.

**Application.** Rudo's costs per tonne fall, so it can lower prices, make more profit or both. Dube's tractors become dearer, so farmers may buy fewer or buy second-hand machines.

**Analysis.** More sales of fertiliser need more workers and transport, helping other firms. For Dube, reduced sales mean lower revenue and possible job losses. If farmers also face higher fertiliser prices because the subsidy ends, the gain would be temporary.

**Judgement.** The policy is good for fertiliser producers in the short term. Dube should seek other products or local assembly to reduce its reliance on imports, because the tax is unlikely to be reversed soon. Whether the subsidy lasts depends on government finances.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing fiscal and monetary policy"}
Fiscal policy uses taxes and government spending. Monetary policy uses interest rates and the money supply. Name the policy correctly before explaining the effect.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a weak currency is always bad"}
A weaker currency makes exports cheaper and benefits exporters, while it raises the cost of imports. Say which kind of firm is affected.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating all firms in the same way"}
A rise in interest rates hurts a firm with big loans much more than one with no debt. Use the case to decide who is affected most.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
Cambridge Paper 3 and Paper 4 (A Level): short calculations or explanations on one policy and extended evaluation of the impact of policies on a case. ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2: help and constraints, and the effect of interest rates, taxes and exchange rates in structured questions and essays.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/identify*: name the policy and the group affected. *Explain*: link the policy to costs or demand for this firm. *Analyse*: follow the chain (higher interest rate, higher loan cost, lower profit, less investment). *Evaluate/discuss*: judge how big the effect is for this firm, over what period, and whether the firm can respond.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Governments help business through grants, subsidies, tax breaks, training and infrastructure, and constrain it through taxes, regulation, price controls, licences and quotas.
- Market failure (externalities, public goods, monopoly power) is a reason for intervention.
- The three main macroeconomic objectives are low unemployment, low inflation and economic growth.
- Monetary policy changes interest rates; fiscal policy changes tax and spending; supply-side policy raises capacity; exchange rate changes alter export and import prices.
- The effect on a firm depends on its borrowing, trade links, product type and the time period.
:::
