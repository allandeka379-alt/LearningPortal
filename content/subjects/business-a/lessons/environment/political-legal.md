## Introduction

Kudzai Transport (a fictional bus operator in Harare) was planning to buy ten more buses when the government announced stricter vehicle-safety inspections and a higher minimum pay for drivers. Overnight the cost of running each bus changed, some older vehicles could no longer be used, and the plan had to be rethought. Kudzai could not alter the rules; it could only adapt.

Governments set the rules within which every business operates, so political and legal factors form part of the external environment in nearly every case study. They are tested in Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 (A Level), and in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 as part of the business environment and the laws affecting enterprises. You should already know the aims of a business and the idea of a stakeholder.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Political and legal environment"}
The government policies, laws and regulations, and the stability of political conditions, that influence how a business operates and what it may do.
:::

:::definition{term="Legislation and regulation"}
**Legislation** is law passed by the government; **regulation** is the rules and supervision, often by a public body, that make sure businesses obey it.
:::

:::definition{term="Consumer protection"}
Laws and bodies that protect buyers from unsafe goods, false descriptions, misleading advertising and unfair contract terms.
:::

:::definition{term="Competition policy"}
Government action to prevent a firm or group of firms from harming consumers through monopoly power, price-fixing or mergers that remove competition.
:::

## How the law controls business

| Area | What the law may require | Effect on business |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Employment practices | Fair recruitment, written terms, no unfair dismissal, no discrimination | Time and cost spent on procedures; fewer disputes; wider hiring pool |
| Conditions of work, including health and safety | Safe equipment, protective clothing, limits on hours, rest breaks | Higher costs, but fewer accidents and less absence |
| Wage levels | A minimum wage; rules on overtime and pay | Higher wage bill; may raise prices or cut jobs |
| Marketing behaviour | True descriptions, honest advertising, safe products, clear labels | Careful claims; costs of testing and labelling; consumer trust |
| Competition | Controls on monopolies, price-fixing and mergers | Limits on growth by takeover; lower prices for consumers |
| Location | Planning permission, zoning, environmental limits | Restricts where factories or shops can be built; may push firms to other areas |
| Particular goods and services | Licences or limits on alcohol, tobacco, medicines, firearms | Extra licence costs; limits on sales and advertising |

## Impact of political change

A change of government policy, new legislation, or political instability alters the conditions for decision-making. Examples:

- **Stability** encourages investment because firms can plan; instability, policy reversals or uncertainty about property rights discourage it.
- **New laws** raise or lower costs (for example, stricter safety rules or tax changes).
- **Trade and investment policy** may open or close markets.
- **Public-sector spending** decisions create or remove customers (for example, contracts to build roads or clinics).

The size of the impact depends on how large the change is, how quickly it must be obeyed, how dependent the firm is on the affected area and whether it can pass the cost to customers.

## Legal requirements for starting and running an enterprise (ZIMSEC outline)

An enterprise normally has to **register** its business or company, obtain **licences** for its activity and premises, register for and pay **tax**, follow **labour law** when employing people, and obey **consumer law** when selling. Failure to comply can bring fines, closure or loss of reputation. Compliance costs fall more heavily on small firms, which have fewer staff to handle paperwork.

## Responding to legal and political change

Businesses respond by monitoring proposed changes, adapting products and processes, training staff, lobbying through trade associations, passing costs on in prices, or moving operations. Well-prepared firms turn compliance into a selling point (for example, advertising their safety standards).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the cost of a minimum wage increase]
Kudzai's bus company (a fictional firm) employs 25 drivers and conductors on the legal minimum of US\$150 a month. The government raises the minimum to US\$180 a month. The firm's yearly revenue is US\$360,000 and its profit before the change is US\$30,000. (The figures are invented for practice.)

| | Before the rise (US\$) | After the rise (US\$) | Change (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Monthly pay per worker | 150 | 180 | 30 |
| Yearly wage bill (25 workers) | 45,000 | 54,000 | 9,000 |
| Yearly profit (other things equal) | 30,000 | 21,000 | (9,000) |

**Working.** Yearly wage bill before = 25 × 150 × 12 = US\$45,000; after = 25 × 180 × 12 = US\$54,000. The extra cost is US\$9,000, which is 20% of the old wage bill (9,000 ÷ 45,000 × 100) and 30% of the old profit (9,000 ÷ 30,000 × 100). It equals 2.5% of revenue (9,000 ÷ 360,000 × 100).

**Interpretation.** A 20% rise in pay costs reduces profit by 30%. To restore profit, the firm could raise fares by about 2.5% on average if customers do not respond, cut jobs or work longer routes. The effect on profit is larger than the rise in the wage bill because profit is a small share of revenue.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, advertising and competition law]
**Context.** Sweet Valley Dairies (a fictional firm in Mutare) advertises its yoghurt as "100% natural" although it contains artificial flavouring. At the same time it proposes to take over the only other dairy in the province.

**Point.** The firm may face action under consumer-protection and competition law, and the effect on decisions is serious.

**Application.** A false description can lead to fines, a ban on the advertisement and refunds. A takeover that would leave one dairy supplying the whole province may be referred to the competition authority, which may block it or set conditions.

**Analysis.** Correcting the advertising costs money and may reduce sales in the short run, but continuing could harm trust and cause a recall. If the takeover is blocked, the firm loses the expected economies of scale and must find growth elsewhere, for example by improving its range or exporting.

**Judgement.** Sweet Valley should correct the advertising now and seek legal advice before bidding. The takeover could still go ahead if it can show benefits to consumers, such as lower prices, but the likelihood of approval is low when the merged firm would have no local competitor.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Naming laws you are not sure of"}
Describe what the law does (for example, "labour legislation sets a minimum wage") and do not invent the name or year of an Act. A wrong name earns no credit; an accurate description does.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that law only increases costs"}
Laws also create benefits: safer workplaces, trusted products and fairer competition. Weigh costs against benefits, and say who gains and who loses.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the size of the firm"}
A new law has a bigger relative effect on a small firm with few staff than on a large one with a legal department. Refer to the size and type of the firm in the case.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
Cambridge Paper 3 and Paper 4 (A Level): short case questions on one law and extended answers evaluating the impact of a legal or political change. ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2: structured questions on the laws that govern enterprises and essays on how the environment affects business.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/identify*: name the law or its aim. *Explain/describe*: say what the law requires and one effect on a business. *Analyse*: follow cause and effect (law, cost, price, demand). *Assess/evaluate/discuss*: weigh costs against benefits, consider the size of the firm and the time period, and give a justified judgement.
:::

:::summary
- The government uses law to control employment, working conditions, wages, marketing, competition, location and particular goods.

- Political stability and policy changes affect investment, costs and demand.
