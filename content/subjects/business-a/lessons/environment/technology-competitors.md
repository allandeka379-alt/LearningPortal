## Introduction

Zuva Printers (a fictional firm in Harare) printed school exercise books and business forms for twenty years. Then a competitor bought a digital press that prints short runs in a day, a mobile-money payment platform made it easy for schools to pay online, and the paper supplier, the only one in the city, raised its prices by 12%. Each of the three forces, technology, competitors and suppliers, reached Zuva from outside, and each required a decision.

These influences are tested in Cambridge Paper 3 and Paper 4 (A Level) and in ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 (Business Enterprise Skills does not examine this topic). You should already know the political and legal influences on business, and the ideas of cost and competition. Porter's five forces, a framework that includes competitors and suppliers, is covered in the lesson on environmental scanning; here the focus is on their impact on decisions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Technological change"}
The introduction of new or improved machines, processes, products and ways of communicating, such as automation, the internet, mobile technology and e-commerce.
:::

:::definition{term="Automation"}
The use of machines or computer-controlled systems to do work that people previously did, which usually raises output and consistency and reduces the need for labour in routine tasks.
:::

:::definition{term="Price and non-price competition"}
**Price competition** is competing by charging lower prices or offering discounts. **Non-price competition** is competing by quality, branding, service, design, convenience and promotion.
:::

:::definition{term="Supplier power"}
The ability of a supplier to influence the terms on which it sells to a business, such as price, delivery and credit period. It is strong when there are few suppliers, when the goods are unique or switching to another supplier is costly.
:::

## Technological change

**New products and processes.** Technology creates new goods (smartphones, solar panels) and new ways of producing and selling them (automation, online shops, mobile payments).

**Benefits to business:**

- Lower unit costs, higher output and more consistent quality.
- Faster communication and wider markets through e-commerce.
- New products that open markets.

**Costs and risks:**

- High cost of buying, installing and maintaining equipment.
- Retraining of staff and possible redundancies, with effects on morale.
- Rapid change makes equipment obsolete; firms that do not keep up lose customers.
- Dependence on power supply, the internet and skilled maintenance.

The impact of technology on any business depends on its size, the nature of the product, the speed of change in the industry and the skills of the workforce. A small firm may lack the finance for new equipment but may find e-commerce a cheap way to reach new customers.

## Competitors

The actions of competitors affect a firm's prices, output and marketing. A firm should consider:

- **Market structure**: how many firms there are and how large they are. In a market dominated by one or two firms, their decisions strongly influence everyone else; in a market with many small firms, each firm is a price-taker.
- **Competitive reactions**: if one firm cuts prices or launches a new product, rivals may copy it, so the gain may be short-lived. A price war can lower profits for all.
- **Price and non-price competition**: firms with a cost advantage may use price; others compete by quality, service and branding.
- **New entrants and substitutes**: easy entry or close substitutes limit a firm's pricing freedom.

## Suppliers

Suppliers affect costs, quality and reliability. The **bargaining power** of a supplier is high when:

- there are few suppliers or only one;
- the inputs are unique or difficult to replace;
- the supplier could sell to many other buyers, while the business buys only a small amount;
- switching supplier would be costly.

A business may respond by building good relationships, using several suppliers, keeping a larger inventory, negotiating long-term contracts, or vertical integration (see the lesson on mergers and takeovers). Unreliable deliveries disrupt production; a close relationship may bring discounts, credit and early notice of problems, but it can also make the firm dependent.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the impact of automation on costs and jobs]
Zuva Printers packs its exercise books by hand. Six workers are paid US\$300 a month each. A packing machine costs US\$30,000, would need one operator at US\$300 a month and would cost US\$4,800 a year to run. The firm packs 48,000 books a year. (Figures are invented for practice.)

| Yearly packing costs | Manual line (US\$) | Machine line (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Wages (6 workers; 1 operator) | 21,600 | 3,600 |
| Running costs of the machine | 0 | 4,800 |
| Total yearly cost | 21,600 | 8,400 |
| Yearly saving | | 13,200 |

**Working.** Wages before = 6 × 300 × 12 = US\$21,600; after = 1 × 300 × 12 = US\$3,600. Total machine-line cost = 3,600 + 4,800 = US\$8,400. Yearly saving = 21,600 − 8,400 = US\$13,200. Cost per book falls from 21,600 ÷ 48,000 = US\$0.45 to 8,400 ÷ 48,000 = US\$0.175 (17.5 cents). The machine's cost is repaid in 30,000 ÷ 13,200 = 2.27 years.

**Interpretation.** Automation cuts the cost of packing by about 61% (13,200 ÷ 21,600) and repays the machine in about two and a quarter years, but five workers lose their jobs. If Zuva retrains them for the digital press or for sales, the firm keeps skills and goodwill; if it dismisses them, morale in the rest of the workforce may fall.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, supplier power and a competitor's reaction]
**Context.** Zuva Printers buys all its paper from one supplier, which raises prices by 12%. A rival, Mhofu Print, has just bought a digital press and is offering low prices on short runs of school exercise books.

**Point.** Zuva faces strong supplier power and an aggressive competitor at once.

**Application.** With only one local supplier of paper, Zuva has little bargaining power and must pay the higher price or search for imports. Mhofu's low price means that if Zuva raises its prices to cover the cost of paper, it may lose school customers.

**Analysis.** Paper is a large part of the cost of printing, so a 12% rise squeezes the profit margin. If Zuva absorbs the rise, profit falls; if it passes the rise on, demand may fall. Finding a second supplier would strengthen Zuva's position and reduce dependence, but imports could involve delays and exchange-rate risk. Investing in its own digital press would help Zuva to compete on service and speed instead of price alone.

**Judgement.** Zuva should find a second supplier and compete on speed and quality for schools that value reliability, while holding prices as long as it can. A price war with a rival using newer technology is unlikely to be won on price alone.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying technology always reduces costs and jobs"}
Technology can create new jobs (maintenance, programming, online sales) as well as remove routine ones, and the high capital cost may outweigh savings in the short run. Weigh both effects.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating all competitors in the same way"}
A large rival with deep pockets can react very differently from a small one. Use the market structure in the case to judge the likely reaction.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that suppliers can be powerful"}
Many answers treat suppliers as passive. A sole supplier of a vital input has a lot of power over price and delivery; discuss how the firm can respond.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
Cambridge Paper 3 and Paper 4 (A Level): a short case question on one effect of technology or a competitor's action, and evaluation of how a firm should respond. ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2: structured questions on the effects of new technology, and essays on competition and supplier relations.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/identify*: name the technology, the competitor action or the supplier issue. *Explain*: link it to costs, quality or sales in the case. *Analyse*: build a chain (new machine, lower unit cost, lower price, more sales). *Assess/evaluate/discuss*: weigh the gain against cost and risk, consider the size of the firm and the time period, and give a justified decision.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Technological change brings new products and processes; benefits are lower costs, higher quality and wider markets, but costs include finance, retraining and redundancy.
- Competitors influence prices, output and marketing; their reactions and market structure shape the outcome.
- Suppliers affect cost, quality and reliability; their power is high where there are few of them or switching is costly.
- Firms can respond by investing, differentiating, building relationships or using several suppliers.
- The size of the impact depends on the size of the firm, the pace of change and how well managers adapt.
:::
