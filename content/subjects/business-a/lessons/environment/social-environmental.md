## Introduction

Kariba Fish Farm (a fictional business) sells tilapia to hotels and shops. When a local newspaper reports that waste water from its ponds is turning the shore green, residents complain, a fishing co-operative threatens to protest, and two hotels ask whether the farm can prove that its fish are raised responsibly. At the same time the town is ageing: more retired people are moving in, who buy smaller quantities, prefer delivery and ask for soft, boneless fillets. The farm's success now depends on its response to people and to nature, not only to prices and costs.

Social, demographic and environmental influences are part of the external environment tested in Cambridge Paper 3 and Paper 4 (A Level). This topic is Cambridge only: it is not a separate ZIMSEC heading, although ZIMSEC students meet its ideas in the lessons on stakeholders, objectives and ethics. You should already know the idea of a stakeholder and the triple bottom line.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Corporate social responsibility (CSR)"}
The idea that a business should take account of the effects of its decisions on society and the environment as well as on its owners, and should act responsibly towards workers, customers, the community and the planet, going beyond what the law requires.
:::

:::definition{term="Social audit and environmental audit"}
A **social audit** is an independent review of a firm's impact on its workers, customers and community compared with its stated aims. An **environmental audit** measures and reports the firm's impact on the physical environment (energy use, waste, emissions, water) and checks that it meets legal and company standards.
:::

:::definition{term="Demographic change"}
A change in the size and make-up of a population, such as its age structure (an **ageing population**), **urbanisation** (movement to towns), **migration** and **population growth**.
:::

:::definition{term="Sustainability"}
Meeting present needs without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs: using resources, energy and land in a way that can continue.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Cambridge detail"}
This lesson is Cambridge A Level only. ZIMSEC students will find related material in the lessons on aims and objectives (triple bottom line, CSR) and on stakeholders (community, pressure groups). The Cambridge syllabus asks for an assessment of CSR issues, the needs of the community and pressure groups, demographic change locally, nationally and globally, physical environmental issues, environmental audits and the growing importance of sustainability.
:::

## Corporate social responsibility and its issues

A socially responsible firm treats employees fairly, deals honestly with customers and suppliers, supports the community and limits environmental harm. Issues that arise include:

- **Accounting practices**: presenting accounts honestly, not hiding losses or inflating profits to mislead investors.
- **Paying incentives to win contracts**: gifts or payments to officials to gain business are bribery. They are illegal and unethical, although some firms argue that "everybody does it".
- **Treatment of workers and suppliers**: fair pay, safe conditions, and not forcing suppliers into unfair terms.
- **Social audits** can check whether the firm is meeting its claims; they build trust but add cost and can reveal embarrassing facts.

Benefits of CSR include a better reputation, customer loyalty and fewer conflicts; costs include higher expenses, which may reduce short-term profit or raise prices. Some firms use CSR as a marketing tool without real change; critics call this "greenwashing".

## The community and pressure groups

The community provides workers and customers and gives a firm its "licence to operate". Local residents care about jobs, noise, dust and traffic. **Pressure groups** campaign to change a firm's behaviour by publicity, petitions, boycotts and lobbying. A firm that consults the community early, creates jobs and supports local projects reduces the risk of conflict. The more a firm depends on local goodwill (a lodge in a tourist area, say), the more important the community is.

## Demographic change

| Change | Possible effect on business |
| --- | --- |
| Ageing population | More demand for health care, pharmacies, retirement housing and leisure; smaller labour force; pension costs |
| Urbanisation | Larger urban markets and labour pools; pressure on housing and transport; fewer farm workers |
| Migration | New customers and skills; language and cultural needs in marketing; remittances |
| Population growth | More customers and workers; pressure on resources and services |
| Fall in birth rate | Less demand for baby products and schools; later, fewer new workers |

Businesses must decide how far to adapt products, prices and promotion to these trends and how quickly.

## Physical environmental issues

Pollution of air, water and land, depletion of resources (timber, fish stocks, minerals), waste and climate change affect firms in two ways. They bring **costs** (clean-up, fines, higher prices for scarce inputs, damage to assets) and **opportunities** (recycling, renewable energy, efficient processes and products labelled as sustainable). Customers and governments increasingly expect firms to reduce their environmental footprint, and firms that are slow may lose customers or face regulation.

An **environmental audit** helps the firm to measure its performance, find waste, show customers and regulators that it takes the issue seriously, and plan improvements. Its limits are cost, the need for expert staff and the fact that an audit does not itself reduce harm unless the firm acts on the findings.

## Sustainability in decision-making

Sustainability is increasingly part of strategy. It affects choices of materials, location, technology and suppliers. A firm may accept a higher cost in the short run (cleaner equipment) in return for lower risk, a better reputation and, eventually, savings from efficiency. Whether sustainability pays depends on the industry, the attitude of customers, the cost of the measures and the strength of regulation.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: demographic change and the market for a pharmacy]
The fictional town of Mutoko Hills has a population that is expected to grow and age. A pharmacy owner wants to estimate the change in demand. (Figures are invented for practice.)

| Town of Mutoko Hills (fictional) | Now | In ten years | Change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Total population | 120,000 | 128,000 | 8,000 |
| Share aged 60 or over (%) | 15 | 18 | 3 |
| People aged 60 or over | 18,000 | 23,040 | 5,040 |
| Other residents | 102,000 | 104,960 | 2,960 |

**Working.** People aged 60 or over = population × share: 120,000 × 15% = 18,000 now and 128,000 × 18% = 23,040 in ten years, a rise of 5,040 or 28.0%. The other residents rise only by 2,960, or 2.9%. If each older person spends US\$60 a year in the pharmacy, spending by this group rises from 18,000 × 60 = US\$1,080,000 to 23,040 × 60 = US\$1,382,400, an increase of US\$302,400.

**Interpretation.** The older age group grows almost ten times faster in percentage terms than the rest of the population, so demand for medicines and health products will grow faster than the town as a whole. The pharmacy should consider a delivery service, easier access and larger-print labels. The forecast depends on the assumptions about population, and the spending per person may change.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, pollution and an environmental audit]
**Context.** Kariba Fish Farm discharges waste water into the lake. Residents complain, a pressure group calls for a boycott, and two hotels ask for proof of responsible practice.

**Point.** The farm should commission an environmental audit and act on its results.

**Application.** The audit would measure the nutrients in the waste water and show which ponds cause most of the harm. The farm could then install settling tanks, reduce feed waste or recycle water for irrigation of nearby gardens.

**Analysis.** The costs of tanks and monitoring will reduce profit in the short term. However, without action, the farm risks a fine, loss of the two hotel contracts and a damaged reputation that could last for years. A published audit can be used in marketing to show the hotels that the fish are responsibly grown, and savings from less wasted feed will partly offset the cost.

**Judgement.** The farm should accept the cost, because the long-term risks of ignoring the issue are greater than the short-term expense. The audit is worthwhile only if the farm acts on its findings and reports honestly; otherwise it would be accused of greenwashing.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating CSR as pure charity"}
CSR is about the way the firm does business (treatment of workers, honesty, environmental care), not only donations. Donations alone do not make a firm socially responsible.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing trends without effects"}
Writing "the population is ageing" earns little. Say how it affects demand, the labour force or costs of this firm, and how the firm should respond.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming sustainability always raises costs"}
Sustainable practices can cut costs (less energy and waste) as well as add them. Weigh both and refer to time: costs come first, savings later.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
Cambridge Paper 3 (A Level): short case questions on one issue (a pressure group, a demographic change, an audit) and the 12-mark evaluation on a firm's response to social and environmental pressures. Paper 4: essays that assess the importance of CSR or sustainability to a business.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define/identify*: name the concept, such as CSR or an environmental audit. *Explain/analyse*: follow the effect of the trend on this firm (customers, costs, labour, reputation). *Assess/evaluate/justify*: weigh the costs against the benefits, consider the size of the firm and the time period, and give a reasoned recommendation with a condition.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- CSR means taking account of society and the environment as well as owners; issues include honest accounting, bribery and the treatment of workers.
- The community and pressure groups can influence a firm through publicity, boycotts and lobbying.
- Demographic changes (ageing, urbanisation, migration, population growth) alter demand and the labour supply.
- Environmental issues bring costs and opportunities; audits measure and report impact but need action to matter.
- Sustainability is increasingly central to strategy: short-term costs may be offset by savings, reputation and lower risk.
:::
