## Introduction

Tendai Moyo (a fictional owner) runs a small bakery in Chinhoyi. In its first year the only aim was to stay open: the bakery had to cover its flour, wages and rent. By its fourth year the bakery was making a profit and Tendai wanted to open a second outlet and sponsor a local school feeding scheme. The goals of the business had changed, and so had the way Tendai judged success.

Business objectives are central to the exam because every later decision, from pricing to recruitment, is judged against them. The topic is examined in Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS Level), ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2. Before this lesson you should know the main ownership types of business; here you learn what each is trying to achieve.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mission statement"}
A short statement of the overall purpose of an organisation and the values that guide it, describing what it is for and whom it serves.
:::

:::definition{term="Aim and objective"}
An **aim** is a broad, long-term goal of the business. An **objective** is a more specific, measurable target that helps the business to achieve its aims, normally within a stated time.
:::

:::definition{term="Strategy and tactics"}
A **strategy** is the long-term plan of how the objectives will be achieved. **Tactics** are the short-term actions and decisions that carry out the strategy.
:::

:::definition{term="Triple bottom line"}
Judging a business on three results instead of one: **economic** (profit), **social** (people and community) and **environmental** (impact on the planet). **Corporate social responsibility (CSR)** is the duty a firm accepts to act in the interests of society as well as its owners.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}

ZIMSEC distinguishes objectives by level: **strategic** (long-term, set by directors), **tactical** (medium-term, set by middle managers) and **operational** (day-to-day, set by supervisors). Cambridge uses the hierarchy from mission to tactics. The two hierarchies describe the same idea of objectives that cascade down the organisation. Constraints on objectives are ZIMSEC detail.
:::

## The hierarchy of goals

1. **Mission statement**: why the business exists.
2. **Aims**: broad, long-term goals (for example, to be the leading bakery in the district).
3. **Objectives**: specific and measurable (for example, to raise yearly sales by 15% within two years).
4. **Strategy**: the plan to reach the objectives (for example, open two new outlets).
5. **Tactics**: the immediate actions (for example, hire a manager for the new outlet this month).

In ZIMSEC terms the second and third steps are strategic objectives; the tactical objectives are those of departments, and the operational ones of teams or individuals.

## Why objectives matter

Objectives give direction and unity, provide a standard against which performance is measured, help managers delegate and motivate staff, and allow stakeholders such as lenders to judge the business. Good objectives are clear, measurable, realistic and communicated to those who must carry them out.

## Common objectives

| Objective | Meaning | Typical user |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Survival | Stay in business by covering costs | New and small firms, firms in recession |
| Profit | Earn revenue greater than costs; **profit maximisation** means the highest possible profit | Private firms, owners and shareholders |
| Growth | Higher sales, output or more branches | Ambitious firms; managers seeking status |
| Market share | Larger percentage of total market sales | Firms in competitive markets |
| Social and environmental | Benefit the community or reduce harm to the environment | Social enterprises, CSR-minded firms |
| Service | Provide a good-quality service to the public | Public-sector bodies such as clinics and schools |

**Public-sector** organisations are owned by the state and usually aim to provide services (health, education, water) at an acceptable cost. **Social enterprises** trade to meet a social or environmental aim and reinvest most profits in it. **Private-sector** firms aim chiefly at profit, survival and growth, though many accept social goals. Profit maximisation is rare in practice because owners often accept satisfactory profit while serving other aims, and because the information needed to find the exact maximum is not available.

## Corporate social responsibility and the triple bottom line

A business that follows the triple bottom line measures what it does for the economy, the community and the environment. Benefits of CSR include a better public image, loyal customers, easier recruitment and fewer conflicts with pressure groups. Costs include higher expenses, which may reduce profit or raise prices, and the risk of being accused of "greenwashing" if claims are not true.

## Conflicts between objectives and constraints

Objectives often conflict. Cutting prices to increase market share may lower profit; keeping a loss-making clinic open serves the community but harms the budget; a rapid growth target may weaken quality. Managers must **prioritise**.

ZIMSEC students must also know the **constraints** that limit what a firm can achieve: shortage of finance, laws and regulations, competition, the demands of stakeholders, and the resources (skills, equipment, time) available.

## Why objectives change

Objectives change because the business moves through stages (survival, then profit, then growth), because of external change (recession, new competitors, new laws or technology), because the owners or managers change, and because performance falls short or exceeds targets.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: case analysis, the hierarchy in a business]
**Context.** Nyaradzo Crafts (a fictional co-operative in Victoria Falls) makes carved wooden items for tourists.

**Point.** The co-operative's goals can be sorted into a hierarchy.

**Application.** Its mission is "to give local families a fair income from our heritage skills". Its aim is "to be the best-known craft brand in the region". Its objective is "to raise yearly sales by 20% in two years". Its strategy is to sell through two lodges and a website, and its tactic this month is to photograph products for the website.

**Analysis.** Each level supports the one above it: the tactic (photographs) enables the strategy (selling online), which delivers the objective (20% growth) and moves towards the aim (best-known brand) in keeping with the mission (fair income). Only the objective is measurable and time-limited.

**Judgement.** The hierarchy is sound because every action links to the mission. The danger is that a growth objective might pressure the carvers to hurry, hurting quality and the fair-income mission, so the co-operative should state a quality standard alongside the growth target.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, a conflict of objectives]
**Context.** Mutare Water Services (a fictional company) must choose between two plans for the year: Plan X, which keeps tariffs low and extends the pipe network to a poor suburb; and Plan Y, which raises tariffs by 25% and cuts the extension.

**Point.** The plans reflect different objectives: Plan X favours the social objective of service, Plan Y the economic objective of profit.

**Application.** The company is owned by the council, so its main purpose is service to residents, but it must still cover its costs. Plan X will reduce profit in the short run; Plan Y will provide cash for repairs but make water less affordable.

**Analysis.** Higher tariffs reduce access for low-income households, which could damage the company's reputation and public health. Lower income under Plan X could lead to poor maintenance later, which would reduce service quality, so even the social objective depends on the financial one.

**Judgement.** A balanced choice is best: extend the network, but raise tariffs modestly and phase them in. The company should rank service first because it is publicly owned, subject to the constraint that it must cover its running costs.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating aims and objectives as the same"}
An aim is broad and long term; an objective is specific, measurable and time-limited. Write "increase market share to 30% by 2028" as an objective, not an aim.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying all private firms only want profit"}
Private firms also pursue survival, growth, market share and social goals. Match the objective to the size, stage and ownership of the business in the case.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to apply the objective to the case"}
A list of objectives earns few marks. Say which objective suits the firm in the question and why, for example survival for a new tuck shop in a recession.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS Level): the objectives of different types of organisation, the hierarchy from mission to tactics, and CSR. ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2: definitions of objectives, strategic, tactical and operational levels, conflict and constraints.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/define*: a brief accurate definition. *Explain/describe*: a reason plus application, for instance why a new firm aims first for survival. *Analyse*: follow the consequences of an objective for other parts of the business. *Discuss/evaluate*: weigh profit against social goals, and justify your final ranking with a condition.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The hierarchy runs from mission statement to aims, objectives, strategy and tactics; ZIMSEC labels objectives as strategic, tactical and operational.
- Common objectives are survival, profit, growth, market share, social and environmental goals, and service.
- Private, public and social-enterprise organisations pursue different mixes of objectives.
- The triple bottom line judges a firm on economic, social and environmental results; CSR has both costs and benefits.
- Objectives can conflict and are limited by constraints; they change with the stage of the business and its environment.
:::
