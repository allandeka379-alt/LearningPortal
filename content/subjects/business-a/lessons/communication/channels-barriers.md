## Introduction

A fictional hardware chain with branches in Harare, Chinhoyi and Mutare decides to stop selling on credit to builders who have not paid within 60 days. The head office manager sends the new rule as a long email in technical finance language. The Harare cashier reads it the next morning, the Chinhoyi branch has no internet for three days, and in Mutare the branch manager hears about it from a customer who was refused credit. Within a week the three branches follow three different versions of the rule. The decision was sound; the **channel** and the **barriers** wrecked it.

This lesson is examined in ZIMSEC Business Studies (Papers 1 and 2, Forms 5 and 6), Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **A Level** (Papers 3 and 4). The communication process model and the methods of communication are in the previous lesson; here you study the **routes** messages travel, what goes wrong and what management can do about it.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Communication channel"}
The route along which a message passes between the sender and the receiver inside or outside a business.
:::

:::definition{term="One-way communication"}
Communication in which the message flows from sender to receiver with no planned feedback, such as a notice or a standard instruction.
:::

:::definition{term="Two-way communication"}
Communication in which the receiver responds, so that the sender and receiver exchange messages, such as a discussion or a meeting.
:::

:::definition{term="Formal communication"}
Communication that follows the official lines of authority and procedures of the business, usually recorded in writing.
:::

:::definition{term="Informal communication (the grapevine)"}
Communication that passes between people outside the official channels, through friendships and conversation.
:::

:::definition{term="Barrier to communication"}
Anything that prevents a message being sent, received or understood as the sender intended.
:::

## Channels

### Vertical communication

Vertical communication flows **up and down** the hierarchy.

- **Downward**: instructions, targets, policies and feedback on performance, passed from managers to subordinates.
- **Upward**: reports, suggestions, complaints and results, passed from workers to managers.

In a tall hierarchy with many levels a message passes through many people, so it is **slow**, may be **filtered** (each person leaves out or changes some detail) and may be **distorted**. A short chain is faster and more accurate, but each manager then has a wider span of control.

### Horizontal (lateral) communication

Horizontal communication flows **between people at the same level** or between departments, for example the production manager and the sales manager agreeing a delivery date. It coordinates departments, avoids duplication and speeds decisions, but it can bypass the manager and cause conflict if departments compete.

### One-way and two-way

| Feature | One-way | Two-way |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feedback | None planned | Built in |
| Speed | Fast to send | Slower: time for replies |
| Accuracy of understanding | Risk of misunderstanding | Questions clear up doubts |
| Effect on staff | May feel ordered about | Feel consulted and involved |
| Suitable for | Routine notices, simple rules, emergencies | Change, complex tasks, grievances |

### Formal and informal

Formal communication is **recorded**, follows authority and is suitable for instructions, contracts and warnings. Its weakness is that it can be slow and rigid. Informal communication is fast and flexible and builds relationships, but it carries **rumour** and half-truths, and management cannot control it.

## The role of informal communication

The grapevine exists in every business. Used carefully, it helps management: it spreads good news quickly, shows how staff feel about a plan before it is announced, and builds team spirit. Its dangers are rumour (for example, a false story that the branch will close), anxiety, lower morale and leaks of confidential information. A sensible manager does not try to abolish the grapevine; the manager **feeds it with accurate, timely formal information** so that rumours have less room to grow.

## Barriers to communication

| Barrier | How it blocks the message | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Language and jargon | Receiver does not understand the words | Technical finance terms sent to shop assistants; Shona, Ndebele and English mixed |
| Noise and physical distance | Message not heard, or branches far apart | Machinery noise in a workshop; branches in three cities |
| Information overload | Too many messages, so important ones are missed | Fifty emails a day, with the key instruction buried |
| Poor medium | Wrong method for the message | A sensitive warning sent by text |
| Technology failure | Message cannot be sent or received | No internet in Chinhoyi for three days |
| Attitudes and mistrust | Receiver ignores, resists or doubts the message | Workers who think management is hiding redundancies |
| Culture and status | Different customs, or fear of speaking to a senior | Workers afraid to report a problem to the manager |
| Poor timing and filtering | Message arrives late or is changed on the way | A rule that reaches a branch after the sale is made |

## The role of management in facilitating communication

Managers can improve communication by:

- choosing a **suitable method and channel** for the message, with the right level of detail;
- using **plain language** and checking understanding through **feedback** (asking the receiver to repeat the instruction);
- keeping the chain short (a flatter structure) and holding regular **team briefings**;
- giving **training** in communication and in the use of the technology;
- providing **two-way channels**: suggestion boxes, meetings, works councils and open-door policies;
- sending messages **in good time** and avoiding overload by sorting what is urgent from what is routine;
- building **trust**, so that employees pass on bad news and ideas without fear;
- giving **interpreters or translated notices** where staff speak different languages.

## Communication and efficiency

Good communication raises efficiency because staff know what to do and why, mistakes and rework fall, decisions are made faster on better information, and workers who are consulted are more motivated and loyal. Poor communication does the opposite: errors, delays, conflict, high labour turnover and lost customers. Better communication also has **costs**: management time, training, equipment and the delay of consulting everyone. In an assessment, judge whether the gain in efficiency is worth the cost *in this business*.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: case analysis, diagnosing a communication failure]
**Context.** In the fictional hardware chain in the introduction, head office sends a technical email about credit rules. The Chinhoyi branch has no internet for three days. The Mutare manager learns of the rule from a customer.

| Step | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Identify the channel | One-way, downward, formal (a written email) |
| Identify the barriers | Jargon (technical finance language); technology failure at Chinhoyi; no feedback; poor timing |
| Effect on the business | Three branches apply three versions of the rule; customers are annoyed; some credit is wrongly given |
| Recommended improvement | Plain-language summary, a short briefing by telephone, and a check that each manager has understood |

**Point.** The channel was chosen for speed, not for understanding.

**Application.** The email reached Harare on time but not Chinhoyi, and the Mutare manager was embarrassed in front of a customer.

**Analysis.** Without feedback head office could not know the rule was misunderstood, so the errors continued until customers complained. The jargon made the rule hard to apply even where the email arrived.

**Judgement.** A telephone briefing and a one-page summary would cost only a little management time and would prevent costly mistakes. The extra step is worth it for a rule that changes how every branch earns money, though it is not needed for routine notices.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, the grapevine and a rumour]
**Context.** A fictional printing company in Bulawayo plans to move one machine to a new building. A cleaner overhears the managers and the story that spreads is that "the factory is closing and everyone will lose their jobs". Productivity falls and two skilled printers apply for other jobs.

| Step | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Type of communication | Informal; the grapevine; one-way; no accurate information |
| Barrier | Mistrust and anxiety; the lack of an official message |
| Consequence | Lower morale and productivity; labour turnover |
| Response | A formal, two-way meeting with the facts, and a written notice |

**Point.** The grapevine filled the gap that management left.

**Application.** The printers believed the rumour because no official statement had been made.

**Analysis.** A timely meeting where staff can ask questions would remove the uncertainty, show that management trusts them and keep the two skilled printers.

**Judgement.** The company cannot stop the grapevine, but it can win the contest with it by being first with the truth. The meeting costs an hour of production, much less than losing skilled printers.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling informal communication useless"}
The grapevine is fast and can help management to learn how staff feel. It is a risk only when it replaces accurate formal information.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing barriers without applying them"}
"Language is a barrier" scores little. Say which message, which people, what went wrong and what the effect was on the business.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the direction with the form"}
Vertical or horizontal describes the **direction** of a message. Formal or informal describes whether it follows the **official route**. A talk between two managers at the same level can be formal (a meeting) or informal (over lunch).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 and Business Enterprise Skills Paper 1 ask you to *state*, *outline*, *describe* or *discuss* channels and barriers; Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 ask you to *analyse*, *assess* or *evaluate* how far better communication improves efficiency in a case. For *explain* and *analyse*, give the barrier, say who is affected and follow the chain to a result on efficiency, cost or morale. For *discuss* and *evaluate*, weigh the benefits of better communication against its cost and time, then give a judgement and a condition (such as the size of the business or the urgency of the message).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Channels run vertically (up and down) and horizontally (between departments), formally or informally, one-way or two-way.
- Long chains slow messages and increase filtering and distortion; two-way channels give feedback.
- Barriers include language, noise, overload, distance, technology, attitudes, culture and poor timing.
- Management overcomes barriers by choosing the right method, using plain language, seeking feedback, shortening the chain and building trust.
- The grapevine cannot be abolished; managers feed it with accurate information.
- Better communication raises efficiency but costs time and money, so judge whether it is worth it in the case.
:::
