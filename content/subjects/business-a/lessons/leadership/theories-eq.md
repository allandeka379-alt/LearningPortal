## Introduction

A fictional construction company in Gaborone has two site managers. Neo has run building sites for 20 years, is calm and tall, and workers say "he looks like a boss". Thabo is quieter, but he always asks gang leaders how a job is going, adjusts his instructions to the experience of each crew, and stays composed when a delivery is late. Whose approach will work better on a new site with an inexperienced crew? Leadership theories give managers different ways to answer this question: who is born to lead, what do successful leaders do, does the best approach depend on the situation, and what makes people follow?

This lesson is examined in ZIMSEC Business Studies (Form 5), Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **A Level** (Papers 3 and 4). Goleman's emotional intelligence is **Cambridge A Level only**. McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y are covered in the lesson on management styles. You should know the difference between leaders and managers.

:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Trait theory"}
The view that effective leaders are born with, or have developed, particular personal qualities such as intelligence, confidence and determination.
:::

:::definition{term="Behavioural theory"}
The view that effective leadership depends on what leaders do, not on who they are, so that leadership behaviour can be learned.
:::

:::definition{term="Contingency theory"}
The view that no single leadership style is best: the most effective style depends on the situation, including the task, the followers and the circumstances.
:::

:::definition{term="Transformational leadership"}
Leadership that inspires followers to look beyond their own interests, share a vision and achieve more than they expected, often by bringing about major change.
:::

:::definition{term="Emotional intelligence"}
The ability to recognise, understand and manage one's own emotions and those of other people. Goleman links it to effective leadership.
:::

## The main theories

| Theory | Main idea | Use | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trait | Leaders have qualities such as confidence, intelligence and drive | Helps to choose people with leadership potential | Many successful leaders lack the supposed traits; ignores the situation; the traits cannot be taught |
| Behavioural | What leaders do (concern for people and for the task) matters, and can be learned | Supports training programmes | May ignore the situation; no single best behaviour in all cases |
| Contingency | The best style depends on the task, followers and circumstances | Reminds managers to adapt style | Hard to apply and to measure the situation; managers may not be flexible |
| Power and influence | Leaders use sources of power (position, reward, expertise, respect) to influence | Explains why followers obey | Power used without respect may produce resistance |
| Transformational | Leaders inspire a shared vision and change | Suits periods of change and growth | Depends on the personality of the leader; may be hard to sustain; rarely suits routine work |

### Blake and Mouton's managerial grid (behavioural theory)

Robert Blake and Jane Mouton plotted two dimensions: **concern for production (task)** and **concern for people**, each scored from 1 (low) to 9 (high).

| Grid position | Name | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1,1 | Impoverished | Minimum effort for task and for people |
| 9,1 | Authority-compliance (produce or perish) | Task first; people treated as tools |
| 1,9 | Country club | People's comfort first; task neglected |
| 5,5 | Middle of the road | Compromise between task and people |
| 9,9 | Team management | High concern for both; commitment through shared goals |

The grid suggests that team management (9,9) is the best style in most cases, but it is difficult to achieve and may not suit emergencies.

### Power and influence

Leaders may draw on different sources of power: **legitimate** power from position; **reward** power from the ability to give rewards; **coercive** power from the ability to punish; **expert** power from skill and knowledge; and **referent** power from respect and admiration. Leaders who rely only on legitimate or coercive power often get compliance but not commitment; expert and referent power usually give more lasting influence.

### Transformational leadership

A transformational leader provides a vision, inspires and challenges followers, and gives individual attention. This contrasts with **transactional** leadership, in which the leader exchanges rewards for performance and keeps a routine going. Transformational leadership suits a business facing major change, such as a turnaround; transactional leadership may suit stable, routine operations.

## Goleman's emotional intelligence (Cambridge A Level only)

Daniel Goleman identified four competencies of emotional intelligence, which he linked to successful leadership.

| Competence | Meaning | Example for a manager |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-awareness | Understanding one's own emotions, strengths and limits | A manager who knows that she gets impatient when tired and delegates difficult talks |
| Self-management | Controlling one's own emotions and behaviour; adaptability | Staying calm when a major delivery fails |
| Social awareness | Understanding other people's emotions and the mood of the group (empathy) | Noticing that a team is anxious about redundancies |
| Social skills | Managing relationships: influence, teamwork, resolving conflict | Building agreement between two departments |

Leaders with high emotional intelligence tend to build trust, handle conflict well and motivate teams. The limits are that it is difficult to measure, it may be hard to develop in adults, and technical skills and the situation also matter.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: placing managers on the Blake and Mouton grid]
**Context.** A fictional printing company in Harare has four managers, each scored on concern for production (task) and concern for people.

| Manager | Concern for production | Concern for people | Grid position and name |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mr Banda | 9 | 2 | 9,2: close to authority-compliance |
| Ms Chikwanha | 2 | 9 | 2,9: close to country club |
| Mr Dube | 8 | 8 | 8,8: close to team management |
| Ms Esau | 1 | 1 | 1,1: impoverished |

**Point.** Only Mr Dube combines a high concern for production and for people.

**Application.** Mr Banda sets high targets but shows little interest in staff, so he may achieve output at the cost of morale. Ms Chikwanha keeps staff happy, but deadlines may be missed. Ms Esau does little for task or people.

**Judgement.** Mr Dube's style is most likely to give both output and commitment. The grid is simple, though, and does not tell a manager what to do in an emergency, where Mr Banda's style may be right. Ms Esau needs training or replacement.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, which approach for a turnaround?]
**Context.** A fictional clothing factory in Bulawayo has lost two large customers and its workers fear redundancies. A new managing director, Rudo, holds meetings in which she sets out a plan to win back customers by making a new range, explains how each department contributes, listens to fears and sets high but achievable targets. She also stays calm when a machine fails.

**Point.** Rudo is showing transformational leadership supported by emotional intelligence.

**Application.** Setting out a vision and plan and explaining how each department contributes is the vision part of transformational leadership. Listening to fears shows social awareness; staying calm when a machine fails shows self-management (Cambridge A Level).

**Analysis.** In a time of change and uncertainty, workers need a clear direction and a leader they trust. Her approach may lift commitment, so workers support the new range. A purely trait view ("is she a born leader?") or a transactional approach (reward for output) would not address their fears.

**Judgement.** Transformational leadership is suitable here, but it relies heavily on Rudo's personality. If she leaves, the momentum may be lost. In a stable period, a more routine approach might suit better.
:::

## Choosing between the theories

No theory explains everything. Trait theory helps with selection, behavioural theory with training, contingency theory with flexibility, power theory with understanding influence, and transformational theory with change. A thoughtful manager draws on several and judges them against the situation.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying 'leaders are born, not made' as a fact"}
This is only the trait view. Behavioural theory says leadership can be learned. Use the phrase as a view, with a limitation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing contingency theory with a style"}
Contingency theory is not a particular style. It says the best style depends on the situation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using Goleman in a ZIMSEC answer without need"}
Goleman's emotional intelligence is a Cambridge A Level requirement. It may be mentioned in a ZIMSEC essay, but it is not required.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (state and outline) and Paper 2 (essays comparing theories); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Paper 3 (short case questions) and Paper 4 (essays: assess a leadership theory or style for a case business).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/identify*: name the theory or competence. *Explain/describe*: say what it means in the case. *Analyse/examine*: trace its effect on followers. *Assess/evaluate/discuss*: weigh the usefulness and limits of the theory for the business, and give a reasoned judgement, for example which approach suits the current situation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Trait theory: leaders have particular qualities; behavioural theory: what leaders do matters and can be learned (Blake and Mouton's grid of concern for production and for people).
- Contingency theory: the best leadership style depends on the situation.
- Power and influence theories look at sources of power: legitimate, reward, coercive, expert and referent.
- Transformational leadership inspires a shared vision and change; transactional leadership exchanges rewards for performance.
- Goleman (Cambridge A Level only): self-awareness, self-management, social awareness and social skills.
- Judge each theory by its use and its limits in the situation described.
:::
