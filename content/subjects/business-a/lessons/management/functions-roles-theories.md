## Introduction

Thandeka manages a fictional hardware shop in Masvingo with eight staff. In one morning she checks that the shop has enough cement for a large order, tells two assistants where to stand, settles an argument about the lunch rota, phones a supplier who has delivered late, and explains to the owner why sales fell last month. She is doing different kinds of work: planning ahead, organising people, directing them, checking results, speaking for the shop, and solving problems. Management thinkers have tried to classify this work in two ways: by **functions** (what managers do) and by **roles** (the parts they play).

This lesson applies to ZIMSEC Business Studies (Form 5), Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge AS Level (Papers 1 and 2). The management theories at the end are ZIMSEC detail. You should know what a business objective is and what a manager is.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |

Management theories by name are ZIMSEC detail. Mintzberg's three groups of roles are in both syllabi.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Management"}
The process of planning, organising, directing and controlling the resources of a business in order to achieve its objectives.
:::

:::definition{term="Fayol's functions of management"}
Henri Fayol's analysis of a manager's work. His five elements are commonly summarised as planning, organising, commanding (directing), coordinating and controlling. The syllabi use the four-part summary: planning, organising, directing and controlling.
:::

:::definition{term="Mintzberg's managerial roles"}
Henry Mintzberg's description of ten roles that managers actually play, grouped as interpersonal (figurehead, leader, liaison), informational (monitor, disseminator, spokesperson) and decisional (entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator).
:::

## The four functions of management

| Function | What the manager does | Example in a hardware shop |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Planning | Sets objectives and decides how to reach them: forecasts, budgets, schedules | Forecasts cement sales for the winter building season and orders stock |
| Organising | Arranges resources and people: who does what, with what authority | Assigns staff to the counter, the yard and the stockroom |
| Directing (leading) | Guides, motivates and communicates with staff to get action | Explains targets to staff and motivates them |
| Controlling | Compares results with plans and takes corrective action | Checks stock against orders and takes action when sales are below target |

The functions form a cycle. Planning sets targets, organising and directing put the plan into practice, and controlling checks the results, which feeds into the next plan. Some lists add **coordinating** (making sure that departments work together) and **staffing**. Fayol (1841-1925), a French mining engineer and manager, was one of the first to write about this; his five elements include "command" and "coordination" where the modern four-part summary has "directing".

## Mintzberg's managerial roles

Mintzberg watched what managers actually did and found that their work was more varied, interrupted and verbal than the neat functions suggest.

| Group | Role | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Interpersonal** | Figurehead | Opens a new branch or signs official documents |
| | Leader | Hires, trains and motivates staff |
| | Liaison | Builds contacts with suppliers, customers and other managers |
| **Informational** | Monitor | Collects information about sales and competitors |
| | Disseminator | Passes information to staff |
| | Spokesperson | Speaks to the media, the bank or the owner on behalf of the business |
| **Decisional** | Entrepreneur | Starts new projects and improvements |
| | Disturbance handler | Deals with sudden problems, such as a late delivery or a strike |
| | Resource allocator | Decides who gets budget, staff and time |
| | Negotiator | Bargains with suppliers, customers and employee representatives |

The two views fit together: functions say what managers are responsible for, roles describe how they carry the work out in a busy day. Roles have an advantage in that they explain unplanned work; functions are easier for learning and for judging the quality of a manager.

## How managers contribute to business performance

Good managers set clear objectives, use resources efficiently, keep workers motivated and respond quickly to problems. Weak management leads to wasted resources, conflict, poor decisions and lost customers. However, results also depend on factors outside the manager's control, such as the economy, competition and the skills of the workforce.

## ZIMSEC detail: management theories

ZIMSEC asks for the main theories of management and how they apply to modern enterprises.

| Theory | Main idea | Strength | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Scientific management (Taylor) | Find the one best way by studying work; pay for output | Raises productivity in repetitive work | Boring work; ignores social needs |
| Administrative or classical (Fayol) | Management principles and functions apply to all organisations (e.g. unity of command, division of work) | Gives a clear structure and basis for training | Rigid and too general; may fit poorly in changing conditions |
| Bureaucratic (Weber) | Rules, a clear hierarchy, written records, jobs given by qualification | Fairness, consistency and control in a large organisation | Slow, impersonal and resistant to change |
| Behavioural or human relations (Mayo and others) | Workers have social needs; groups and communication matter | Higher morale and commitment | Slower and may not raise output |

Modern businesses use elements of all four: a small shop may use standard procedures (bureaucratic), clear job duties (administrative), a bonus (scientific) and team spirit (behavioural). The best choice depends on the size of the enterprise, the work and the staff.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: classifying a manager's day by function]
**Context.** Thandeka's tasks at the hardware shop are listed below.

| Task | Function | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Forecasts sales of cement for the next quarter | Planning | Sets targets and decides what to order |
| Draws up a rota showing who works at the counter and in the yard | Organising | Arranges people and tasks |
| Briefs staff and encourages them to reach the weekly target | Directing | Guides and motivates staff |
| Compares weekly sales with the target and finds a shortfall | Controlling | Compares actual with plan |
| Cancels a promotion that is not working and substitutes a discount | Controlling (corrective action) | Takes action after checking results |

**Judgement.** The tasks form a cycle: forecasting leads to the rota and the briefing, and the weekly check shows whether the plan worked. If sales are below target, Thandeka should plan again. A manager who only does the first three functions has no way of knowing whether the plan succeeded.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis with Mintzberg's roles]
**Context.** A fictional bus company in Bulawayo loses a driver on the morning of a busy route. The manager phones a driver at home, reallocates two buses, tells passengers at the station about the delay and then telephones the owner to explain.

**Point.** The manager played decisional, interpersonal and informational roles in one morning.

**Application.** Phoning a driver and reallocating buses is the disturbance handler and resource allocator roles. Telling passengers is the disseminator or spokesperson role. Reassuring the owner and keeping good relationships with drivers uses the liaison and leader roles.

**Analysis.** The roles show why a manager's day is rarely planned: the function of planning cannot predict a sudden absence. A manager who is good at disturbance handling reduces lost revenue.

**Judgement.** Mintzberg's view better explains the busy morning than the functions alone, but the functions are still the way the owner will judge long-term performance: whether the company planned enough drivers in the first place.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing functions without applying them"}
Naming planning, organising, directing and controlling is not enough. Say what the manager does in the case, for example "comparing sales with the budget is controlling".
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Fayol with Mintzberg"}
Fayol described functions (what managers do). Mintzberg described roles (the parts they play). Do not mix them up or attribute roles to Fayol.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the theories as one right answer"}
No theory is best for all businesses. Compare the size, work and workers in the case before judging.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (state and outline functions or roles) and Paper 2 (essays on management theories); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Paper 1 (five-mark questions and the essay) and Paper 2.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/identify*: name the function or role. *Outline/describe/explain*: say what the manager does in the case. *Analyse/examine*: link a manager's action to business performance. *Discuss/evaluate/assess*: weigh the strengths and limits of a theory or style for the business and give a judgement.
:::
