## Introduction

Farai Moyo has just been promoted to supervisor at a fictional quarry in Mutare. His predecessor gave orders and expected them to be followed without question. At first output stayed steady, but the best workers left and mistakes were hidden. Farai is considering other ways to manage: asking the crew for ideas before deciding, leaving experienced drillers to organise their own shifts, or looking after workers' welfare as a good employer should. Each of these is a management style, a consistent way in which a manager makes decisions and deals with staff. A manager's style is influenced by assumptions about people, which Douglas McGregor described as Theory X and Theory Y.

This lesson is examined in ZIMSEC Business Studies (Form 5), Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge AS Level (Papers 1 and 2). It follows the lesson on the functions and roles of managers.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Management style"}
The way in which a manager makes decisions, communicates with employees and uses authority.
:::

:::definition{term="Autocratic style"}
A style in which the manager makes decisions alone, gives orders and expects obedience, with one-way communication from the top down.
:::

:::definition{term="Democratic style"}
A style in which employees are involved in decision making. In the consultative form the manager asks for views but decides; in the participative form employees take part in the decision.
:::

:::definition{term="Laissez-faire style"}
A style in which the manager leaves employees to organise their own work with very little direction, and intervenes only when asked.
:::

:::definition{term="Paternalistic style"}
A style in which the manager makes the decisions but acts in the interests of employees, explaining decisions and caring for their welfare.
:::

## The four styles

| Style | How decisions are made | Communication | Effect on motivation | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Autocratic | Manager alone | One-way, downward | Can lower motivation; may give fast, clear action | Emergencies; unskilled or new staff; tasks needing tight control |
| Paternalistic | Manager decides, with care for staff | Mostly downward, with explanations | Staff feel valued and loyal, but may feel dependent | Businesses with long-serving staff; family firms |
| Democratic: consultative | Manager asks, then decides | Two-way | Staff feel heard; decisions are better informed | Skilled staff; changes that affect many people |
| Democratic: participative | Group decides together | Two-way and between staff | High involvement and commitment; slow decisions | Skilled teams; creative work; where staff need to accept change |
| Laissez-faire | Employees decide for themselves | Mostly between employees | High freedom for the capable; confusion for others | Highly skilled, self-motivated professionals |

**Choosing a style.** No style is best everywhere. The right style depends on the **task** (urgent or routine, complex or simple), the **employees** (skilled or unskilled, experienced or new, willing or unwilling), the **time available**, and the manager's own personality. A good manager can use more than one style: autocratic in a safety emergency, consultative when planning a rota.

## McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y

Douglas McGregor described two sets of assumptions that managers hold about workers.

| | Theory X | Theory Y |
| --- | --- | --- |
| View of workers | Dislike work, avoid responsibility, need to be controlled and threatened | Can enjoy work, seek responsibility, can direct themselves |
| Source of motivation | Money, close supervision, penalties | Interesting work, responsibility, recognition |
| Likely style | Autocratic, close supervision | Democratic, delegating |
| Likely effect | Obedience but low creativity; some workers behave as expected | Commitment and ideas; may be slow where workers are not ready |

The assumptions can become **self-fulfilling**: workers who are closely controlled may become passive and do only what they are told, which seems to prove the manager right. McGregor did not say that all workers are the same; Theory Y does not suit every worker or task.

## Effects of style on the business

| Area | Autocratic | Democratic | Laissez-faire |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Motivation | Low for many workers | Higher | High for capable workers; low if staff need direction |
| Speed of decisions | Fast | Slower | Variable |
| Quality of decisions | Depends on one person's knowledge | Draws on staff knowledge | Depends on staff ability |
| Communication | One-way | Two-way | Informal |
| Staff turnover | Often higher | Lower | Depends |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: choosing a style for a quarry]
**Context.** Farai supervises 12 workers at a fictional quarry in Mutare. Three situations arise this week.

| Situation | Best style | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A slope shows signs of collapse and the crew must move at once | Autocratic | Speed and clear orders are vital for safety; there is no time to consult |
| The crew rota must change because of a new transport schedule | Consultative or participative democratic | Workers know what suits them; involvement increases acceptance |
| Two experienced drillers want to plan their own drilling sequence | Laissez-faire | They are skilled and the task is within their competence |

**Judgement.** Farai should not use a single style all the time. His predecessor's autocratic style may have been suitable in an emergency but was the wrong choice for routine planning, where it lost good workers. The limitation is that switching style can confuse workers, so Farai should explain why he is changing.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, Theory X in a bakery]
**Context.** The manager of a fictional bakery in Bulawayo believes that workers only work for money and will take breaks if not watched. She checks each baker's output every hour, deducts pay for any waste and allows no suggestions. Output is steady but two skilled bakers leave and waste is hidden.

**Point.** The manager holds Theory X assumptions and uses an autocratic style.

**Application.** Hourly checks and no suggestions show that she does not trust workers to direct themselves. Pay deductions for waste encourage workers to hide it.

**Analysis.** The close control gives predictable output, but the skilled bakers, who could be self-directed, feel untrusted and leave. Hiding waste means the manager lacks information to reduce it. Workers who are treated as lazy may become passive, which seems to prove her view right.

**Judgement.** A consultative approach would give bakers a voice in solving waste and could reduce turnover. Close control may remain suitable for new trainees. Therefore the style should be matched to the staff, not set by one assumption.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing paternalistic as the same as democratic"}
A paternalistic manager still makes the decisions alone, but cares about employees and explains. Democratic managers involve employees in the decision.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying autocratic is always bad"}
Autocratic management is effective in emergencies, with inexperienced staff and where quick, clear action is needed. Judge the style against the situation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up Theory X and Theory Y"}
Theory X: workers dislike work and need control. Theory Y: workers can enjoy work and accept responsibility. X is not an "extra" or advanced theory.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (state, outline or identify a style from a short case) and Paper 2 (essays); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Paper 1 (five-mark questions and the essay) and Paper 2 (data response).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Identify/state*: name the style or the theory from evidence in the case. *Explain/describe*: say what the style involves in this business. *Analyse/examine*: trace the effect on motivation, decisions or communication. *Evaluate/discuss/justify*: weigh the style against the task, the staff and the time available, and give a reasoned recommendation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Styles: autocratic (manager decides alone), paternalistic (decides with care and explanation), democratic (consultative or participative) and laissez-faire (staff decide).
- The best style depends on the task, the employees, the time available and the manager.
- McGregor: Theory X assumes workers dislike work and need control; Theory Y assumes they can enjoy work and accept responsibility.
- Theory X tends to produce autocratic management; Theory Y tends to produce democratic or delegating management.
- Style affects motivation, speed and quality of decisions, communication and staff turnover.
- Judge a style against the situation and avoid saying that one style is always best.
:::
