## Introduction

A young entrepreneur in Lusaka wants to open a fast-food outlet. She can start from nothing and build her own brand, or pay for the right to trade under a famous name with a tested menu and training. Elsewhere, two firms with different strengths combine to build a business neither could manage alone, and a group of teachers runs a bakery whose profit pays for school meals. These are franchises, joint ventures and social enterprises.

This lesson is for Cambridge AS Level (Papers 1 and 2): you need to define each, explain benefits and drawbacks, and judge which form of ownership best suits a business in a given case. It is not a separate topic in the ZIMSEC syllabi, but the ideas help with ownership questions generally. You should already know sole traders, partnerships and companies from the previous lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Franchise"}
An agreement in which the owner of a business idea and brand (the franchisor) allows another person or company (the franchisee) to trade using the brand, products and methods in return for fees and royalties.
:::

:::definition{term="Joint venture"}
An agreement between two or more businesses to set up and share the ownership, risks and rewards of a specific project or new business.
:::

:::definition{term="Social enterprise"}
A business that trades to meet a social or environmental purpose, with most of its profit reinvested in that purpose rather than paid to owners.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Board note"}
This lesson is Cambridge AS Level content. A reference to the "triple bottom line" (people, planet, profit) links to social and environmental objectives met in later lessons.
:::

## Franchises

The **franchisor** sells the right to use its name, products, systems and training. The **franchisee** pays an initial fee, then usually a royalty (a percentage of sales) and contributes to advertising.

| For the franchisee | For the franchisor |
| --- | --- |
| Proven brand and methods: lower risk | Grows the brand without investing all the capital |
| Training and support | Income from fees and royalties |
| Easier to obtain bank finance | Franchisee's local knowledge and effort |
| Costs: fees, royalties, limits on freedom to change products or suppliers | Risk: a poor franchisee can damage the brand; loses some control |

## Joint ventures

Two firms form a new business to which each contributes money, skills or access to markets. Reasons: share costs and risks; combine different strengths (one firm has technology, the other local knowledge); enter a new market more cheaply. Drawbacks: disagreement over control and profits; clashes of culture; one partner may gain the other's secrets; the arrangement is hard to end.

## Social enterprises

A social enterprise earns its income by trading, not mainly by donation, and uses most of its profit for its purpose, such as employing disabled workers or supplying clean water. Benefits: public goodwill and loyal customers, motivated workers, and a clear purpose. Drawbacks: profit is a secondary aim, so growth and rewards for owners may be limited; it may be hard to raise commercial finance; balancing social and financial aims is difficult.

## Public sector bodies in outline

Public corporations and state-owned bodies are owned by the state or local authority, aim to provide a service rather than to maximise profit and are funded by revenue and government support. Compared with private businesses they may be less pressed to cut costs but better placed to provide essential services to everyone.

## Choosing the most appropriate form of ownership

Judge each form against the owner's situation using these factors:

| Factor | Question to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Liability | How much personal risk can the owner accept? |
| Finance | How much capital is needed, and who can supply it? |
| Control | Does the owner want to decide alone or share? |
| Risk | How uncertain is the venture; would a tested brand help? |
| Tax and administration | How much paperwork and cost is acceptable? |
| Size and purpose | Is the aim profit, growth or a social purpose? |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: franchisee's profit and the initial fee]
A fictional franchisee runs a chicken restaurant in Lusaka. The franchisor charges an initial fee of US\$30,000 and a royalty of 5% of revenue each year. In a year, revenue is US\$240,000, cost of sales US\$120,000 and running costs US\$70,000.

| Item | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Revenue | 240,000 |
| Cost of sales | (120,000) |
| Running costs | (70,000) |
| Royalty (5% of revenue) | (12,000) |
| Profit before the initial fee | 38,000 |

1. Royalty = 5% × 240,000 = US\$12,000.
2. Profit = 240,000 − 120,000 − 70,000 − 12,000 = US\$38,000.
3. The initial fee of US\$30,000 is a one-off cost. Profit in the year is 38,000, so the fee is recovered in 30,000 ÷ 38,000 = 0.79 years (about 9 to 10 months) if sales stay at this level.
4. Evaluation: the royalty takes US\$12,000 a year, which a non-franchised owner would keep, but the franchisee gains a tested brand and training. The franchise is worthwhile if sales are higher than they would be without the brand.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: sharing a joint venture]
Two fictional firms, Zambezi Logistics and Kalahari Cold Storage, set up a joint venture to build a refrigerated depot. They invest US\$450,000 in the ratio 60 : 40 and agree to share profits in the same ratio. In its first year the joint venture earns a profit of US\$90,000.

1. Zambezi invests 60% × 450,000 = US\$270,000; Kalahari invests 40% × 450,000 = US\$180,000.
2. Zambezi's profit share = 60% × 90,000 = US\$54,000; Kalahari's = 40% × 90,000 = US\$36,000. Together these make US\$90,000.
3. Each partner shares both cost and risk, and neither could easily afford the depot alone.
4. The risk is disagreement: if Zambezi wants to expand the depot and Kalahari does not, the venture could stall, so the agreement should state how decisions are made.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, which form of ownership?]
**Context.** Thandeka, a fictional owner in Gaborone, wants to open a coffee shop. She has US\$15,000 and no experience. A franchisor offers its brand for US\$25,000 plus 6% of revenue; a bank would lend the shortfall if she offers her house as security.

**Point.** A franchise reduces risk, but it increases costs and her personal exposure.

**Application.** The brand and training suit her lack of experience; the loan of US\$10,000 or more would be secured on her house if she is a sole trader.

**Analysis.** The franchise lowers the chance of failure but the royalty reduces profit every year. If she forms a private limited company to run the franchise, her house is better protected, but the bank may still ask for a personal guarantee.

**Judgement.** The franchise is sensible given her inexperience, provided she can cover the royalty and fee from projected sales. She should consider a company structure to protect her home, but must compare the extra costs against that protection.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing franchise and joint venture"}
A franchise is a licence to use another firm's brand in return for fees. A joint venture is a new business jointly owned by two or more firms.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying social enterprises make no profit"}
They aim to make a profit but reinvest most of it in the social or environmental purpose.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving one 'best' form of ownership"}
The best form depends on liability, finance, control, risk, tax and purpose. Apply them to the case before judging.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
Cambridge Paper 1 (definitions and short explanations; essay on ownership) and Paper 2 (data response with choosing a form of ownership, AS Level). Not a separate ZIMSEC topic.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define/identify*: precise meaning. *Explain/analyse*: link a benefit or drawback to the case. *Assess/evaluate/advise*: compare at least two forms of ownership against the owner's aims and recommend one, stating the condition on which the advice depends.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A franchisee pays fees and royalties to use a franchisor's brand and methods, gaining a tested business with less risk but less freedom.
- A joint venture shares cost, risk and reward between firms but can suffer from disagreement.
- A social enterprise trades for a social or environmental purpose and reinvests most of its profit.
- Public sector bodies aim to provide a service rather than maximise profit.
- Choosing a form of ownership means weighing liability, finance, control, risk, tax and purpose for the specific case.
:::
