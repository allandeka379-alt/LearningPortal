## Introduction

Chipo's Bakery started as a sole trader's stall in Gweru. Ten years later it supplies five supermarkets, owns two delivery vans and needs US\$150,000 to build a bigger factory. A bank will lend only if Chipo offers her house as security, and she is afraid of losing it if the plan fails. Her accountant suggests turning the bakery into a **company**, in which the business has its own legal identity and its owners are not personally liable for its debts.

Questions on companies appear in ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (features, advantages and disadvantages, calculations of dividends and control), Paper 2 (essays on changing the form of ownership), Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2, and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS Level). You should know the sole trader and partnership from the previous lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Company (limited company)"}
A business that is a separate legal person from its owners. It can own property, make contracts and be sued in its own name. Its owners are shareholders.
:::

:::definition{term="Limited liability"}
The shareholders' responsibility for the company's debts is limited to the amount they have invested (or agreed to invest) in shares. Their personal property cannot be claimed to pay the company's debts.
:::

:::definition{term="Private limited company"}
A company whose shares are sold privately, by agreement among existing owners, and cannot be offered to the general public.
:::

:::definition{term="Public limited company"}
A company that may offer its shares to the general public, usually through a stock exchange listing, and whose shares can be freely bought and sold.
:::

:::definition{term="Divorce of ownership from control"}
The situation, common in large companies, where the shareholders (owners) are different people from the directors and managers who run the business.
:::

## Features of companies

- **Separate legal personality**: the company owns the assets and owes the debts, not the shareholders.
- **Limited liability**: shareholders can lose only what they have invested.
- **Continuity**: the company continues if a shareholder dies or sells shares.
- **Shares**: ownership is divided into shares; each ordinary share normally carries one vote and a right to a dividend when one is declared.
- **Directors**: elected by the shareholders to run the company and answer to them.

## Forming a company (outline)

Founders register the company with the proper authority, deliver the documents that state its name, purpose and rules and how shares and directors will be dealt with, and pay a registration fee. Once registered, a private company may start trading; a public company must also satisfy rules for offering shares to the public.

## Private and public limited companies compared

| Feature | Private limited company | Public limited company |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shares offered to | Selected people (family, friends, investors) by private agreement | General public through a stock exchange |
| Share transfer | Restricted; needs agreement | Freely traded |
| Capital raised | Limited by who will invest | Potentially very large |
| Control | Usually stays with the founders | May pass to outside shareholders |
| Disclosure of accounts | Less demanding | More demanding; accounts published |
| Cost and formality | Moderate | High (listing, reporting, legal fees) |

## Raising capital

A private company raises capital by **private placement** (selling shares to chosen investors), by retained profit and by loans. A public company may also issue shares to the public in a **stock exchange listing**. Listing brings large sums and makes shares easy to sell, but it brings more rules and the risk of a takeover.

## Advantages and disadvantages of companies

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Limited liability protects personal property | Legal formalities and costs of forming and running the company |
| Easier to raise capital from many shareholders | Accounts must be filed; less privacy |
| Continuity | Profits shared with shareholders as dividends |
| Separation of ownership and control allows professional management | Directors may act in their own interest rather than the owners' (the *divorce* problem) |

## Changing the form of ownership

**Sole trader to private limited company:** gains limited liability and easier finance, but loses privacy, simple administration and sole control if others hold shares. **Private to public:** gains large amounts of capital and a higher profile, but risks loss of control, takeover, demanding disclosure and short-term pressure from shareholders for profit. Whether the change is wise depends on the need for capital, the owners' attitude to losing control and the cost of compliance.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: shareholding, votes and dividends]
Moyo Furniture (Pvt) Ltd has 100,000 ordinary shares. Each share has one vote. The company declares a dividend of US\$0.12 per share.

| Shareholder | Shares | Share of votes | Dividend (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Tendai Moyo | 45,000 | 45.0% | 5,400 |
| Rudo Chari | 30,000 | 30.0% | 3,600 |
| Farai Sibanda | 15,000 | 15.0% | 1,800 |
| Employees' group | 10,000 | 10.0% | 1,200 |
| Total | 100,000 | 100.0% | 12,000 |

1. Dividend = shares × 0.12; for example Tendai: 45,000 × 0.12 = US\$5,400. The total is 100,000 × 0.12 = US\$12,000.
2. Tendai's votes = 45,000 ÷ 100,000 × 100 = 45%, which is less than half, so he cannot decide alone.
3. If the employees' group supports him, together they hold 45,000 + 10,000 = 55,000 shares, or 55%, so they could carry a vote.
4. Conclusion: owning the largest single holding does not guarantee control; control requires more than 50% of the votes, either alone or with allies.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: limited liability compared with unlimited liability]
A fictional business fails with debts of US\$80,000. Its assets sell for US\$55,000. The owner has invested US\$15,000 in it and has personal property worth US\$40,000.

1. Shortfall = 80,000 − 55,000 = US\$25,000.
2. **If the business is a limited company**, the owner's loss is limited to the US\$15,000 invested. Creditors cannot claim the US\$25,000 from her personal property, which stays at US\$40,000.
3. **If it were a sole trader**, the owner would also lose the investment and would have to pay the shortfall from personal property: 40,000 − 25,000 = US\$15,000 left.
4. Limited liability therefore protects US\$25,000 of personal wealth here. The trade-off is that lenders know this and may refuse to lend to small companies unless directors give personal guarantees.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, should Chipo's Bakery become a company?]
**Context.** Chipo needs US\$150,000 and fears losing her house. Her accountant suggests forming a private limited company.

**Point.** A private limited company would protect her personal property and widen her sources of finance.

**Application.** She could sell shares to two investors for part of the US\$150,000, and her house would not be at risk if the company fails, unless she signs a personal guarantee.

**Analysis.** The investors would own part of the business and have a say in decisions and a share of the profit, so her control would fall. There would also be extra costs for registering and for preparing accounts.

**Judgement.** Forming a private company is a sensible step, if Chipo can accept shared control and the extra costs and if she avoids personal guarantees. Becoming a public company would be premature because the capital needed is moderate.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying limited liability means the company can never fail"}
Limited liability limits the owners' loss to their investment. The company can still fail.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing 'public company' with the public sector"}
A public limited company is a private-sector business whose shares can be bought by the public. The public sector is owned by the state.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that control follows votes"}
A shareholder with the most shares may not have a majority. Use percentages.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (features; calculate dividend or shareholding) and Paper 2 (discuss changing to a company); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS Level; assess the move from sole trader to private limited company).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/define*: one precise sentence, for example for limited liability. *Explain/analyse*: chain such as "limited liability → personal property protected → easier to attract investors → more capital". *Discuss/assess/evaluate*: weigh protection and capital against cost, loss of control and disclosure, and conclude with a judgement that depends on the case.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A company is a separate legal person; shareholders have limited liability.
- Private companies sell shares privately; public companies may sell to the public through a stock exchange.
- Directors run the company for the shareholders; ownership and control are often divorced in large firms.
- Companies raise more capital and give continuity, but face formalities, disclosure and loss of control.
- Changing form is a trade-off between protection and capital on one side and control, cost and privacy on the other.
:::
