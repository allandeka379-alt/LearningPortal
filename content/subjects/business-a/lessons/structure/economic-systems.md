## Introduction

A bus company in a fictional town is owned by the state. It loses money every year, its buses are old and passengers complain about delays. The government has three options: sell the company to private owners, keep it but order its managers to run it like a business, or close it and leave routes to private operators. Each option reflects a different view about how far the state should be involved in business.

This topic is tested in ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (definitions and short structured parts) and Paper 2 (essays), in Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2, and in Cambridge Paper 3 and Paper 4 at A Level, where it is examined as part of the political and economic setting of a business. You should already know the public and private sectors from the lesson on sectors; government policy tools such as taxation and interest rates come in a later lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Command economy"}
An economic system in which the state owns most resources and decides what is produced, how and for whom, usually through central planning.
:::

:::definition{term="Free-market economy"}
An economic system in which resources are mostly privately owned and decisions are made by buyers and sellers through the price system, with little state interference.
:::

:::definition{term="Mixed economy"}
An economic system in which both the state and private owners play a major part. The state provides some goods and services and regulates markets, while private businesses provide others.
:::

:::definition{term="Privatisation"}
The sale of state-owned assets or businesses to private owners.
:::

:::definition{term="Commercialisation"}
Running a public sector body on commercial lines, with a focus on efficiency, cost control and charging for its services, while it remains publicly owned.
:::

:::definition{term="Nationalisation"}
The transfer of privately owned assets or businesses into state ownership.
:::

## The three economic systems

| Feature | Command | Free market | Mixed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who owns resources | State | Private individuals | Both |
| Who decides what to produce | Central planners | Consumers' demand through prices | Market for most goods; state for some |
| Competition | Little or none | Strong | Present but regulated |
| Advantages for business | Stable demand from the state; no unemployment as a policy | Freedom, efficiency, innovation, wide choice | Balance of efficiency and social aims |
| Disadvantages for business | Little freedom or incentive; shortages and waste | Inequality, neglect of public goods, boom and slump | Disagreements over how far the state should go |

Most economies today are mixed; they differ in how far to the market or the state they lean.

## Economic philosophies (ZIMSEC)

- **Capitalism**: private ownership of the means of production; profit and markets guide decisions.
- **Socialism**: resources are owned or controlled in the interest of the community; the state plays a large role and aims to reduce inequality.
- **Communism**: all means of production are owned collectively and the state plans the economy.
- **Indigenisation**: a policy of increasing ownership and control of businesses and resources by local citizens. Questions ask about its aims and effects in general terms; do not rely on the details of current legislation.

## Privatisation, commercialisation and nationalisation

| Measure | What happens | Reasons | Possible advantages | Possible disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Privatisation | Sale of state assets to private owners | Raise money; improve efficiency; reduce state spending | Sale proceeds; more efficiency; less subsidy | Loss of control; job cuts; higher prices; services to poor areas may fall |
| Commercialisation | State body is run on business lines | Reduce losses without selling | Better cost control; less subsidy; state keeps control | Prices may rise; social aims may suffer |
| Nationalisation | Private assets taken into state ownership | Protect key industries or jobs; prevent monopoly; provide essential services | Control over key services; social aims can be met | Cost of compensation; lack of efficiency; burden on taxpayers |

Which measure is best depends on the situation: the industry, the state's finances, how competitive the market is and whether the service is essential.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: privatisation proceeds and what is kept]
A fictional state-owned water bottling company has 8,000,000 shares. The government sells 40% of the shares to private investors at US\$1.75 each and keeps the rest.

1. Shares sold = 8,000,000 × 40% = 3,200,000.
2. Sale proceeds = 3,200,000 × 1.75 = US\$5,600,000.
3. Shares kept = 8,000,000 − 3,200,000 = 4,800,000, which is 60%. At the sale price these are valued at 4,800,000 × 1.75 = US\$8,400,000.
4. The government raises US\$5.6 million now and still holds control (60%), but it gives up 40% of future dividends. Whether this is wise depends on whether the investors improve efficiency enough to raise the value of the remaining shares.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, which measure for a loss-making bus company?]
**Context.** The state-owned bus company in a fictional town earns revenue of US\$2,400,000 a year and has costs of US\$2,760,000, a loss of US\$360,000 paid for by taxpayers. Bus routes serve poor suburbs.

**Point.** Commercialisation could reduce losses without selling the company.

**Application.** If managers cut costs to US\$2,280,000 through route planning and better maintenance, the company would make a profit of 2,400,000 − 2,280,000 = US\$120,000 and need no subsidy.

**Analysis.** Privatisation might bring investment but a private owner could drop unprofitable suburban routes or raise fares, harming poorer passengers. Commercialisation keeps the social aim but may need fare increases.

**Judgement.** Commercialisation is a sensible first step, because it keeps public control of a service that poorer people depend on. If the company still fails to cover costs, privatisation with conditions on routes could be considered.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing privatisation and commercialisation"}
Privatisation changes ownership; commercialisation changes how a state body is run while it stays state-owned.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying one system is simply best"}
Each system has advantages and disadvantages. Good answers apply them to the case and reach a conditional judgement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using unsupported political claims"}
Discuss the aims and likely effects of policies in general terms, supported by the information given. Do not quote laws or figures you cannot be sure of.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 (definitions, comparison of systems, essays on privatisation); Cambridge Paper 3 and Paper 4 (A Level only: assess privatisation or nationalisation in a case).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/define*: one sentence. *Describe/outline*: give the main features. *Discuss/evaluate/assess*: set advantages against disadvantages for the stakeholders in the case, then conclude with a reasoned judgement and the condition on which it depends.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A command economy is state-planned, a free-market economy is price-driven and a mixed economy combines both.
- Capitalism, socialism, communism and indigenisation are philosophies behind economic systems (ZIMSEC).
- Privatisation sells state assets; commercialisation runs a state body like a business; nationalisation takes private assets into state ownership.
- Each measure has advantages and disadvantages that depend on the industry and the situation.
- Evaluate by weighing stakeholders' interests and stating what the best choice depends on.
:::
