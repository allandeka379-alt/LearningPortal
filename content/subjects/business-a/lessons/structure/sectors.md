## Introduction

A cotton farmer in Gokwe sells her crop to a ginnery. The ginnery sells lint to a textile mill in Kwekwe; the mill's cloth goes to a clothing factory in Bulawayo; the finished shirts are sold by a shop in Harare, advertised by a marketing agency and paid for through a bank. At each step a different type of business adds value, and each belongs to a different sector of the economy.

Sector questions appear in ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (classify firms, describe industrialisation), Paper 2 (essays on changes in the sectors), Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2, and in Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 at AS Level. You should know what a business is and what adding value means from the first lesson of this course.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Primary sector"}
Businesses that extract or grow natural resources: farming, fishing, forestry and mining.
:::

:::definition{term="Secondary sector"}
Businesses that turn raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods: manufacturing, processing and construction.
:::

:::definition{term="Tertiary sector"}
Businesses that provide services to consumers and other businesses: retail, transport, banking, insurance, tourism, education and health.
:::

:::definition{term="Quaternary sector"}
Knowledge-based activities: research and development, information technology, consulting and data services.
:::

:::definition{term="Industrialisation"}
The process by which an economy shifts from mainly primary production towards secondary (manufacturing) activity, with machines and factories replacing much hand work.
:::

## The sectors and their firms

| Sector | What happens | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Growing or extracting | Tobacco farm, chrome mine, fishery |
| Secondary | Making and building | Bakery, furniture workshop, construction firm |
| Tertiary | Services | Supermarket, bank, bus company, hotel |
| Quaternary | Knowledge and information | Software company, research laboratory, data analytics firm |

Many firms operate in more than one sector (a farmer who also packs and sells vegetables is both primary and secondary), so classification depends on the main activity.

## The chain of production

The **chain of production** shows the stages by which a product reaches the consumer. In the cotton shirt example the farmer (primary) sells to the ginnery and mill (secondary), the clothing factory (secondary) sells to the shop (tertiary). Value is added at each stage, and each business in the chain depends on the others.

## Industrialisation and its effects

Industrialisation is usually a goal of development because manufacturing can raise output per worker and add more value than raw materials sold unprocessed.

**Effects:** more jobs in factories and in supporting services; higher incomes and exports of finished goods; new technology and skills. **Problems:** pollution and pressure on resources; migration to towns and pressure on housing; decline of some traditional activities.

## Public and private sectors

| Sector | Owned by | Main aim | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Private | Individuals or groups of owners | Profit | Sole traders, partnerships, companies |
| Public | The state or local authority | Service to the public; sometimes profit | State-owned enterprises, municipal water, public hospitals |

Public sector firms may exist because the state wants to provide essential services to everyone, to protect important industries or to prevent private monopoly.

## Changes in the importance of the sectors

As incomes rise, the primary sector usually falls as a share of output and jobs; the secondary sector grows then declines; and the tertiary sector grows. Reasons:

- **Productivity growth in primary and secondary work** means fewer workers are needed.
- **Rising incomes** raise demand for services such as tourism, education and banking more than for basic foods.
- **Technology** creates new services and quaternary activity.
- **Deindustrialisation**: the decline of manufacturing as firms close, move abroad or become more efficient.

Consequences: unemployment in declining sectors if workers cannot retrain; a need to build new skills; a change in what governments tax and support.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: reading sector data]
The table shows employment by sector in a fictional country in 2005 and 2025.

| Sector | 2005 jobs (000) | 2005 share (%) | 2025 jobs (000) | 2025 share (%) | Change (percentage points) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Primary | 1,200 | 46.2 | 900 | 29.0 | −17.2 |
| Secondary | 500 | 19.2 | 450 | 14.5 | −4.7 |
| Tertiary | 800 | 30.8 | 1,500 | 48.4 | +17.6 |
| Quaternary | 100 | 3.8 | 250 | 8.1 | +4.3 |
| Total | 2,600 | 100.0 | 3,100 | 100.0 | |

1. Total jobs in 2005 = 1,200 + 500 + 800 + 100 = 2,600 thousand; in 2025 = 900 + 450 + 1,500 + 250 = 3,100 thousand.
2. Tertiary share in 2025 = 1,500 ÷ 3,100 × 100 = 48.4%; in 2005 = 800 ÷ 2,600 × 100 = 30.8%. The change is 48.4 − 30.8 = 17.6 percentage points.
3. Primary jobs fell by 300 thousand, but the share fell from 46.2% to 29.0% because total jobs also grew.
4. Conclusion: the economy has shifted towards services and knowledge-based work. A school or training college should prepare people for tertiary and quaternary employment, but the secondary sector still employs 450 thousand people, so its decline should not be exaggerated.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, classifying and judging a firm]
**Context.** Tatenda, a fictional owner in Mutare, grows tomatoes, packs them in labelled punnets and sells them from a roadside shop.

**Point.** His business operates in three sectors.

**Application.** Growing is primary; washing, grading and packing are secondary; selling to customers is tertiary.

**Analysis.** By carrying out all three stages he keeps the value added at each stage, but he also needs more finance, more skills and more time than a farmer who sells tomatoes in bulk. Selling in bulk would be simpler and lower risk but would earn less per kilogram.

**Judgement.** He should continue to pack and sell directly if the extra income exceeds the extra cost of labour and packaging; otherwise it would be better to specialise in growing.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Classifying by the product, not the activity"}
A firm that sells tomatoes is tertiary (retail), even though tomatoes are a farm product. Classify by what the firm does.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the primary sector disappears"}
The primary sector falls as a share of output and jobs but remains vital: it supplies food and raw materials.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up public sector and public company"}
The public sector is owned by the state. A public limited company is a private-sector company whose shares may be bought by the public.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (classify, calculate sector shares) and Paper 2 (discuss changes in the sectors); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (identify sectors, explain reasons for change).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/identify*: name the sector. *Describe*: say what the firms in the sector do. *Explain/analyse*: link a change (such as rising incomes) to its effect on a sector. *Discuss/evaluate*: weigh benefits and costs of industrialisation or deindustrialisation and conclude with a justified judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Primary firms extract or grow; secondary firms make and build; tertiary firms provide services; quaternary firms deal in knowledge.
- The chain of production links businesses across sectors, with value added at each stage.
- Industrialisation shifts an economy towards manufacturing, with both benefits and costs.
- The public sector is owned by the state; the private sector by individuals and companies.
- As economies develop, services and knowledge-based work usually grow as a share of output and jobs.
:::
