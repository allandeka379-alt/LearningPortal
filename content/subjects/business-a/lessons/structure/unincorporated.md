## Introduction

Nyasha sells second-hand clothing from a stall in Mbare. She keeps all the money she makes and decides everything herself. When a bad month leaves her unable to pay the wholesaler, the wholesaler can claim her own household property to settle the debt. Her neighbour Farai runs a hardware shop with his brother Tendai; the two share profits, and each is fully responsible for what the other signs for. Across the road, thirty women who grow vegetables together own and run a co-operative that sells their produce to hotels.

These three are **unincorporated** forms of business: the law does not treat the business as separate from its owners. The topic is tested in ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (features and advantages), Paper 2 (essays on choosing a form of ownership), Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2, and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS Level). Companies are the next lesson; franchises and joint ventures follow.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sole trader"}
A business owned and usually run by one person, who receives all the profit and is personally responsible for all the business's debts.
:::

:::definition{term="Partnership"}
A business owned by two or more people (partners) who agree to share the capital, work, profits and risks, usually under a written partnership agreement.
:::

:::definition{term="Co-operative"}
A business owned and run by its members, each of whom normally has one vote regardless of how much they have invested, and which shares profit among members.
:::

:::definition{term="Unlimited liability"}
The owner's responsibility for all the debts of the business, to the extent of their personal property as well as the capital invested in the business.
:::

## The sole trader

**Features:** one owner; easy to start (little paperwork); owner keeps all profit and takes all decisions; unlimited liability.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Total control and quick decisions | Unlimited liability: personal property at risk |
| Keeps all profit | Limited finance (own savings and loans) |
| Few legal formalities and low start-up cost | Heavy workload; business may end if the owner is ill or dies |
| Close contact with customers | Limited skills and ideas; hard to grow |

## The partnership

**Features:** two or more owners; a **partnership agreement** sets capital contributions, duties, profit shares and what happens if a partner leaves; most partners have unlimited liability (a limited partner arrangement exists in some places but is not required here).

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| More capital and skills than one owner | Unlimited liability, including for a partner's mistakes |
| Shared workload and decisions | Disagreements between partners |
| Easy to form with an agreement | Profits must be shared |
| Risks are shared | Limited capital compared with a company; ends or changes when a partner leaves |

## The co-operative

**Features:** member-owned; one member, one vote; profits are shared among members according to the rules (often by use rather than by investment); members may be producers, workers or consumers. In ZIMSEC, kinds include **producer** co-operatives (members sell farm produce together), **consumer** co-operatives (members buy goods together at lower cost) and **worker** co-operatives (members run the business and share the profit).

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Members share costs, giving buying and selling power | Decisions can be slow because members must agree |
| Democratic: one member, one vote | May find it hard to raise large sums of capital |
| Members' liability is often limited, depending on how it is registered | Profit is shared among many members, so each receives little |
| Members have a direct interest in success | Management skills may be weak |

## Unlimited liability and why it matters

Because the law does not separate the owner from the business, a sole trader or partner must pay the business's debts from personal property if the business's assets are not enough. This discourages risk-taking and is a main reason owners move to limited liability forms (see the lesson on companies).

## Starting each form in outline

A sole trader registers a trading name and obtains any licence the trade needs. Partners also draw up a partnership agreement. A co-operative registers under co-operative law with rules for members. Exact legal steps vary, so exam answers refer to registration, licences and a written agreement in general terms.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: sharing partnership profits]
Tendai and Rudo are partners in a hardware shop. Profit for the year is US\$45,000. They have agreed in writing that Rudo, who works full time in the shop, receives a salary of US\$6,000 first, and that the remainder is shared equally.

| Partner | Salary (US\$) | Share of remainder (US\$) | Total (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Tendai | 0 | 19,500 | 19,500 |
| Rudo | 6,000 | 19,500 | 25,500 |
| Total | 6,000 | 39,000 | 45,000 |

1. Remainder after salary = 45,000 − 6,000 = US\$39,000.
2. Equal share = 39,000 ÷ 2 = US\$19,500 each.
3. Rudo's total = 6,000 + 19,500 = US\$25,500; Tendai's total = US\$19,500. The two add up to the profit, US\$45,000.
4. If there were no agreement, partners would normally share profits equally, giving US\$22,500 each. The agreement therefore matters because it rewards Rudo for the extra work.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: unlimited liability]
A fictional sole trader, Chipo, runs a welding workshop in Masvingo. When the workshop fails, its debts are US\$52,000 and its assets can be sold for US\$38,000. Chipo has personal property worth US\$30,000.

1. Shortfall = 52,000 − 38,000 = US\$14,000.
2. Because she has unlimited liability, she must pay the US\$14,000 from her personal property, leaving 30,000 − 14,000 = US\$16,000.
3. If the business had been a limited company with the same figures, the shareholder would lose only the money invested in shares; creditors could not claim her personal property for the shortfall.
4. Conclusion: unlimited liability put US\$14,000 of Chipo's personal wealth at risk, which is a strong reason to consider a company if the business grows or takes on large debts.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, choosing a form]
**Context.** Sipho, a fictional baker in Bulawayo, wants to open a second bakery and needs US\$20,000 more than he has. A friend, Thandeka, offers to put in US\$20,000 and to manage the new site.

**Point.** A partnership could meet Sipho's needs, but it carries risks.

**Application.** Thandeka's money and management skills fill the gaps in finance and workload. Both partners would be fully liable for the business's debts, including those arising from decisions made by the other.

**Analysis.** Sharing finance and work could allow expansion without a bank loan. But a disagreement over prices or hiring could stall decisions, and if the bakery fails each must pay from personal property.

**Judgement.** A partnership is suitable if there is a clear written agreement on duties, profit shares and exit. If Sipho is unwilling to risk personal property, he should consider forming a company instead.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the sole trader works alone"}
A sole trader may employ many workers. "Sole" refers to ownership by one person, not to the number of people working.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a partnership agreement as optional"}
Without an agreement, general rules apply (for example equal sharing of profits), which may be unfair. State that the agreement avoids disputes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that unlimited liability applies to each partner"}
Each partner may be responsible for all the debts, not just their own share.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (state features, advantages and disadvantages; calculate profit shares) and Paper 2 (essay on the most suitable form); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (identify and explain features; judge the most appropriate form in a case).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/identify*: give a feature or advantage. *Explain/describe*: say why, using the case. *Analyse*: build a chain such as "unlimited liability → owner's property at risk → reluctance to borrow → slower growth". *Discuss/evaluate/justify*: compare forms for the specific owner's aims and finish with a reasoned recommendation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sole traders and partners have unlimited liability; co-operative members have one vote each and often limited liability, depending on registration.
- Sole trader: full control and profit, but limited finance and heavy risk.
- Partnership: more capital and skills, but shared control and joint responsibility for debts.
- Co-operatives are member-owned and democratic but can be slow to decide and raise little capital.
- Judge the best form by liability, finance, control, risk and the owner's aims.
:::
