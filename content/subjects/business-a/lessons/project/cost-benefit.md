## Introduction

A village council in a fictional district near Chinhoyi wants to drill a borehole. The drilling and pump will cost money, someone will have to maintain it, and a stretch of grazing land will be lost to the pump house. Against that, families will spend less time walking to a distant river, fewer children will fall ill from unclean water, and women will have time for other work. How can the council decide whether the borehole is worth building? It could compare everything the project costs the community with everything it gives back. That comparison is **cost-benefit analysis (CBA)**.

CBA is a ZIMSEC topic (Business Studies Forms 5-6 and Business Enterprise Skills Form 6). It is tested in structured questions and essays on project decisions, and it supports the enterprise project. It is not a Cambridge topic. You should already know the project cycle; this lesson shows how a project is judged at the proposal stage. Investment appraisal of a firm's own cash flows (payback, ARR, NPV) is a separate topic.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cost-benefit analysis"}
A method of deciding whether a project is worthwhile by listing all its costs and benefits, giving each a money value as far as possible, and comparing the totals. It includes effects on people outside the business, not only the owners' cash flows.
:::

:::definition{term="Economic and social costs and benefits"}
**Economic** (private) costs and benefits are those that can be measured in money by the organisation, such as building cost or sales income. **Social** costs and benefits fall on the community or environment: for example congestion, pollution or loss of land (costs) and jobs, better health or time saved (benefits).
:::

:::definition{term="Net benefit and benefit-cost ratio"}
**Net benefit** = total benefits − total costs. The **benefit-cost ratio** = total benefits ÷ total costs. A project is worthwhile when net benefit is positive or the ratio is above 1.
:::

:::definition{term="Discounting"}
Reducing the value of future costs and benefits because money received or spent later is worth less than money today. It is used in careful CBA, but is only outlined in this syllabus.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC detail"}
This lesson is ZIMSEC only (Business Studies Forms 5-6, Business Enterprise Skills Form 6). The syllabus asks for the listing and valuing of costs and benefits, comparing the totals, and the advantages and limitations of CBA with a local project such as a clinic, borehole, road or factory. The calculation of a firm's investment return is in the lessons on payback, ARR and NPV.
:::

## Steps in a cost-benefit analysis

1. **Define the project** and the period over which it will be judged.
2. **List the costs**: capital (building, equipment), running costs, and social or environmental costs.
3. **List the benefits**: income or savings, and social benefits such as health or time saved.
4. **Value each item in money**: market prices where they exist; estimates (shadow values) where they do not, such as the value of time saved or a life saved.
5. **Add up** total costs and total benefits.
6. **Compare**: net benefit and benefit-cost ratio; decide, or rank projects.
7. **Test the result**: change the main assumptions to see whether the decision changes (sensitivity).

## Examples of costs and benefits

| | Economic | Social |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Costs | Building, equipment, wages, maintenance | Noise, congestion, pollution, loss of farmland, displaced families |
| Benefits | Sales, savings in cost, extra output | New jobs, better health, shorter journey times, safer roads, new skills |

## Advantages of CBA

- It looks at the whole community, not only the owners.
- It forces planners to list all effects, including those that are easily forgotten.
- It gives a common measure (money) for comparing different projects.
- It makes decisions easier to explain and justify to funders and the public.

## Limitations of CBA

- **Valuing social effects is difficult**: how much is a healthy child or a clean river worth? Estimates differ.
- **Forecasts are uncertain**: costs may overrun and benefits may not arrive as expected.
- **Timing**: benefits that come many years later are worth less today, and a simple total ignores this unless costs and benefits are discounted.
- **Who gains and who loses** is hidden in a total: one group may bear the costs while another gains.
- **Bias**: planners who favour a project may overstate benefits.
- It can be slow and costly for small projects.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: a cost-benefit analysis of a borehole]
The council values a borehole over ten years. Maintenance is US\$900 a year. Households save time worth US\$4,200 a year, and fewer cases of illness are worth US\$1,800 a year. The grazing land lost is valued at US\$6,000. (All values are estimates invented for practice.)

| Borehole project over 10 years | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| **Costs** | |
| Drilling and pump (once) | 18,000 |
| Maintenance (US\$900 a year for 10 years) | 9,000 |
| Loss of grazing land (once, social cost) | 6,000 |
| Total costs | 33,000 |
| **Benefits** | |
| Household time saved (US\$4,200 a year for 10 years) | 42,000 |
| Fewer cases of illness (US\$1,800 a year for 10 years) | 18,000 |
| Total benefits | 60,000 |
| Net benefit (benefits − costs) | 27,000 |

**Working.** Total costs = 18,000 + 9,000 + 6,000 = US\$33,000. Total benefits = 42,000 + 18,000 = US\$60,000. Net benefit = 60,000 − 33,000 = US\$27,000. Benefit-cost ratio = 60,000 ÷ 33,000 = 1.82 (to two decimal places), so each dollar of cost brings about US\$1.82 of benefit.

**Interpretation.** The project is worthwhile on these figures. But most of the benefit comes from the value placed on time and health, which are estimates. If the time saved were valued at only US\$2,000 a year, benefits would fall to 20,000 + 18,000 = US\$38,000 and the net benefit to US\$5,000, so the decision is sensitive to the valuation. The council should also check who pays for the grazing land loss.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: comparing two projects and judging the limits of the numbers]
A district can fund only one of two projects. (The values are invented for practice.)

| | Clinic (US\$) | Road upgrade (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Total costs | 95,000 | 120,000 |
| Total benefits | 140,000 | 150,000 |
| Net benefit | 45,000 | 30,000 |
| Benefit-cost ratio | 1.47 | 1.25 |

**Working.** Clinic: net benefit = 140,000 − 95,000 = US\$45,000; ratio = 140,000 ÷ 95,000 = 1.47. Road: net benefit = 150,000 − 120,000 = US\$30,000; ratio = 150,000 ÷ 120,000 = 1.25.

**Point.** The clinic is better on both measures, although the road has the larger total benefits.

**Application.** The clinic gives US\$15,000 more net benefit and more benefit for each dollar spent. The road costs more, so its larger benefit is outweighed.

**Analysis.** The figures do not show who gains. A clinic may serve only the villages near it, while the road may link several districts and help trade. Benefits of the road such as lower prices for farmers may be hard to value and may be underestimated. The clinic's benefits depend on staff and medicines being available, which the budget may not include.

**Judgement.** On the numbers, the clinic should come first. But the council should check the estimates, test the result with lower benefit values and consider who gains and who loses, before making a final choice. If the road is essential to open markets, it could be phased in later.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Counting only the costs and benefits to the owners"}
CBA includes social costs and benefits such as jobs, health and environmental harm. A private cash-flow list is not a full CBA.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the totals as exact facts"}
Many values are estimates. A good answer recognises the uncertainty and says how the decision might change if the estimates change.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the time period"}
Costs and benefits arise over time. Say what period is used, and note that a simple total ignores the fact that benefits later in time are worth less than benefits now.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 (Forms 5-6) and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 (Form 6): classifying costs and benefits, calculating total costs, benefits and net benefit, and discussing advantages and limitations. It is not tested in Cambridge papers.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/list*: name costs or benefits and say whether they are economic or social. *Calculate*: show totals and net benefit with units. *Explain/describe*: say why a cost or benefit arises. *Discuss/evaluate*: weigh the totals against the difficulty of valuing social effects and give a judgement on whether the project should go ahead.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- CBA lists and values all costs and benefits of a project, including social and environmental ones, and compares totals.
- Net benefit = total benefits − total costs; benefit-cost ratio = total benefits ÷ total costs; a project is worthwhile if net benefit is positive or the ratio exceeds 1.
- Economic costs and benefits can be measured in money by the organisation; social ones fall on the community and are harder to value.
- Advantages: whole-community view, forces a full list, common measure; limitations: valuing social effects, uncertain forecasts, timing and discounting, hidden winners and losers.
- Test the result with different assumptions before deciding.
:::
