## Introduction

Form 5 students at a fictional school in Masvingo are asked to run an enterprise project during the year. A group list three ideas: keeping layers (egg-laying hens), growing mushrooms and sewing school uniforms. They have a small budget, six months and different skills. They must decide which idea to follow, write it up for their teacher and a possible lender, plan what is done each month, carry it out, and finally judge whether the project worked.

This is the logic of **project formulation**, and it is the heart of the practical enterprise project in both ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills. The topic is tested in ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2 (Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills) and assessed through the practical project in continuous assessment. It is not a Cambridge topic. You should already know what a business plan contains; the business plan itself is in another lesson, and network analysis is in the lesson on critical path analysis.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Project"}
A one-off, temporary undertaking with a clear objective, a start and an end date, a budget and a set of resources, carried out to produce a particular result.
:::

:::definition{term="Project proposal"}
A written document that presents a project idea to those who must approve or fund it, explaining the objective, the need, the activities, the resources, the cost, the expected benefits and the risks.
:::

:::definition{term="Project plan"}
A detailed statement of what will be done, by whom, when and at what cost, including activities in order, deadlines, resources and a budget.
:::

:::definition{term="Project cycle"}
The stages through which a project passes: identification, formulation (proposal and plan), implementation and evaluation.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC detail"}
This lesson is ZIMSEC only (Business Studies Forms 5-6; Business Enterprise Skills Forms 5-6). The Business Enterprise Skills practical project is part of continuous assessment and is not simulated here; the questions test the knowledge that supports it. :::

## The stages of the project cycle

1. **Identification**: spotting a need or opportunity and listing project ideas, from the market, the community or the group's own skills.
2. **Formulation**: choosing the best idea, writing the **proposal**, and drawing up the **plan** and **budget**.
3. **Implementation**: acquiring resources, carrying out the activities, supervising, keeping records and controlling cost and time.
4. **Evaluation**: comparing results with the objective, finding what went well and what did not, and recording lessons.

## Identifying and choosing a project

Ideas may come from observing community needs, from market research, from the group's skills or resources, from existing businesses and from problems customers mention. Because resources are limited, the group must **choose**. Common criteria are:

- **Demand**: will enough customers buy?
- **Cost**: how much money is needed to start?
- **Skills**: does the team have the knowledge to do it well?
- **Resources and timing**: are materials, land, equipment and time available, and does the season suit it?
- **Risk**: what could go wrong, and how bad would it be?
- **Fit with objectives**: does it meet the group's aims (profit, learning, service)?

A **scoring matrix** gives each idea a score for every criterion, weights the criteria by importance and totals the results. It makes the decision clearer, but the weights and scores are judgements.

## The proposal and the plan

A proposal normally includes: the project title and objective; the problem or need; the activities; the resources and costs; the people responsible; the timetable; expected income and benefits; risks and how they will be managed; and how success will be measured. The plan sets out the order of activities and the **budget**, and often includes a **contingency** (a reserve for unexpected cost).

## Implementation and monitoring

Implementation means obtaining materials, organising tasks, supervising the team, recording income and spending, and keeping to the timetable. Monitoring compares progress with the plan and allows quick corrections. Good records make evaluation possible.

## Evaluation

Evaluation asks: Did the project meet its objective? Was it completed on time and within budget? What was the profit or benefit? What were the problems and their causes? What would the team do differently? The result is a report that can guide future projects.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: choosing a project with a scoring matrix]
The Masvingo group scores three ideas from 1 (poor) to 5 (best) against four weighted criteria. For cost and risk, a higher score means cheaper or less risky. (The scores and weights are invented.)

| Criterion | Weight (%) | Layers (egg) project | Mushroom project | School uniform project |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Local demand | 40 | 4 | 3 | 5 |
| Start-up cost (higher score = cheaper) | 25 | 2 | 4 | 3 |
| Skills the team has | 20 | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| Risk (higher score = lower risk) | 15 | 3 | 3 | 4 |
| Weighted score (out of 5) | 100 | 3.15 | 3.45 | 3.75 |

**Working.** Weighted score = sum of (score × weight) ÷ 100. For the school uniform project: (5 × 40 + 3 × 25 + 2 × 20 + 4 × 15) ÷ 100 = (200 + 75 + 40 + 60) ÷ 100 = 3.75. For the mushroom project: (3 × 40 + 4 × 25 + 4 × 20 + 3 × 15) ÷ 100 = 3.45, and for layers: (4 × 40 + 2 × 25 + 3 × 20 + 3 × 15) ÷ 100 = 3.15.

**Interpretation.** The uniform project scores highest because demand is strongest, but it also has the weakest skills score (2). If the group put a higher weight on skills, mushrooms could win. The matrix helps the group to explain its choice, but the group should check the demand figure with a short survey before committing.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a project budget and its evaluation]
The group chooses the uniform project. Its budget is below. It includes a contingency of 10% of the subtotal.

| Budget item | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Cloth and thread | 640 |
| Sewing machine hire | 280 |
| Transport | 150 |
| Advertising | 130 |
| Subtotal | 1,200 |
| Contingency (10%) | 120 |
| Total project budget | 1,320 |

**Working.** Subtotal = 640 + 280 + 150 + 130 = US\$1,200. Contingency = 10% of 1,200 = US\$120. Total = US\$1,320.

**Implementation and evaluation.** After four months, the group has sold 220 uniforms for a total of US\$2,420 and spent US\$1,290 on the project. Profit = 2,420 − 1,290 = US\$1,130. Spending stayed within the budget of US\$1,320, so the group saved US\$30, and the profit was 1,130 ÷ 1,290 × 100 = 87.6% of the money spent (to one decimal place).

**Judgement.** The project met its objective of a profit and stayed within budget. The group should record that late delivery of cloth delayed the first orders by two weeks, and next time order materials earlier. The sales were achieved in a season of high demand, so the same return should not be assumed for the rest of the year.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the project proposal with the project plan"}
The proposal persuades others to approve the project; the plan sets out how the project will be carried out. Both are written before implementation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Skipping evaluation"}
Evaluation is part of the project cycle. State whether the objective, time and budget were met, and what lessons were learnt.
:::

:::mistake{title="Choosing a project on one criterion only"}
A project with high demand but no skills or money will fail. Use several criteria and refer to the resources of the group.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2: stages of the project cycle, choosing a project, the proposal and plan, and evaluation, often in structured questions or a case study. The practical project is assessed in continuous assessment, not in the written papers.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/list*: name the stages or contents of a proposal. *Outline/describe*: say what happens at each stage and relate it to the project in the question. *Explain*: give a reason a stage matters. *Discuss/evaluate*: weigh ideas or stages against the group's resources and conclude with a justified choice.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A project is a one-off undertaking with a clear objective, time limit, budget and resources.
- The project cycle runs from identification through formulation (proposal and plan) and implementation to evaluation.
- Project ideas are chosen against demand, cost, skills, resources and timing, and risk; a scoring matrix helps, but weights are judgements.
- A proposal presents the idea for approval; a plan sets activities, timetable and budget, including a contingency.
- Evaluation compares results with the objective, time and budget and records lessons for the future.
:::
