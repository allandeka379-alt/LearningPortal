## Introduction

A fictional firm in Gweru makes steel roof brackets. It sells some to builders' merchants, who resell them to households, and others directly to a roofing contractor who buys 20,000 a month. A second firm bakes birthday cakes for families in Gweru and takes orders on a messaging app. Both firms "have customers", but the customers, the buying decisions and the marketing needed are very different. To market well, a business first needs to understand **which market it is in**, how big it is, how fast it is growing and what share the business holds.

This lesson applies to ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **AS**. Calculations of market share and growth are examined on all papers. Elasticity and segmentation have separate lessons.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |

Industrial markets are sometimes called producer or business markets. The classification of goods as durable and non-durable is common to both.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Market"}
Any arrangement in which buyers and sellers of a good or service come into contact to trade.
:::

:::definition{term="Market share"}
A firm's sales as a percentage of the total sales of the market, in value or volume.
:::

:::definition{term="Market growth"}
The percentage change in the total sales of a market over a period.
:::

:::definition{term="Market orientation"}
A business approach in which products are designed after finding out what customers want, rather than selling what the firm already makes.
:::

## Types of market

### Consumer and industrial markets

| Feature | Consumer market (B2C) | Industrial market (B2B) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buyers | Households buying for their own use | Firms, farms or government buying for use in production or resale |
| Number of buyers | Very many | Fewer, often larger orders |
| Buying decision | Quick; influenced by price, image, fashion | Careful; specification, quality, reliability, after-sales and credit terms |
| Promotion | Mass advertising, social media, discounts | Personal selling, trade fairs, tenders, catalogues |
| Relationships | Often short-term | Often long-term contracts; personal contact |
| Demand | Depends on household income and taste | Often derived from demand for the final product |

### Local, national and international markets

A **local** market serves one town or area (a Gweru bakery); a **national** market covers a whole country; an **international** (global, in BES: regional or global) market crosses borders. Moving to a wider market gives more customers and economies of scale, but also more competitors, transport costs, regulations and exchange-rate risk. Markets may also be **physical** (a shop or a street market) or **virtual** (online shops and apps); a virtual market removes the limit of distance but depends on internet access and delivery.

### Classification of products

- **Consumer goods**: bought by households, either **durable** (used many times: furniture, a fridge) or **non-durable** (used up: bread, soap), and **services** (haircuts, banking).
- **Producer (capital) goods**: used by firms to make other goods (machinery, tools, raw materials).

Marketing differs by type: a durable good is bought rarely, so the buyer compares carefully; a non-durable good is bought often, so brand and availability matter.

## Orientation

A **product-orientated** firm designs what it can make and tries to sell it. A **market-orientated** (customer-orientated) firm researches what customers want, then designs the product and the mix. A market orientation is more likely to match demand but costs more to research and may react slowly to change. In stable markets with scarce supply a product orientation can still succeed. The marketing orientations are compared in the lesson on the role of marketing.

## Market size, competitors and location (ZIMSEC detail)

When analysing a market, a business finds: its **size** (value or volume, and the number of customers); its **growth** (rising, stable, falling); its **competitors** (how many, how strong, what they sell); and its **location** (where the customers are, which affects distribution and promotion). A market that is small but growing, with few competitors, may be a better opportunity than a large market with many strong rivals.

## Measuring market share and growth

| Measure | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Market share (%) | Firm's sales ÷ total market sales × 100 |
| Market growth (%) | (New market sales − old market sales) ÷ old market sales × 100 |
| Firm's sales growth (%) | (New firm sales − old firm sales) ÷ old firm sales × 100 |

Use the same time period and the same measure (value or volume) for the firm and the market. Market share rises when the firm grows **faster** than the market and falls when the market grows faster than the firm.

**Implications.** A high share can mean lower unit costs (economies of scale), power with suppliers and a strong brand. A rising share suggests the mix works; a falling share in a growing market is a warning even if sales are rising. A low share in a fast-growing market may be a chance to expand but also a threat if rivals grow faster.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: market share and growth]
**Context.** A fictional furniture firm in Mutare sells in a market that grows from US\$4,000,000 in 2025 to US\$4,400,000 in 2026. The firm's sales rise from US\$560,000 to US\$594,000.

| Measure | 2025 | 2026 | Change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Market sales (US\$) | 4,000,000 | 4,400,000 | 10% |
| Firm sales (US\$) | 560,000 | 594,000 | 6.1% |
| Market share | 14.0% | 13.5% | −0.5 percentage points |

1. Share 2025 = 560,000 ÷ 4,000,000 × 100 = 14.0%. Share 2026 = 594,000 ÷ 4,400,000 × 100 = 13.5%.
2. Market growth = 400,000 ÷ 4,000,000 × 100 = 10.0%. Firm growth = 34,000 ÷ 560,000 × 100 = 6.1% (to one decimal place).
3. To keep a 14% share the firm needed 14% × 4,400,000 = US\$616,000, which is US\$22,000 more than it achieved.
4. **Judgement.** Sales rose, but the share fell because the market grew faster. The firm is losing ground and should investigate its mix (price, promotion, distribution) before rivals take more customers.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, choosing the right marketing approach]
**Context.** The fictional steel-bracket firm in the introduction sells to two markets: builders' merchants and households through a hardware shop.

| Aspect | Builders' merchants (industrial market) | Households (consumer market) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buyers | Few, large, repeat | Many, small |
| Decision | Based on price per thousand, quality and delivery | Based on price and availability in a shop |
| Best promotion | Personal visits, a price list and credit terms | Packaging and shelf displays |

**Point.** The two markets need different marketing mixes.

**Application.** A merchant who orders 20,000 brackets wants a reliable delivery date and a discount for volume, which a salesperson can arrange. A household buying six brackets will pick the brand on the shelf.

**Analysis.** Spending on mass advertising to households would reach few merchants and cost much; spending on a sales representative would give a good return from merchants but not from households.

**Judgement.** The firm should concentrate personal selling on the industrial market, where each customer is worth much more, and keep consumer marketing simple and cheap.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing market share with sales"}
Sales are an amount of money; market share is a **percentage** of the whole market. A firm's sales can rise while its share falls.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up percentage change and percentage points"}
A share that moves from 14% to 13.5% has fallen by 0.5 *percentage points*, which is about 3.6% of its original value. Say which you mean.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using different periods or measures"}
Do not divide annual firm sales by monthly market sales, or a volume figure by a value figure.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *state* types of market, *calculate* share and *describe* or *discuss* the effect of market size and competitors. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 add *explain* and *assess* the implications of a change in share or growth. Show the formula, the substitution and the answer with the unit (%); then comment on what the change means for the business. For an *assess* answer, give a judgement with a condition, for example whether the market is growing and whether rivals are gaining.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Markets differ by buyer (consumer and industrial), by area (local, national, international) and by form (physical and virtual).
- Industrial buyers are fewer, buy larger quantities, decide carefully and expect personal service.
- Goods can be durable, non-durable, services or producer goods, and the marketing differs.
- Market share = firm's sales ÷ market sales × 100; market growth = change in market sales ÷ old sales × 100.
- A firm's share falls if the market grows faster than the firm, even when its sales rise.
:::
