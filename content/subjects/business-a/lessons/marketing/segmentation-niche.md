## Introduction

A fictional gym in Harare opens with the slogan "A gym for everyone". After six months it has few members. Students find it too expensive, young professionals find it too crowded after work and retired people find the music too loud and the instructors unhelpful. A rival gym down the road decides instead to serve one group only: people over 60, with morning classes, gentle equipment and a quiet room. It is small, but it is full. The first gym tried to sell to the whole market and satisfied almost nobody; the second divided the market and **chose a segment**.

This lesson applies to ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **AS**. The vocabulary is slightly different: ZIMSEC uses *target marketing*, *product positioning* and *differentiation*; Cambridge uses *mass market*, *niche market*, *segmentation*, *targeting* and *positioning*. The ideas are the same.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |
| Market segmentation; target marketing; product positioning; differentiation | Mass marketing; niche marketing; market segmentation; targeting; positioning |

Both boards expect you to choose a segment for a stated product and justify it.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Market segmentation"}
Dividing a market into groups of customers who share similar needs, characteristics or behaviour, so that each group can be served with a suitable marketing mix.
:::

:::definition{term="Mass marketing"}
Selling a standard product to the whole market, with the same marketing mix for all customers.
:::

:::definition{term="Niche marketing"}
Aiming a specialised product at a small, clearly defined part of a larger market.
:::

:::definition{term="Positioning"}
The image of a product in customers' minds compared with competing products, created by the marketing mix.
:::

## Mass and niche markets

| Feature | Mass marketing | Niche marketing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customers | Large, varied market | Small, specific group |
| Product | Standard, not tailored | Specialised, tailored |
| Sales volume | High | Low |
| Price | Often low, from economies of scale | Often higher: customers pay for a special fit |
| Competition | Strong; many rivals | Fewer rivals, though large firms may enter |
| Promotion | Mass media | Targeted media |
| Risk | Rivals copy and compete on price | Small market; a change in taste or one customer can ruin it |

**Advantages of mass marketing:** large sales and economies of scale, lower unit cost, a wide reach. **Disadvantages:** little loyalty, fierce price competition and costly promotion.

**Advantages of niche marketing:** less competition, higher prices and margins, closer customer loyalty, and a small firm can survive next to large ones. **Disadvantages:** limited growth, dependence on one group, and the danger that a larger rival will notice the niche and move in.

## Segmenting a market

Segmentation divides a market using one or more criteria:

| Criteria | Basis | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Geographic | Where customers live | Urban or rural; region; climate; different towns |
| Demographic | Population characteristics | Age; sex; income; occupation; family size; education |
| Psychographic | Lifestyle, values and attitudes | Health-conscious; status-seekers; environmentally concerned |
| Behavioural | How customers use or respond to the product | Heavy or light users; loyalty; occasions; benefits sought |

Firms often **combine** criteria, for example "women aged 25 to 40 in towns who buy organic food weekly".

**Advantages of segmentation:** the mix fits customers more closely; resources are not wasted on uninterested groups; unmet needs are found; it can raise loyalty and sales. **Disadvantages:** costs of research and of several mixes; smaller production runs; segments may be too small or may overlap; customers' behaviour may change.

### Conditions for a good segment

A segment is worth serving if it is **measurable** (its size is known), **reachable** (promotion and distribution can get to it), **large enough** to be profitable, and **different** in its response to the mix.

## Targeting, positioning and differentiation

**Targeting** (target marketing) is the choice of which segment or segments to serve. A firm can target one segment (concentrated), several segments with different mixes (differentiated), or the whole market (undifferentiated, mass). **Positioning** places the product in the customer's mind: cheap and practical, premium and exclusive, local and traditional. **Differentiation** makes the product different from competitors in a way customers value, such as design, quality, service or brand. The firm should check that the positioning chosen is **credible**: a low-quality product cannot be positioned as luxury for long.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: choosing a segment]
**Context.** A fictional gym in Harare considers three segments. The table shows the estimates.

| Segment | People in area | Expected sign-up rate | Members | Monthly fee (US\$) | Annual revenue (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Students (18-24) | 6,000 | 5% | 300 | 15 | 54,000 |
| Young professionals (25-40) | 4,000 | 10% | 400 | 30 | 144,000 |
| Retired people (60+) | 1,500 | 20% | 300 | 20 | 72,000 |

1. Members = people × sign-up rate. For students: 6,000 × 5% = 300.
2. Annual revenue = members × monthly fee × 12. For young professionals: 400 × 30 × 12 = US\$144,000.
3. Young professionals give the highest revenue (US\$144,000), more than retired people (US\$72,000) and students (US\$54,000).
4. **Judgement.** Revenue is not the only test. Young professionals may already be served by competing gyms and may need late opening hours and expensive equipment. Retired people are the smallest segment but have the highest sign-up rate, so promotion would be cheap. The gym should choose young professionals if it can afford the equipment and the promotion, but could start with retired people to reduce risk.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, niche marketing]
**Context.** A fictional bakery in Bulawayo bakes only gluten-free and diabetic-friendly bread, sold in four shops and through a delivery service. It charges 40% more than ordinary bread. A large supermarket chain announces its own gluten-free loaf.

**Point.** The bakery is a niche marketer; its high prices depend on special needs.

**Application.** Its customers are people with medical needs who cannot buy ordinary bread, so they accept the higher price. The supermarket will compete for these customers with a cheaper loaf.

**Analysis.** If the supermarket loaf is cheaper but of lower quality, the bakery can keep customers who value taste and trust. If it is just as good, the bakery will lose the customers who are price-sensitive, and its small scale gives it no cost advantage.

**Judgement.** The bakery should strengthen its **differentiation** (freshness, advice from a dietician, home delivery) and build loyalty with a customer database, rather than cut its price. The strategy works only while customers see a real difference, so the bakery should review it each year.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing niche with small"}
A niche market is a **specialised** part of a bigger market, not merely a small firm. A small firm can still sell to a mass market.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming a criterion without applying it"}
"Demographic" scores only if you say *which* feature (age, income) and *why* it matters for this product.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the cost side"}
Segmentation fits the mix to customers but costs more in research, design and promotion. A good evaluation weighs both.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *state*, *outline* or *describe* the criteria and *discuss* targeting and positioning. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *identify* a segment, *explain* the advantages of niche marketing or *assess* whether a firm should segment. Always tie the criterion to the product in the case. For *assess* or *evaluate*, weigh the benefit of a closer fit against the cost and size of the segment, then give a judgement with a condition such as the firm's resources or the response of competitors.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mass marketing sells one standard mix to all; niche marketing serves a small, specialised group with a tailored mix.
- Markets can be segmented geographically, demographically, psychographically and by behaviour; criteria are often combined.
- A good segment is measurable, reachable, large enough and different in response.
- Targeting chooses the segment, positioning creates an image and differentiation sets the product apart.
- Niche marketing gives less competition and higher margins but limits growth and carries risk.
:::
