## Introduction

Sipho runs a fictional dairy in Bulawayo that sells fresh milk and yoghurt to shops and households. For years he produced as much as his cows allowed and sold whatever he could. Then a new competitor opened and his sales fell. Sipho began to ask customers what they wanted: smaller bottles, yoghurt without added sugar, delivery to the door. He also kept a list of regular customers and gave a free litre of milk to anyone who had bought every week for three months. His sales recovered. What changed was not the milk; it was that the business began to start from the **customer**.

This lesson applies to ZIMSEC Business Studies (Form 5), Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **AS** (Papers 1 and 2). ## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Marketing"}
The management process of identifying, anticipating and satisfying customers' needs and wants profitably.
:::

:::definition{term="E-marketing"}
Marketing carried out through electronic media such as websites, email, social media and mobile phones.
:::

:::definition{term="Marketing objective"}
A specific, measurable target for the marketing function, set to help the business reach its corporate objectives.
:::

:::definition{term="Customer relationship marketing (CRM)"}
Marketing that aims to build a long-term, profitable relationship with individual customers, using information about them, so that they buy again and stay loyal.
:::

## The role of marketing

Marketing connects the business to its market. It:

- finds out what customers want (market research) and how that is changing;
- helps to design the product and decides its price, promotion and distribution (the marketing mix);
- creates demand and builds the brand;
- keeps customers satisfied and loyal after the sale;
- provides information on competitors and trends to the rest of the business.

For an enterprise the purpose of marketing is to make a profit by meeting needs better than competitors, not simply to advertise.

### Links with other functions

| Function | How marketing depends on it | How it depends on marketing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Finance | Needs a marketing budget; prices must cover costs | Sales forecasts are used for the cash flow forecast and budgets |
| Operations | Must make the product in the quantity, quality and time promised | Needs forecasts of demand to plan production and inventory (stock) |
| Human resources | Staff must be trained to serve customers well | Needs to know the skills and numbers required for the sales plan |

If marketing promises a delivery date that operations cannot meet, customers are lost. If marketing forecasts too high a demand, inventory piles up and finance suffers. The functions must work together to the same corporate objective.

## Marketing objectives

Marketing objectives are **specific and measurable**, and they follow from the **corporate objectives** (for example, to double profit in three years). Typical marketing objectives:

- increase **sales** (value or volume);
- increase **market share**;
- launch a **new product** or enter a **new market**;
- raise **brand awareness** or improve the brand's image;
- improve **customer retention** and satisfaction.

A marketing objective is **not** the same as the corporate objective: it is the marketing part of the plan. For example, if the corporate objective is a profit of US\$50,000, the marketing objective may be a 15% share of the town market; the objective for operations may be a cost target.

### Business strategy and the objective

Marketing objectives must be **consistent** with the business's resources. Growing market share quickly may need heavy promotion spending that finance cannot afford, or more output than operations can supply. They are also reviewed against results: if the target was missed, the managers ask whether the objective, the market or the mix was wrong.

## Marketing orientations

| Orientation | The business starts from | Typical when | Strength | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Production (product) | What it can make | Demand is greater than supply | Low costs, efficient output | May make what no one wants |
| Sales | Selling what it already makes, by persuasion | Competition rises and supply is large | Raises sales in the short term | Ignores customers' needs; poor loyalty |
| Marketing (market, customer) | What customers want, found through research | Competitive, informed markets | Products fit demand; loyal customers | Research costs; customers may not know what they want |

The marketing orientation is the usual modern approach, though not every firm needs it. A producer of a scarce product, or a monopoly, may do well with a production orientation. The correct answer to an exam question depends on the **market** in the case.

## Customer relationship marketing (CRM)

**Aims:** to keep existing customers, increase the value of each customer over time (repeat purchases, extra products) and turn satisfied customers into advocates who recommend the business.

**Tools:**

- a **customer database** recording purchases and preferences;
- **loyalty cards** and rewards for repeat buying;
- **personal contact**: birthday messages, regular offers by text or email;
- good **after-sales service** and a quick complaints system;
- **social media** groups and feedback surveys.

**Benefits:** keeping a customer is usually cheaper than winning a new one; repeat customers spend more and recommend others; information helps to design products; sales are more stable.

**Costs and limits:** the cost of the database, rewards and staff time; discounts may reduce the profit per sale; data must be kept secure and customers' privacy respected; it works best where customers buy often.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: a marketing objective linked to a corporate objective]
**Context.** Sipho's fictional dairy has a corporate objective to raise annual profit. The market for dairy products in Bulawayo is worth US\$1,500,000 a year (assume it stays constant). The dairy sells US\$180,000 a year. Sipho sets a marketing objective: raise market share to 15% in two years.

| Item | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Current share | 180,000 ÷ 1,500,000 × 100 | 12% |
| Target sales | 15% × 1,500,000 | 225,000 |
| Extra sales | 225,000 − 180,000 | 45,000 |
| Percentage rise | 45,000 ÷ 180,000 × 100 | 25% |

1. The dairy needs sales of US\$225,000, which is US\$45,000 (25%) more than now.
2. Operations must raise output by about a quarter; finance must pay for more milk and distribution; human resources may need another driver.
3. **Judgement.** The objective is measurable and fits the corporate objective, but it depends on cows' output and on competitors' reactions. A 25% rise over two years is realistic only if capacity can expand.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, a loyalty scheme]
**Context.** Sipho introduces a loyalty card. The scheme costs US\$6,000 a year (cards, a simple database and rewards). He estimates that it keeps 150 customers who would otherwise leave. Each spends US\$30 a month for 12 months, and the dairy earns a 25% gross profit on sales.

| Item | Calculation | Result (US\$) |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Sales kept per customer | 30 × 12 | 360 |
| Sales kept in total | 360 × 150 | 54,000 |
| Gross profit on kept sales | 25% × 54,000 | 13,500 |
| Cost of the scheme |  | 6,000 |
| Net benefit | 13,500 − 6,000 | 7,500 |

**Point.** Customer relationship marketing can pay for itself by keeping customers.

**Application.** On the estimates, the scheme earns US\$13,500 of gross profit for a cost of US\$6,000.

**Analysis.** The figure depends on the 150 customers being kept *because of* the scheme. If only 60 are kept, gross profit is US\$5,400 and the scheme loses US\$600. The benefits also include information about customers that helps to plan production.

**Judgement.** The scheme is worth trying if the estimate is reasonable, but Sipho should check after a year how many members really stayed.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Equating marketing with advertising"}
Advertising is only one part of promotion, which is one part of the mix. Marketing starts with research and covers the product, price and place.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing marketing and corporate objectives"}
"Increase profit" is a corporate objective. "Gain 15% of the market by December 2027" is a marketing objective that helps to achieve it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the marketing orientation is always best"}
It depends on the market. A firm in a market with few competitors or scarce supply may do well with a product or sales orientation, and research has costs.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 test the meaning and purpose of marketing (*state*, *outline*, *describe*); Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 test the role of marketing, objectives, orientations and CRM (*identify*, *explain*, *analyse*, *assess*). For an *explain* answer, link the point to the case: say what the objective or tool does for *this* business. For an *assess* or *evaluate* answer on CRM or an orientation, weigh benefits against costs and give a judgement with a condition (market type, size of the business, how often customers buy). Calculations on market share are covered in the lesson on markets.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Marketing identifies, anticipates and satisfies customer needs profitably; it depends on and informs finance, operations and human resources.
- Marketing objectives are specific and measurable and must support the corporate objectives and fit the resources.
- Orientations: production (what can be made), sales (selling what is made) and marketing (what customers want).
- CRM builds long-term customer relationships through databases, loyalty schemes and good service.
- CRM keeps customers more cheaply than winning new ones but has costs and needs reliable data.
:::
