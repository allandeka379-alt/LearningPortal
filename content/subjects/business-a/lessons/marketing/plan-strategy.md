## Introduction

A fictional furniture maker in Bulawayo has a good year. The owner decides to "do more marketing" and quickly buys a radio slot, orders new brochures and offers a discount in two shops. Nobody has written down what the money is for. Sales rise a little, but the radio advertisement targets young families while the furniture is aimed at offices, the discount is cancelled for one shop by mistake and the brochures promise delivery in a week, which the workshop cannot meet. The owner spent US\$20,000 and cannot say what it achieved. A **marketing plan** and a **marketing strategy** would have linked the money to a clear objective, a target market and a coordinated mix.

This lesson is examined in **Cambridge A Level** (Papers 3 and 4); it is Cambridge only. ZIMSEC students will find that the ideas support essays on marketing objectives and the marketing mix. The lesson on the role of marketing and objectives is the foundation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Marketing plan"}
A detailed written document setting out marketing objectives, the target market, the actions, the marketing mix, the budget and the timetable for a period, usually a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Marketing strategy"}
A long-term approach for achieving the marketing objectives, deciding which markets to compete in, which products to offer and how to position and differentiate them.
:::

:::definition{term="Marketing tactics"}
The short-term actions that carry out the strategy, such as a particular promotion or price change.
:::

## The marketing plan

A plan usually contains:

| Section | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Situation analysis | Where the business is now: market research, sales, share, competitors, strengths and weaknesses |
| Objectives | Specific, measurable targets, linked to corporate objectives (for example +20% sales) |
| Target market | Segments to be served and the positioning |
| Marketing mix | Product, price, promotion and place decisions |
| Resources and budget | Money, people, equipment and time needed |
| Timetable and responsibilities | Who does what and by when |
| Control and review | Measures of success, monitoring and corrective action |

**Benefits:** it forces managers to think and to set clear goals; coordinates the mix and the departments; gives a basis for the budget and for judging results; helps to spot problems early; and communicates the plan to staff and lenders.

**Limitations:** it costs time and money to prepare; it is based on forecasts, which may be wrong; it can be **inflexible** if the market changes; the budget may be too small; and a detailed plan may never be followed.

## Marketing strategy

A **strategy** is the broader approach behind the plan. It must be **consistent** with:

- the **business**: its corporate objectives, resources, skills and image;
- the **product**: its stage in the life cycle and its strengths;
- the **market**: customers' needs, competitors' actions and the market's growth.

For example, a small firm with limited cash and a niche product should not copy the mass-market promotion strategy of a large rival. A strategy is **coordinated** when the four Ps point the same way: a premium positioning needs a quality product, a high price, selective outlets and refined promotion. A strategy aimed at *increasing market share* will use different tools (lower prices, wide distribution, heavy promotion) from one aimed at *building the brand* (quality, image advertising, exclusive outlets). Strategy is reviewed when objectives are missed or conditions change.

**Strategy and tactics.** A strategy of "become the premium brand for business customers" uses tactics such as sponsoring a business awards event or offering a free design consultation. Tactics change quickly; strategy changes slowly.

## Information technology and artificial intelligence in marketing

Information technology (IT) and artificial intelligence (AI) are changing marketing:

| Use | Example | Benefit | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Data analysis | Studying sales and website data to find buying patterns | Better targeting and forecasting | Needs quality data and skills; privacy rules |
| Personalisation | Recommending products or sending messages based on a customer's past purchases | Higher sales and loyalty | Can seem intrusive; mistakes are noticed |
| Automation | Scheduling messages and posts; automatic reminders | Saves time and cost | Less human feel; errors may spread quickly |
| Chatbots | Answering simple customer questions at any hour | Cheap, always available | Cannot handle complex complaints; customers may prefer a person |

These tools lower costs and improve targeting, but they require investment, skilled staff and reliable internet and electricity, and they raise issues of **data protection** and trust. A small business in Zimbabwe may find that mobile messaging and simple customer lists give most of the benefit at low cost.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: costing and judging a marketing plan]
**Context.** The fictional furniture maker now writes a plan to raise sales from US\$300,000 to US\$360,000. The planned budget is shown. The gross profit margin on extra sales is 40%.

| Budget item | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Market research | 4,000 |
| Promotion (radio, social media, in-store) | 18,000 |
| Product improvement and packaging | 6,000 |
| Distribution (two new delivery vans) | 8,000 |
| **Total marketing budget** | **36,000** |

1. Sales objective: +US\$60,000, which is 60,000 ÷ 300,000 × 100 = 20%.
2. Budget as a percentage of target sales: 36,000 ÷ 360,000 × 100 = 10%.
3. Year-1 extra gross profit = 40% × 60,000 = US\$24,000. Net result = 24,000 − 36,000 = −US\$12,000 (a loss).
4. If 70% of the extra sales are repeated in year 2 with no further budget, extra gross profit in year 2 = 40% × (0.7 × 60,000) = US\$16,800. Cumulative result = −12,000 + 16,800 = +US\$4,800.
5. **Judgement.** The plan does not pay for itself in the first year and breaks even only in the second, if customers return. It is worthwhile if the vans and the research have lasting value and the repeat rate is realistic. The owner should set a review point after six months and check the repeat rate.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, an AI chatbot for a small business]
**Context.** A fictional tourism lodge near Victoria Falls receives many questions by message about rooms and prices, often at night. A software agent proposes an AI chatbot to answer them. The lodge has 12 rooms and a marketing budget of US\$800 a month.

**Point.** A chatbot can answer routine questions all day and night.

**Application.** Most questions are about prices, dates and directions. A chatbot could handle these and pass difficult requests (a group booking, a complaint) to a staff member.

**Analysis.** Quick replies may prevent lost bookings, because tourists often choose the first lodge that answers. But setting up and maintaining the system costs money, mistakes could damage the lodge's reputation, and guests who want a personal welcome may be put off. A small lodge may not receive enough messages to justify the cost.

**Judgement.** The lodge should try a low-cost tool for common questions only, check the answers regularly and keep a person for bookings and complaints. The plan is worthwhile if it raises bookings by more than its monthly cost, which should be measured after a trial period.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the plan with the strategy"}
The **strategy** is the long-term approach; the **plan** is the detailed document with actions, budget and timetable for a period. Tactics are the short-term actions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing the contents without linking them"}
A strong answer shows that the mix is **coordinated** and fits the objective and the business. Naming four Ps is not enough.
:::

:::mistake{title="Praising IT or AI without costs"}
Technology brings benefits but also costs, skills, security and privacy issues. A balanced answer weighs both and refers to the size of the business.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
This concept is tested on Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 only. For *analyse* and *explain*, link a plan feature or a tool to the business's objective with a chain of reasoning. For *evaluate* and *advise*, judge the plan or strategy against the objective, the resources and the market, weigh the benefits against the cost and limits, and make a clear recommendation with a condition or time-frame. Use figures (budget, expected extra sales) to support your judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A marketing plan sets objectives, target market, mix, budget, timetable and controls for a period.
- Plans give direction and coordination but are costly, rely on forecasts and may be inflexible.
- A marketing strategy is a long-term approach that must fit the business, product and market; tactics are short-term actions.
- A coordinated strategy makes the four Ps support the same objective and positioning.
- IT and AI bring data analysis, personalisation, automation and chatbots; they cost money and raise privacy and trust issues.
:::
