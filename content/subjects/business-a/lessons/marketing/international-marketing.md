## Introduction

A fictional manufacturer of fruit juice in Mutare has saturated the Zimbabwean market: the home market is small and competitors are many. Zambia and Malawi are growing and their shops sell few juices from outside the country. The owner thinks of exporting, but there are questions. Will Zambian consumers like the taste? Which currency will pay for the juice? Should the firm sell through an agent, open a factory or let a Zambian firm make the juice under licence? **International marketing** extends the marketing process across borders and adds new opportunities and new risks.

This lesson applies to ZIMSEC Business Enterprise Skills (Form 6) and Cambridge **A Level** (Papers 3 and 4). The ZIMSEC Business Enterprise Skills syllabus emphasises the benefits and challenges of foreign markets and strategies for breaking in; Cambridge asks for judgement on entry methods and on adaptation versus a global approach. Trade blocs and multinational firms are covered in the lessons on international trade and globalisation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="International marketing"}
The marketing of goods or services to customers in more than one country, which may require changes to the product, price, promotion and distribution.
:::

:::definition{term="Exporting"}
Selling goods or services produced in one country to customers in another country.
:::

:::definition{term="Licensing"}
Giving another firm in a foreign country the right to produce or sell a product or use a brand in return for a fee or royalty.
:::

:::definition{term="Pan-global marketing"}
Selling a standardised product with the same marketing mix in all markets.
:::

## Globalisation and economic collaboration

Falling trade barriers, trade agreements and blocs (such as SADC and COMESA), cheaper transport and the internet make it easier to sell abroad. Implications for marketing:

- **More opportunities**: larger markets for local firms, and the chance to use spare capacity.
- **More competition**: foreign firms can enter the home market.
- **Easier information**: market research and promotion online at lower cost.
- **Rules and standards**: tariffs, quotas, labelling and product standards still differ.
- **Regional collaboration** reduces tariffs among members, which makes exporting to neighbours more attractive.

### Why do firms market internationally?

To find **growth** when the home market is saturated; to gain **economies of scale**; to **spread risk** over several economies and seasons; to follow customers and competitors; to use **spare capacity**; and to exploit a differentiated product.

## Identifying and selecting markets

A firm should research candidate countries and compare them. Factors include **market size and growth**, income and tastes, **competition**, **political and economic stability** (and exchange rates), **trade barriers** and regulation, distance and **transport costs**, language and culture, and the **ease** of finding distributors. Sources: trade bodies, embassies, published statistics and field visits. A **weighted scoring** table can summarise the comparison.

## Global approach or adaptation?

| | Pan-global (standardised) | Local adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product and mix | Same everywhere | Changed for each market (taste, packaging, language, price) |
| Advantages | Lower costs (scale in production, promotion and design); consistent brand image | Fits local tastes and rules; fewer cultural errors |
| Disadvantages | May ignore local tastes and laws; mistakes in meaning | Higher costs; the brand image may be less consistent |
| Suits | Products with universal appeal (technology, some luxury brands) | Food, clothing, services and goods affected by culture |

Many firms use a middle path ("think global, act local"): a global brand with local variations.

## Methods of entry

| Method | Description | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Exporting (direct or through an agent or distributor) | Produce at home, ship abroad | Low investment; little risk; easy to start | Transport costs, tariffs, exchange-rate risk; less control over the market |
| Licensing | Foreign firm produces or sells for a fee | Low cost; quick; avoids tariffs | Less control over quality; a licensee may learn the know-how and compete |
| Franchising | Foreign operator uses the brand and system | Rapid expansion with others' capital | Risk to brand if standards fall; training and support costs |
| Joint venture | Share ownership of a venture with a local partner | Local knowledge and shared risk and cost | Conflicts; shared profit; loss of control |
| Direct investment (own subsidiary or factory) | Build or buy a business abroad | Full control; avoids tariffs; close to customers | Highest cost and risk; political and legal exposure |

**The choice** depends on the firm's resources and experience, the size and risk of the market, tariffs and transport costs, the need for control, the speed required and the importance of local knowledge.

## Benefits and challenges of foreign markets (BES detail)

**Benefits:** larger sales; higher prices in some markets; longer product life; spreading risk; foreign currency earnings; learning from tough competition.

**Challenges:** different tastes and culture; language; unfamiliar laws; exchange-rate changes; slow or uncertain payment; transport and delays; competition from local firms; political instability.

**Strategies for breaking in:** choose a nearby, similar market first; use an experienced local agent or partner; adapt the product and packaging; use the trade fair or a sample shipment; use online sales; build trust with small orders; and use trade agreements that remove tariffs.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: selecting a market with a weighted scoring table]
**Context.** The fictional juice manufacturer scores three markets from 1 (poor) to 5 (very good) on five criteria. The weights show the importance it gives to each criterion.

| Criterion | Weight | Zambia | Botswana | Malawi |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Market size | 30% | 4 | 2 | 3 |
| Market growth | 25% | 4 | 3 | 5 |
| Low competition | 20% | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| Low risk | 15% | 3 | 5 | 2 |
| Ease of entry | 10% | 4 | 4 | 3 |
| **Weighted score** | 100% | 3.65 | 3.30 | 3.15 |

1. Weighted score for Zambia = 4 × 0.30 + 4 × 0.25 + 3 × 0.20 + 3 × 0.15 + 4 × 0.10 = 1.20 + 1.00 + 0.60 + 0.45 + 0.40 = 3.65.
2. The same method gives Botswana 3.30 and Malawi 3.15.
3. Zambia scores highest. Malawi has the fastest growth but the highest risk and the strongest competition.
4. **Judgement.** The result depends on the weights and on the scores, which are judgements. Zambia is the best choice on these figures, but the firm should check the scores with market research before committing money, and consider Botswana if it puts more weight on low risk.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: export profit and the entry decision]
**Context.** The juice manufacturer plans to export 5,000 cartons to Zambia. Production cost is US\$30 a carton. It pays freight of US\$4 and insurance of US\$2 a carton, and a tariff of 10% on the value of cost, freight and insurance. It sells at a delivered price of US\$48 and has fixed export costs (agent, promotion) of US\$15,000.

| Item | Calculation | US\$ per carton |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Production cost | | 30.00 |
| Freight and insurance | 4 + 2 | 6.00 |
| Cost before tariff | 30 + 6 | 36.00 |
| Tariff | 10% × 36.00 | 3.60 |
| Landed cost | 36.00 + 3.60 | 39.60 |
| Profit per carton | 48.00 − 39.60 | 8.40 |

1. Profit before fixed export costs = 8.40 × 5,000 = US\$42,000.
2. Profit after fixed costs = 42,000 − 15,000 = US\$27,000.
3. **Judgement.** Exporting looks profitable on these estimates, but the margin is small (US\$8.40 on US\$48, about 17.5%). A rise in freight or a change in the tariff, or a slower rate of sale, would cut the profit quickly. The firm should start with a trial shipment and consider licensing a Zambian bottler if the tariff rises.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Assuming that foreign markets are always better"}
Foreign markets bring new costs and risks. A good answer weighs growth against competition, risk, adaptation costs and the firm's resources.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating all entry methods as the same"}
Each method has a different cost, risk and amount of control. Link the method to the firm's resources, experience and the market.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring culture and law when standardising"}
A standardised mix can fail if tastes, language or regulation differ. Explain what would need to be adapted, and what the cost of adapting would be.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *describe*, *explain* and *discuss* the benefits and challenges of foreign markets and strategies for entering them; Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 ask you to *analyse*, *assess* and *evaluate* the choice of market or entry method and of adaptation or a global approach. Apply the answer to the case: the firm's size and resources, the product, the country's risks. In evaluations, give a judgement with a condition (for example, start with exporting and move to a joint venture as experience grows).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Globalisation and trade collaboration create opportunities and competition for marketers.
- Firms go abroad to grow, gain scale, spread risk and use spare capacity.
- Markets are chosen by size, growth, competition, risk, barriers and ease of entry; a scoring table can compare them.
- Pan-global marketing saves cost but may ignore local tastes; adaptation fits better but costs more.
- Entry methods: exporting, licensing, franchising, joint venture and direct investment, with rising cost, control and risk.
:::
