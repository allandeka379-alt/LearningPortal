## Introduction

Every Saturday a fictional vegetable market in Mutare sells crates of tomatoes. In a good season many farmers bring tomatoes and the price per crate falls to the point where some farmers go home with unsold crates. When a cold spell damages the crops, few crates arrive, traders compete for them and the price jumps. The farmers have not changed their minds about what they want; the **market** has changed the price for them. Business managers need the same understanding to decide how much to produce, what to charge and what will happen when costs, incomes or competitors change.

This lesson applies to ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **AS** (Papers 1 and 2). Elasticity, which measures *how much* demand responds to price, is a separate lesson. Curves are described in words and **schedules** (tables), as you can read them without a graph.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Demand"}
The quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at each price in a given period.
:::

:::definition{term="Supply"}
The quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at each price in a given period.
:::

:::definition{term="Equilibrium price"}
The price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied, so there is no shortage or surplus and no pressure on the price to change.
:::

:::definition{term="Effective demand"}
Demand backed by the ability to pay: a wish to buy that is supported by money.
:::

## Demand

The **law of demand**: other things equal, as the price of a good rises the quantity demanded falls, and as the price falls the quantity demanded rises. The demand curve slopes downwards.

**Movement along the curve.** A change in the *price of the good itself* changes the quantity demanded: a movement along the same curve (an extension or contraction).

**Shift of the curve.** A change in any *other* factor changes demand at every price. Demand increases (shifts right) or decreases (shifts left). The main factors are:

| Factor | Effect on demand |
| --- | --- |
| Income | Higher income raises demand for normal goods; for inferior goods demand falls |
| Prices of substitutes | A rise in the price of a substitute (for example one brand of bread) raises demand for the other |
| Prices of complements | A rise in the price of a complement (for example cooking oil) lowers demand for the good used with it |
| Tastes and fashion | Favourable changes raise demand |
| Population | More consumers raise demand |
| Advertising and promotion | Successful promotion raises demand |
| Expectations | Expected price rises encourage buying now |
| Seasons and events | Winter raises demand for blankets |

**Demand concepts for enterprises (ZIMSEC detail).** *Joint demand* is demand for goods used together (tea and sugar). *Derived demand* is demand for a product because it is needed to make another (flour is demanded because bread is). *Effective demand* needs purchasing power.

## Supply

The **law of supply**: other things equal, as the price rises producers supply more, because higher prices raise profit and attract more output. The supply curve slopes upwards.

A change in the price of the good changes the **quantity supplied** (a movement along the curve). A change in other factors shifts the supply curve:

| Factor | Effect on supply |
| --- | --- |
| Costs of production (wages, materials, energy, rent) | Higher costs reduce supply; lower costs raise it |
| Technology | Better technology lowers costs and raises supply |
| Weather and natural events | A drought reduces agricultural supply |
| Taxes and subsidies | Taxes reduce supply; subsidies increase it |
| Number of firms | More firms increase market supply |
| Prices of other goods the firm could produce | A better price for maize may reduce the supply of beans |
| Expectations | Expected higher prices may reduce supply now |

## Equilibrium

At a price **above** equilibrium the quantity supplied is greater than the quantity demanded: a **surplus** (excess supply). Sellers cut prices to clear it. At a price **below** equilibrium the quantity demanded is greater than the quantity supplied: a **shortage** (excess demand). Buyers compete and the price rises. The price moves towards equilibrium.

### Changes in equilibrium

| Change | Effect on price | Effect on quantity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand increases (right shift) | Rises | Rises |
| Demand decreases (left shift) | Falls | Falls |
| Supply increases (right shift) | Falls | Rises |
| Supply decreases (left shift) | Rises | Falls |

When *both* shift the result depends on the sizes: for example, if demand increases and supply increases, quantity rises for certain but the price could rise, fall or stay the same.

### Using the information in business

A firm that expects demand to rise may raise output or price; one expecting a rival's supply to rise may expect lower market prices and cut its costs. Real markets differ from the model: there may be few sellers, differentiated products, controlled prices, and consumers and producers may lack information. The model is a **useful guide to direction**, not an exact prediction of size.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: finding equilibrium and the effect of a rise in demand]
**Context.** The tomato market in Mutare has the following schedule (a fictional market, crates per Saturday).

| Price per crate (US\$) | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied | Market position |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 1.00 | 500 | 100 | Shortage of 400 |
| 1.50 | 400 | 200 | Shortage of 200 |
| 2.00 | 300 | 300 | Equilibrium |
| 2.50 | 200 | 400 | Surplus of 200 |
| 3.00 | 100 | 500 | Surplus of 400 |

1. Equilibrium is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied: US\$2.00 and 300 crates.
2. At US\$2.50 there is a surplus of 200 crates, so sellers will cut the price. At US\$1.50 there is a shortage of 200, so the price will rise.
3. Now a successful advertising campaign raises demand by 200 crates at every price:

| Price per crate (US\$) | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied | Market position |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 1.00 | 700 | 100 | Shortage of 600 |
| 1.50 | 600 | 200 | Shortage of 400 |
| 2.00 | 500 | 300 | Shortage of 200 |
| 2.50 | 400 | 400 | Equilibrium |
| 3.00 | 300 | 500 | Surplus of 200 |

4. New equilibrium: US\$2.50 and 400 crates. Price rises by US\$0.50 and quantity rises by 100 crates. Quantity supplied rises along the supply curve; supply itself has not shifted.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, a drought]
**Context.** A drought reduces the tomato harvest. Farmers now supply 200 crates fewer at each price (but no less than zero).

| Price per crate (US\$) | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied | Market position |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 1.00 | 500 | 0 | Shortage of 500 |
| 1.50 | 400 | 0 | Shortage of 400 |
| 2.00 | 300 | 100 | Shortage of 200 |
| 2.50 | 200 | 200 | Equilibrium |
| 3.00 | 100 | 300 | Surplus of 200 |

**Point.** A fall in supply raises price and reduces quantity.

**Application.** In the table the equilibrium moves from US\$2.00 and 300 crates to US\$2.50 and 200 crates.

**Analysis.** The price rise of 25% rations the scarce tomatoes to buyers who value them most. Farmers who still have a harvest gain from the higher price, but their total income depends on how much they lost: a farmer who lost half the crop but sells at a quarter more may earn less.

**Judgement.** Traders who buy tomatoes should expect higher costs and may pass them on or switch to a substitute vegetable. The model gives the direction (up, down), but the size depends on how sharply buyers respond to price, which is the subject of the lesson on elasticity.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing a movement with a shift"}
A change in the price of the good causes a movement along the curve. A shift needs a change in another factor, such as income, tastes or costs. Do not say "demand increased because the price fell".
:::

:::mistake{title="Wrong direction at a surplus or a shortage"}
At a price above equilibrium there is a **surplus** and the price falls. At a price below equilibrium there is a **shortage** and the price rises.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaving out the quantity"}
A full answer states what happens to **both** the price and the quantity, and it quotes figures from the table when they are given.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *state* determinants, *describe* how a change affects price, and *explain* with a schedule. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 use *explain*, *analyse* and *assess* with a described change in demand or supply. Quote the figures when a table is given, state the effect on both price and quantity, and say whether it is a movement or a shift. For a judgement, comment on the size of the effect and on the real-market limits (few sellers, price controls, imperfect information).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Demand falls as price rises; supply rises as price rises. A change in the price of the good moves along the curve.
- A change in income, tastes, other prices, population or promotion shifts demand; a change in costs, technology, weather, taxes or the number of firms shifts supply.
- Equilibrium is where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied; above it there is a surplus and below it a shortage.
- A rise in demand raises price and quantity; a fall in supply raises price and lowers quantity.
- The model shows the direction of change, not the exact size, and real markets can differ.
:::
