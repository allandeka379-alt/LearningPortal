## Introduction

A fictional dairy in Bulawayo raises the price of a 2-litre bottle of milk by 10%, and sales fall by 20%. A bus company in Gweru raises its fares by 10% and loses only 3% of its passengers. In both cases the price went up, but the dairy's revenue fell and the bus company's rose. Whether a price rise helps or harms a business depends on **how strongly customers respond** to it. That response is measured by **elasticity of demand**, which links what a business does (price, promotion) or what happens around it (customers' incomes, rivals' prices) to the quantity it sells.

This lesson is examined in ZIMSEC Business Studies (Form 5) and in Cambridge **A Level** (Papers 3 and 4). Price, income and promotional elasticity are in both syllabi. **Cross elasticity** is ZIMSEC detail. Elasticity of supply is not required. Every calculation below uses percentage changes measured on the **original** value.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |
| Price elasticity of demand; income elasticity of demand; cross elasticity of demand (ZIMSEC only); elasticity and promotional spending | Price elasticity of demand (PED); income elasticity of demand (YED); promotional elasticity of demand; limitations of elasticity |

Cross elasticity is ZIMSEC detail only. Cambridge A Level expects judgement on how elasticity affects pricing and promotion decisions.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Price elasticity of demand (PED)"}
A measure of the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of a product to a change in its price.
:::

:::definition{term="Income elasticity of demand (YED)"}
A measure of the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of a product to a change in consumers' incomes.
:::

:::definition{term="Promotional elasticity of demand"}
A measure of the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of a product to a change in spending on promotion.
:::

:::definition{term="Cross elasticity of demand (ZIMSEC detail)"}
A measure of the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of one product to a change in the price of another product.
:::

## The formulae

:::keyfact{title="Elasticity formulae"}
Percentage change = (new value − original value) ÷ original value × 100.

- PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price
- YED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income
- Promotional elasticity = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in promotional spending
- Cross elasticity = % change in quantity demanded of product A ÷ % change in price of product B
:::

A price **rise** normally lowers quantity demanded, so PED is **negative**. Many examiners ignore the sign and quote the size, but you should say which you are doing; in this bank the sign is kept.

## Interpreting price elasticity

| Value of PED (ignoring the sign) | Name | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Greater than 1 | Elastic | Quantity demanded changes by a bigger percentage than the price |
| Equal to 1 | Unitary | Quantity changes by the same percentage as the price |
| Less than 1 | Inelastic | Quantity changes by a smaller percentage than the price |

### Effect on revenue

| | Price rise | Price fall |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Elastic demand | Revenue **falls** | Revenue **rises** |
| Inelastic demand | Revenue **rises** | Revenue **falls** |
| Unitary demand | Unchanged | Unchanged |

**Revenue = price × quantity.** A firm with elastic demand gains by cutting prices (if its costs allow); a firm with inelastic demand gains revenue by raising prices, though costs and long-run loyalty must be considered.

### What determines PED?

- **Availability of substitutes**: more close substitutes make demand more elastic.
- **Necessity or luxury**: necessities (salt, bread) are inelastic; luxuries are elastic.
- **Proportion of income spent**: small items (matches) are inelastic; large purchases (a car) are elastic.
- **Time**: demand is more elastic in the long run as consumers find alternatives.
- **Brand loyalty and habit**: strong loyalty makes demand inelastic.
- **Definition of the market**: demand for a brand is more elastic than for the whole category.

## Income, promotional and cross elasticity

**YED.** A **positive** YED means a **normal** good: demand rises with income. If YED is greater than 1 the good is a **luxury**, and demand rises faster than income. If it is between 0 and 1 it is a **necessity**. A **negative** YED means an **inferior** good: demand falls as income rises (low-quality alternatives). A business selling goods with high YED will grow in a boom and suffer in a recession.

**Promotional elasticity.** It is normally positive: more promotion increases sales. A value above 1 means that sales respond strongly to promotion; a value below 1 means that extra spending raises sales only a little. The business should compare the **extra revenue (or contribution)** with the extra spending.

**Cross elasticity (ZIMSEC detail).** A **positive** value means the products are **substitutes** (a rise in the price of one brand increases demand for the other). A **negative** value means **complements** (a rise in the price of one reduces demand for the other). A value near zero means the goods are unrelated.

## Using elasticity in decisions, and its limitations

Elasticity helps to set prices, forecast the effect of a price rise or discount, plan promotion budgets and predict demand when incomes change. However, it has **limitations**:

- it is based on **past data**, and customers' responses change;
- it assumes **other things are constant**, but rivals' prices, tastes and incomes also change;
- the data from surveys or sales records may be **inaccurate**, and estimates differ with the **time period** and the size of the change;
- elasticity is **not constant** along a demand curve; a 5% change may give a different result from a 30% change;
- it does not show **costs**, so it cannot say whether a price change raises *profit*.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: price elasticity and revenue]
**Context.** A fictional dairy in Bulawayo raises the price of a bottle of milk from US\$2.00 to US\$2.20. Weekly sales fall from 5,000 to 4,000 bottles.

| Item | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| % change in price | (2.20 − 2.00) ÷ 2.00 × 100 | +10% |
| % change in quantity | (4,000 − 5,000) ÷ 5,000 × 100 | −20% |
| PED | −20 ÷ +10 | −2.0 |
| Revenue before | 2.00 × 5,000 | US\$10,000 |
| Revenue after | 2.20 × 4,000 | US\$8,800 |
| Change in revenue | 8,800 − 10,000 | −US\$1,200 |

1. PED is −2.0, so demand is **elastic** (greater than 1 in size).
2. Because demand is elastic, the price rise lowered revenue by US\$1,200 a week.
3. **Judgement.** The dairy should reverse the rise or, if it wants more revenue, try cutting the price: with the same PED a 5% cut (to US\$1.90) would raise sales by about 10%, to 5,500 bottles, and revenue to US\$10,450. A cut raises revenue, but profit depends on whether the extra bottles cost less to produce than the extra revenue, so costs must be checked.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: income and promotional elasticity, case analysis]
**Context.** A fictional furniture shop in Mutare finds that when local incomes rise from US\$500 to US\$540 a month its sales rise from 1,000 to 1,120 units a month. The shop also spends US\$2,000 a month on promotion and sells 800 units of its chairs. It raises promotional spending to US\$2,500 and sales rise to 880. Each chair sells for US\$5 and costs US\$3 to make (variable cost).

| Item | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| % change in income | (540 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 | +8% |
| % change in sales | (1,120 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 × 100 | +12% |
| YED | 12 ÷ 8 | +1.5 |
| % change in promotion | (2,500 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 × 100 | +25% |
| % change in chair sales | (880 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 | +10% |
| Promotional elasticity | 10 ÷ 25 | 0.4 |
| Extra contribution | 80 × (5 − 3) | US\$160 |
| Extra spending | 2,500 − 2,000 | US\$500 |

**Point.** Furniture sales respond strongly to income but only weakly to the extra promotion.

**Application.** YED is +1.5 (greater than 1), so furniture is a normal good and a luxury: demand rises faster than income. Promotional elasticity is 0.4, below 1.

**Analysis.** The extra spending of US\$500 earns only US\$160 of extra contribution, so the extra promotion lowers profit by US\$340 a month. In a boom the shop's sales will increase without extra promotion; in a recession they will fall sharply.

**Judgement.** The shop should not increase this promotion. It should instead plan stock for booms and recessions, and test other promotions that may be more effective, though a single month's data is a weak basis for a decision.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calculating the percentage change on the new value"}
Percentage changes are always on the **original** value. Using the new value gives a wrong elasticity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Stating the revenue effect the wrong way round"}
For **elastic** demand a price rise lowers revenue; for **inelastic** demand it raises revenue. Learn the table and test it with numbers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating elasticity as proof of a profit rise"}
Elasticity shows the effect on **revenue and quantity**. Profit also depends on costs, so a revenue rise does not guarantee a profit rise.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *calculate* and *explain* elasticity values (with cross elasticity); Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 ask you to *calculate*, *analyse* and *evaluate* the usefulness of elasticity. Show the percentage changes, the ratio, the sign and the meaning (elastic, inelastic). For a decision answer, quote the effect on revenue in dollars and then bring in costs, the time period and the limitations before you reach a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Elasticity = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in the causal factor, with changes measured on the original value.
- PED is normally negative; elastic means the size is more than 1, inelastic less than 1.
- With elastic demand a price rise lowers revenue; with inelastic demand it raises revenue.
- YED positive means normal, above 1 luxury, negative inferior; cross elasticity positive means substitutes, negative complements (ZIMSEC).
- Promotional elasticity compares the response of sales with extra promotion; the extra contribution must exceed the extra spending.
- Elasticity is based on past data, assumes other factors constant and ignores costs.
:::
