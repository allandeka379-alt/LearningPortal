## Introduction

A fictional soap maker in Mutare notices that more of its customers ask for soap that does not dry the skin. A packer suggests a version with shea butter. The owner asks the chemist to test a recipe, the sales staff to ask shop owners, and the accountant to cost the idea. After two failed recipes the third works, a pilot batch is sold in two shops, and the soap is then launched across the province. The process cost about US\$40,000 and took 14 months. Many ideas do not get this far. **New products** are the lifeblood of a business whose existing products are ageing, but developing them is costly and risky.

This lesson applies to ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 6) and Cambridge **A Level** (Papers 3 and 4). ZIMSEC states *new product development*, its importance and the sources of ideas; Cambridge adds the costs, risks and benefits of R&D. The product life cycle and portfolio analysis are separate lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Product development"}
The process of creating a new product or improving an existing one, from the first idea to its launch on the market.
:::

:::definition{term="Research and development (R&D)"}
The scientific and technical work that a business does to invent new products or processes, or to improve existing ones.
:::

:::definition{term="Patent"}
A legal right, granted for a limited period, that stops others from making, using or selling an invention without the owner's permission.
:::

:::definition{term="Prototype"}
A first working model of a product, made to test the design before full production.
:::

## Why develop new products?

- **Replace ageing products** whose sales are falling at the decline stage.
- **Keep up with competitors** and changing tastes.
- **Spread risk** by not relying on one product.
- **Use spare capacity** and the skills of employees.
- **Grow** by entering new markets.
- **Charge higher prices** for a new, differentiated product while few rivals can match it.

## Sources of new ideas

| Source | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Customers | Complaints, requests and surveys: "we want a smaller pack" |
| Employees | Sales staff hear what customers say; production staff suggest improvements |
| Competitors | Studying a rival's new product (copying within the law) |
| Market research | Trends, gaps in the market and customer panels |
| R&D | Scientific breakthroughs and technical improvement |
| Suppliers and others | New materials or technology offered by suppliers; universities; imports |

## Stages in new product development

1. **Idea generation**: collect ideas from all sources.
2. **Screening**: remove ideas that do not fit the business, are too costly, illegal or unlikely to sell.
3. **Development**: design the product and make a prototype; estimate costs and the price.
4. **Testing**: test the product with customers (market testing) and for safety and quality.
5. **Launch**: produce in volume and promote; a **pilot launch** in one area reduces risk.
6. **Review**: compare results with targets and improve.

Each stage costs money, so weak ideas are dropped early. Most ideas never reach the market, and many launched products fail.

## Research and development (R&D)

**Importance.** R&D can give a technical or quality advantage, create patentable products and cut production costs. Firms in pharmaceuticals, mining equipment and agriculture rely on R&D.

**Benefits:** unique products; higher prices and profits; stronger brand; a legal monopoly if patented; process improvements that lower unit costs.

**Costs and risks:** high cost of scientists and equipment; results are uncertain and take years; a competitor may copy or leapfrog; the product may fail to sell; the money could have been used in other ways (opportunity cost). **Small firms** often cannot afford large R&D and rely on imitation, customer ideas and incremental improvement.

**Patents.** A patent gives the inventor the exclusive right for a fixed number of years, which protects the R&D investment. A patent costs money, protection does not stop other firms inventing a way round it, and publishing the idea may help competitors after the patent expires. Do not state a particular number of years unless the question does.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the new product funnel]
**Context.** A fictional food company in Harare records what happens to its new product ideas in a year.

| Stage | Number of products | % of ideas remaining |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Ideas generated | 200 | 100% |
| Passed screening | 40 | 20% |
| Developed into prototypes | 12 | 6% |
| Passed market tests | 6 | 3% |
| Launched | 4 | 2% |
| Still selling after two years | 2 | 1% |

1. Percentage remaining = number ÷ 200 × 100. For example, 12 ÷ 200 × 100 = 6%.
2. Only 2 of 200 ideas (1%) become lasting products.
3. Suppose the company spends US\$60,000 in total. The cost per lasting product is 60,000 ÷ 2 = US\$30,000.
4. **Judgement.** The low success rate shows why screening matters. Each rejected idea at screening saves the far greater cost of development and testing. The firm should still keep generating ideas, because the few successes may bring the profits that pay for the failures.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, is the new product worth developing?]
**Context.** The fictional soap maker in the introduction spent US\$30,000 on R&D and US\$10,000 on the launch. It expects the new soap to add a net cash inflow of US\$16,000 a year for five years.

| Item | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Total cost | 30,000 + 10,000 | US\$40,000 |
| Payback | 40,000 ÷ 16,000 | 2.5 years |
| Total inflows over five years | 16,000 × 5 | US\$80,000 |
| Net gain over five years | 80,000 − 40,000 | US\$40,000 |

**Point.** The product recovers its cost in 2.5 years and earns a gain of US\$40,000 over five years.

**Application.** For a small firm, tying up US\$40,000 for 2.5 years is a substantial risk, but the return is the same size as the investment.

**Analysis.** The forecast assumes five years of steady sales. If a rival copies the formula after two years, the inflows may fall and the payback may not be reached. The firm cannot patent a soap recipe easily, so the advantage may be short-lived.

**Judgement.** The project looks worthwhile if the forecast is realistic, but the soap maker should protect its advantage by building a brand and by launching in one province first to test sales before spending on a national launch.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating R&D as the same as market research"}
R&D is the technical work to create or improve products and processes. Market research collects information about customers and competitors.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying R&D always gives a competitive advantage"}
R&D is costly and uncertain, and competitors can copy. The answer must weigh the benefits against cost and risk.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the stages"}
Do not jump from idea to launch. A good answer places an action in its correct stage (for example, testing before launch) and shows why that stage reduces risk.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *state*, *outline* and *describe* the stages and sources of ideas, and to *discuss* the importance of new products. Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 ask you to *analyse*, *assess* or *evaluate* whether a business should invest in R&D or launch a new product. Use the context: size of the business, funds, competitors, how easily the product can be copied. In an evaluation, balance the benefits of new products with the costs and risks, and state a condition (for example, pilot launch first) in your judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- New products replace ageing ones, spread risk and support growth.
- Ideas come from customers, employees, competitors, research and R&D.
- Stages: idea generation, screening, development, testing, launch and review; most ideas are dropped.
- R&D can create unique, patentable products but is costly, uncertain and may be copied.
- Patents protect an invention for a limited time but cost money and may not stop imitation.
- Judge a new product by its costs, risks and expected payback.
:::
