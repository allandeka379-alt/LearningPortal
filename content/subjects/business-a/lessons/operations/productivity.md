## Introduction

Farai runs a fictional garment factory in Bulawayo. This year he hired two more machinists and bought new sewing machines. Output went up, but is the factory really doing better? To answer, he must compare what comes **out** with what goes **in**: how many shirts for each worker, for each dollar of equipment, and at what labour cost for each shirt. Operations managers use these measures to find out whether resources are being used well, to compare years and rivals, and to decide where to improve.

This lesson serves ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **AS** (Papers 1 and 2). You should know the transformational process (inputs and outputs) and how to calculate percentage change. Lean production and other A Level topics are covered elsewhere.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Efficiency"}
Producing output at the lowest possible cost, or getting the most output from given inputs, with little waste.
:::

:::definition{term="Effectiveness"}
How well the output meets the objectives and the needs of customers; doing the right thing, not only doing it cheaply.
:::

:::definition{term="Productivity"}
The amount of output produced for each unit of input used in a given period.
:::

:::definition{term="Sustainability (of operations)"}
Operating in a way that meets present needs without damaging the environment or the ability of future generations to meet theirs, for example by reducing waste, energy and pollution.
:::

:::keyfact
**Labour productivity = output ÷ number of employees** (or per hour worked). **Capital productivity = output ÷ capital employed.** **Unit labour cost = labour cost ÷ output.** Raising productivity usually lowers unit labour cost, provided pay does not rise faster than output.
:::

## Efficiency and effectiveness

A bakery can be efficient (low waste and cost per loaf) but ineffective if it makes bread that customers do not want. A business needs both: efficiency lowers cost and effectiveness secures sales. Efficiency is measured with productivity and cost per unit; effectiveness with customer satisfaction, quality and the achievement of objectives.

## Measuring productivity

| Measure | Formula | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Labour productivity | Output ÷ employees (or hours) | Output per worker or per hour |
| Capital productivity | Output ÷ capital employed | Output for each dollar of capital |
| Unit labour cost | Labour cost ÷ output | Labour cost of each unit |

Higher labour productivity lowers the labour cost per unit if wages stay the same, so the firm can cut prices, raise profit or both. High productivity helps competitiveness, but it is not the same as high output: a firm can produce more by hiring more workers without any rise in productivity.

### Ways of increasing productivity

- **Training** and better supervision; **motivation** through fair pay and job satisfaction.
- **Better equipment** and technology, and better maintenance.
- **Improved layout** and organisation of work, reducing waste.
- **Better materials** and fewer defects (less rework).
- **Value analysis (ZIMSEC detail)**: examining each part of a product and the process to find ways to reduce cost without lowering quality, for example removing an unnecessary component.

Each method has a cost and a delay: training takes time and may be wasted if staff leave; new machines need capital and may displace workers.

## Sustainability

Sustainable operations reduce waste, energy and water use, use recycled materials, avoid pollution and treat workers fairly. **Benefits**: lower costs in the long run (less waste and energy), a better image, ability to meet legal rules and to attract customers who care about the environment. **Costs and limits**: the cost of new equipment and training in the short run, slower output during change, and higher prices for some materials. The balance depends on the size of the saving, customers' response and the law.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: measuring productivity over two years]
**Context.** Farai's factory made the following in two years.

| Measure | Year 1 | Year 2 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Output (shirts) | 18,000 | 21,600 |
| Employees | 30 | 32 |
| Labour cost (US\$) | 54,000 | 59,400 |
| Capital employed (US\$) | 120,000 | 144,000 |
| Labour productivity (shirts per employee) | 600 | 675 |
| Capital productivity (shirts per US\$1,000 of capital) | 150 | 150 |
| Unit labour cost (US\$ per shirt) | 3.00 | 2.75 |

1. Labour productivity: 18,000 ÷ 30 = 600; 21,600 ÷ 32 = 675. The increase is 75 ÷ 600 × 100 = **12.5%**.
2. Capital productivity: 18,000 ÷ 120 = 150 and 21,600 ÷ 144 = 150 shirts per US\$1,000: **no change**. The extra capital produced extra output at exactly the same rate.
3. Unit labour cost: 54,000 ÷ 18,000 = US\$3.00; 59,400 ÷ 21,600 = US\$2.75, a fall of 0.25 ÷ 3.00 × 100 = **8.3%**.
4. **Interpretation.** Each worker is producing more and each shirt costs less in labour, so the factory is more competitive. But the new machines did not raise capital productivity; their value will depend on whether the extra shirts can be sold.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, productivity and sustainability]
**Context.** A fictional bakery in Mutare employs 12 bakers who together make 480 loaves an hour. Tendai considers (a) training the bakers in a better way of organising the work, which she expects to raise output to 540 loaves an hour at a cost of US\$900, and (b) buying a solar oven costing US\$5,400 that cuts the bakery's firewood bill from US\$1,500 to US\$600 a month and uses no wood.

**Point.** Both options raise efficiency, but in different ways.

**Application.** Labour productivity now is 480 ÷ 12 = 40 loaves an hour per baker. After training it would be 540 ÷ 12 = 45, a rise of 5 ÷ 40 × 100 = 12.5%. The solar oven saves 1,500 − 600 = US\$900 a month, so the oven repays its cost of US\$5,400 in 5,400 ÷ 900 = 6 months.

**Analysis.** Training lowers the labour cost per loaf and costs only US\$900; the training also makes the workers more skilled, which may help motivation. The oven lowers fuel costs and reduces smoke and the use of wood, which improves sustainability and the bakery's image. However, the oven needs sunshine, and does nothing on cloudy days.

**Judgement.** Tendai should do the training first because it is cheap and certain. She should buy the oven only if her backup source of heat is reliable and the payback is short. The two together would improve both productivity and sustainability.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing output with productivity"}
A firm that doubles its workforce and doubles its output has not improved labour productivity. Divide output by the inputs used.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up efficiency and effectiveness"}
Efficiency is doing things with little waste; effectiveness is achieving the goals and satisfying customers. A firm can be efficient at making products that nobody wants.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting units"}
Say "shirts per employee" or "shirts per hour". Productivity is a ratio, so state what is being divided by what.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *state* the meaning of productivity, *calculate* labour productivity and *discuss* ways of raising it. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 use *define*, *calculate* (productivity or unit labour cost), *explain*, *analyse* and *assess*. Show the formula, the substitution and the units. In an evaluation, state the cost and time of each method and judge by a condition, for example "if the extra output can be sold" or "if workers are paid by output".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Efficiency is output with little waste or cost; effectiveness is meeting objectives and customer needs.
- Labour productivity = output ÷ employees (or hours); capital productivity = output ÷ capital; unit labour cost = labour cost ÷ output.
- Productivity rises through training, motivation, better equipment, layout, materials and value analysis (ZIMSEC).
- Raising productivity lowers unit labour cost if pay does not rise faster than output.
- Sustainable operations reduce waste and pollution; they may cost more at first but can lower costs and improve the image.
:::
