## Introduction

Chipo grows mangoes near Mutare. In a good season she cannot sell all her fruit before it rots, and the price she gets for a tonne of fresh mangoes is low. A neighbour dries mangoes, packs them in small bags and sells them to shops in Harare for far more per kilogram. Both are doing the same thing in different ways: **they are taking inputs, transforming them and selling outputs that customers value more than the inputs**. This is the work of **operations management**, the part of a business that makes goods or provides services.

This lesson serves ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **AS** (Papers 1 and 2). It prepares you for every later operations topic, such as productivity, methods of production and quality. You should know that businesses use resources to produce goods and services, and that revenue minus cost is profit.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Operations management"}
The management of the resources and processes used to turn inputs into goods and services that customers want.
:::

:::definition{term="Transformational process"}
The process by which inputs (resources) are changed into outputs (goods and services) of greater value.
:::

:::definition{term="Added value (value added)"}
The difference between the selling price of a product and the cost of the bought-in materials and services used to make it.
:::

:::definition{term="Beneficiation (value addition)"}
Processing a raw material locally so that it is sold in a more finished and more valuable form, instead of being sold in its raw state.
:::

:::keyfact
**Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in inputs.** It pays for wages, rent, interest, tax and profit.
:::

## Inputs, processes and outputs

| Part | Meaning | Example: a bakery | Example: a clinic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Inputs | Resources used | Flour, yeast, energy, ovens, bakers | Doctors, medicines, equipment, buildings |
| Process | Work that changes inputs | Mixing, baking, packing | Examination, treatment, record-keeping |
| Outputs | Goods or services produced | Loaves | Treated patients |

**Factors of production.** The inputs are grouped as **land** (natural resources), **labour** (human effort), **capital** (machinery, buildings, tools) and **enterprise** (the risk-taking and organising skills of the entrepreneur). The **production function** is the relationship between the amount of the inputs used and the amount of output produced. A business can increase output by using more inputs or by using them better (see the lesson on productivity).

**Why operations matters.** Operations decides the cost of each unit, the quality of the product, the speed of delivery and how well the business can respond to changes in demand. These affect customers and profit.

## Stages of production

| Stage | Activity | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Extracting or growing raw materials | Farming, mining, fishing, forestry |
| Secondary | Making and processing goods from raw materials | Milling, manufacturing, construction |
| Tertiary | Services that support production and distribution | Transport, banking, retail, insurance, repairs |

Each stage adds value. In an economy where most output is primary, jobs and income are lower; moving to secondary and tertiary stages creates more jobs and earns more.

## Value addition and beneficiation

**Value addition** means turning a basic product into a more finished product that sells for more, for example roasting and packing groundnuts instead of selling them raw. **Beneficiation** is the term often used for processing minerals or crops in the country where they are produced, instead of exporting them in raw form. Benefits include higher earnings, more jobs, a skilled workforce and reduced dependence on raw prices. Costs and difficulties include the need for investment, power, skills, reliable water and transport, and competition from established processors elsewhere.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: added value along a supply chain]
**Context.** A kilogram of maize meal passes through three stages in a fictional chain. Each stage buys inputs from outside and sells at a higher price.

| Stage | Cost of bought-in inputs (US\$ per kg) | Selling price (US\$ per kg) | Value added (US\$ per kg) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Farm (primary) | 0.12 | 0.30 | 0.18 |
| Mill (secondary) | 0.30 | 0.50 | 0.20 |
| Packing and shop (tertiary) | 0.50 | 0.75 | 0.25 |

1. Value added at each stage = selling price − bought-in inputs: 0.30 − 0.12 = 0.18; 0.50 − 0.30 = 0.20; 0.75 − 0.50 = 0.25.
2. Total value added = 0.18 + 0.20 + 0.25 = US\$0.63, which equals the final price less the first stage's inputs: 0.75 − 0.12 = US\$0.63.
3. The tertiary stage adds the most value per kilogram (US\$0.25) even though it does not grow or mill anything: packing, transport and retail are worth paying for.
4. Added value is not profit. From each stage's added value the business must pay wages, rent and other costs before profit remains.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, beneficiation of mangoes]
**Context.** Chipo can sell a tonne of fresh mangoes for US\$150. If she dries them, a tonne gives 120 kg of dried mango which sells for US\$6 a kilogram. Drying costs US\$330 a tonne for energy, packaging and labour.

| Item | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Sell 1 tonne of raw mangoes | 150 |
| Sell 120 kg of dried mango at US\$6 | 720 |
| Extra revenue from processing | 570 |
| Processing costs (energy, packaging, labour) | 330 |
| **Extra profit from beneficiation** | 240 |

**Point.** Processing adds value and raises profit per tonne.

**Application.** Dried mango earns US\$720 against US\$150 for raw fruit, an extra US\$570; after US\$330 of extra costs she gains US\$240 a tonne.

**Analysis.** The gain depends on selling every kilogram at US\$6. Dried fruit stores for longer than fresh fruit, which reduces waste, and the processing creates work. However, she needs a dryer, a reliable power supply and a market for the product. If the price fell to US\$4 a kilogram, revenue would be US\$480 and the gain would be only US\$0, so the margin for error is small.

**Judgement.** Beneficiation is worthwhile for Chipo if she can find buyers at about US\$6 and keep her costs controlled. She should start with a small batch to test the market before investing in a larger dryer.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing added value with profit"}
Added value is selling price minus bought-in inputs. It must still pay wages, rent and other costs; what remains is profit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing inputs without the process"}
The transformational process is **inputs → process → outputs**. A good answer says what happens in the middle, for example mixing and baking, not only what goes in and what comes out.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking that beneficiation is always profitable"}
Processing needs investment, skills, power and markets. It adds value only if customers pay more than the extra cost.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *state* the factors of production, *describe* the stages of production and *explain* or *discuss* value addition with local examples. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 use *define*, *calculate* (added value), *explain* and *analyse*. Use a short example from the case for each point. For evaluation, weigh the gain in value against the cost, skills and market needed, and make your judgement depend on a condition.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Operations turns inputs (land, labour, capital, enterprise) through a process into outputs that customers value.
- The production function links the amount of inputs to the amount of output.
- Added value is selling price less the cost of bought-in inputs; it pays wages, interest, rent, tax and profit.
- Production is primary (raw materials), secondary (manufacturing) and tertiary (services).
- Beneficiation and value addition process raw materials locally to earn more, but need investment, skills, power and markets.
:::
