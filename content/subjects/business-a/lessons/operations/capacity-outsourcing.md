## Introduction

Rudo owns a fictional printing press in Masvingo. In January, schools are busy and her press runs day and night; deliveries are late and mistakes creep in. In July, the press stands idle for half of each day while wages, rent and machine costs continue. Both months show a **mismatch between the capacity of the business and the demand it faces**. Operations managers measure this with the capacity utilisation rate and look for ways to bring output and capacity closer together, including buying services from other firms instead of doing the work themselves (**outsourcing**).

This lesson serves ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **AS** (Papers 1 and 2). It uses fixed and variable costs and unit cost. Outsourcing is named in the Cambridge syllabus; ZIMSEC covers sub-contracting and rationalisation as ways of managing capacity.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |
| Capacity utilisation; sub-contracting; rationalisation | Capacity utilisation; outsourcing (Cambridge detail) |

Sub-contracting is a form of outsourcing used to deal with extra demand. Rationalisation means cutting capacity (closing sites or machines) to match demand.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Capacity"}
The maximum output that a business can produce in a given period with its existing resources.
:::

:::definition{term="Capacity utilisation"}
The proportion of the maximum possible output that is actually being produced, shown as a percentage.
:::

:::definition{term="Outsourcing"}
Using another business to carry out a function or part of the production process that the firm could do for itself.
:::

:::definition{term="Rationalisation"}
Reducing capacity, for example by closing a factory or removing machines, to remove spare capacity and cut costs.
:::

:::keyfact
**Capacity utilisation (%) = actual output ÷ maximum possible output × 100.** **Spare capacity = maximum output − actual output.**
:::

## Operating under capacity

When utilisation is low:

- **Unit costs are high**, because fixed costs are spread over fewer units;
- workers and machines are idle, which can hurt morale;
- the business may seem unstable to lenders and investors.

On the other hand, spare capacity allows the business to accept a special order or a rise in demand without delay.

## Operating over capacity

When a business operates at or above its planned maximum (using overtime, extra shifts or working machines without rest):

- **Machines break down** more often and maintenance is neglected;
- staff become tired, make errors and may leave, so **quality falls** and waste rises;
- deliveries are late;
- the business may turn customers away.

Unit costs can rise too because of overtime pay and repairs. The business may earn more in the short term but cannot continue for long.

## Improving capacity utilisation

| Situation | Methods |
| --- | --- |
| Under capacity (spare capacity) | Raise demand through promotion, price cuts or new markets; find new products for idle machines; **rationalise** (close or sell surplus capacity); lease out unused space or machines; make workers flexible (part-time or annualised hours) |
| Over capacity | Increase capacity (new machine, shift, site); **sub-contract** or **outsource** part of the work; raise prices to slow demand; use overtime for short periods only |

## Outsourcing

| Impact | Benefits | Problems |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost | The supplier may be more efficient and have lower unit costs; fixed costs fall; no need to buy equipment | The supplier needs a profit; costs may rise at renewal |
| Focus | The firm can concentrate on what it does best | It may lose skills that were once core |
| Quality control | A specialist may provide better quality | Less direct control; poor quality harms the firm's name |
| Risk | Risks of machinery and demand shift to the supplier | Dependence on the supplier; secrecy and delivery risk |
| Jobs | Employees may be redeployed to core work | Redundancies and low morale among remaining staff |

A business may use outsourcing for functions such as payroll, cleaning, transport, or manufacture of a component.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: capacity utilisation and unit cost]
**Context.** A fictional pottery in Mutare can make at most 10,000 mugs a month. Its fixed costs are US\$24,000 a month and its variable cost is US\$3 a mug.

| Output a month (units) | Capacity used | Fixed cost per unit (US\$) | Total cost per unit (US\$) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 4,000 | 40% | 6.00 | 9.00 |
| 6,000 | 60% | 4.00 | 7.00 |
| 8,000 | 80% | 3.00 | 6.00 |
| 10,000 | 100% | 2.40 | 5.40 |

1. Capacity utilisation at 6,000 mugs = 6,000 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = 60%. Spare capacity = 10,000 − 6,000 = 4,000 mugs.
2. Fixed cost per mug at 6,000 = 24,000 ÷ 6,000 = US\$4.00, so total cost per mug = 4.00 + 3.00 = US\$7.00.
3. Raising output from 40% to 100% of capacity cuts total cost per mug from US\$9.00 to US\$5.40, a fall of 40%.
4. **Interpretation.** If the mugs sell at US\$6.50 the pottery makes a loss at 60% utilisation (cost US\$7.00) and a profit at 80% or more (cost US\$6.00 or less). Break-even is where fixed cost is covered: 24,000 ÷ (6.50 − 3.00) = 6,857 mugs, or about 69% of capacity.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, outsource the boxes?]
**Context.** Tendai Foods is a fictional biscuit maker in Harare. It makes its own packaging boxes at a full cost of US\$1.70 a box for 5,000 boxes a month. A specialist packaging firm offers to supply the same boxes for US\$1.60. The company's box workers number four, and part of its fixed costs would continue even if it stopped making boxes.

**Point.** Outsourcing appears cheaper by US\$0.10 a box but the full cost includes fixed costs that may not disappear.

**Application.** The apparent saving is 0.10 × 5,000 = US\$500 a month. If part of the fixed cost remains after outsourcing, the true saving is less, and may become a loss. The four jobs may be lost or the workers moved to biscuit making.

**Analysis.** Outsourcing would free space and let the company focus on baking. However, quality depends on the supplier, a late delivery would stop packing, and the supplier could raise its price later. Workers made redundant would damage morale.

**Judgement.** Tendai Foods should outsource only if the true saving, after the fixed costs that continue, is clear, and the supplier has a good delivery record. A one-year contract with a quality check allows the company to return to making boxes if the arrangement fails.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using the wrong denominator"}
Capacity utilisation = actual output ÷ **maximum** output, not actual output ÷ planned or average output.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that 100% utilisation is always best"}
Operating at maximum for long leaves no room for maintenance, error or special orders, and quality may fall. A utilisation of 80–90% may be better.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that fixed costs may continue"}
When comparing making and buying, only the costs that **disappear** if the firm outsources should be counted as savings.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Capacity utilisation = actual output ÷ maximum output × 100; spare capacity = maximum − actual.
- Under capacity raises unit costs because fixed costs are spread over fewer units; over capacity risks breakdowns, errors and late deliveries.
- Under-used capacity can be improved by more demand, new uses, rationalisation or flexible working; over capacity by expansion, sub-contracting or higher prices.
- Outsourcing may lower cost and allow focus, but reduces control, creates dependence and may cost jobs.
- Judge a decision to outsource by the saving that really remains after continuing fixed costs.
:::
