## Introduction

Three fictional businesses in Zimbabwe each make something different. Sipho in Bulawayo builds a single wedding cake to a customer's design. Rudo's bakery in Gweru bakes 200 loaves at a time, then switches the oven to buns. A soft-drink bottler in Harare fills thousands of identical bottles an hour without stopping. Each uses a different **method of production**, chosen to match the product, the number of customers and the cost. A business must also decide how far it should rely on machines (**capital-intensive**) or on people (**labour-intensive**).

This lesson serves ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **AS** (Papers 1 and 2). Quality systems and lean production are covered in later lessons. You should know the difference between fixed and variable costs.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Job production"}
Making a single, one-off product to a customer's order, such as a wedding dress or a bridge.
:::

:::definition{term="Batch production"}
Making a group of identical products together, one stage after another, before the equipment is changed to make a different batch.
:::

:::definition{term="Flow production"}
Continuous production of identical products on a line, with the product moving from stage to stage without a break.
:::

:::definition{term="Mass customisation"}
Producing large numbers of products on a flow-production system while allowing each customer to choose features to suit them.
:::

:::definition{term="Capital-intensive production"}
Production that uses a high proportion of machinery and equipment compared with labour.
:::

:::definition{term="Labour-intensive production"}
Production that uses a high proportion of workers compared with machinery.
:::

## The methods compared

| Method | Product | Advantages | Disadvantages | Suited to |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Job | One-off, to order | High quality; customer's needs met; can charge a high price; flexible | Costly per unit; slow; needs skilled workers; no economies of scale | Tailoring, building, art |
| Batch | Groups of identical items | Flexible between products; some economies of scale; moderate cost | Machines idle between batches; stock between stages; change-over time | Bakeries, clothing, furniture ranges |
| Flow | Standard products, continuously | Lowest cost per unit; high output; economies of scale; less skill needed | High set-up cost; inflexible; a breakdown stops the line; monotony may lower morale | Soft drinks, cement, cars |
| Mass customisation | Mass output with individual options | Choice for customers at lower cost than job production; can charge extra | Needs flexible systems and ICT; more complex; higher cost than pure flow | Printed shirts, computers built to order |

**Choosing a method** depends on the size of demand, whether the product is standard or unique, the skills and capital available, the price customers will pay, and how quickly output must change.

**Changing from one method to another.** Moving from job to batch, or from batch to flow, raises output and lowers unit cost, but it needs new equipment, a new layout and retraining. Production may fall during the change, workers may resist, and the business may lose the flexibility that its customers valued.

## Capital-intensive and labour-intensive production

| | Capital-intensive | Labour-intensive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical cost structure | High fixed costs, low variable cost per unit | Low fixed costs, higher variable cost per unit |
| Benefits | Low unit cost at high output; consistent quality; fewer workers; fast | Flexible; lower start-up cost; personal touch; many jobs; suited to small output |
| Limitations | Large investment; inflexible; skilled maintenance; risky if demand falls | Higher cost per unit at high output; slower; quality varies; labour cost grows with wages |
| Justified when | Output is large and steady, labour is costly or scarce, and quality must be uniform | Output is small or varied, capital is scarce, labour is cheap, or the personal touch is the product |

**Kaizen (ZIMSEC detail).** Kaizen is the Japanese word for continuous improvement in small steps. In organising production, workers and managers constantly look for small changes that reduce waste, errors and delay, often meeting in small groups. The result is steady gains in quality and efficiency, at low cost, but it needs the cooperation of workers and the patience to wait for the results.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: comparing two methods by cost]
**Context.** A fictional brick maker in Chinhoyi can use a labour-intensive method (fixed cost US\$1,500 a month and variable cost US\$0.40 a brick) or a capital-intensive method (fixed cost US\$6,000 and variable cost US\$0.10 a brick).

| Bricks a month | Labour-intensive total cost (US\$) | Per brick (US\$) | Capital-intensive total cost (US\$) | Per brick (US\$) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 5,000 | 3,500 | 0.70 | 6,500 | 1.30 |
| 10,000 | 5,500 | 0.55 | 7,000 | 0.70 |
| 15,000 | 7,500 | 0.50 | 7,500 | 0.50 |
| 20,000 | 9,500 | 0.48 | 8,000 | 0.40 |
| 30,000 | 13,500 | 0.45 | 9,000 | 0.30 |

1. Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost per brick × bricks. At 10,000 bricks the labour-intensive method costs 1,500 + 0.40 × 10,000 = US\$5,500.
2. The two methods cost the same where 1,500 + 0.40q = 6,000 + 0.10q, so 0.30q = 4,500 and **q = 15,000 bricks**.
3. Below 15,000 bricks the labour-intensive method is cheaper; above it the capital-intensive method is cheaper.
4. **Interpretation.** The right method depends on expected output. If the brick maker expects to sell 10,000 bricks a month, the labour-intensive method is cheaper; if sales are expected to grow past 15,000, the capital-intensive method is better. The brick maker must also weigh the risk that demand falls and leaves a large fixed cost to pay.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, moving from job to batch]
**Context.** Chipo's fictional furniture workshop in Masvingo makes each cabinet to a customer's order (job production). A school asks for 40 identical desks every term. Chipo is thinking of switching to batch production.

**Point.** Batch production would lower the cost of the desks but may reduce the personal service she gives to other customers.

**Application.** Making 40 desks in one batch lets her cut all the wood at once and set up the machines once, so each desk takes less time. She would keep a stock of parts. For single cabinets she would still need job production.

**Analysis.** Batch production reduces cost per desk through economies of scale, and the guaranteed order from the school makes the change worthwhile. But machines would sit idle between batches, the cost of holding stock rises, and workers used to craft work may dislike repetitive tasks.

**Judgement.** Chipo should use batch production for the school desks and keep job production for custom cabinets. This keeps her skilled customers while lowering the cost of the repeating order, but she should check that the school order is large and regular enough to repay the change.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling any large factory flow production"}
Flow production is **continuous** production of an identical product. A large factory that makes products in separate groups is using batch production.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that capital-intensive is always cheaper"}
Capital-intensive methods have lower unit cost only at a high enough output. At low output the large fixed cost makes the unit cost higher.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the problems of change"}
Changing the method needs equipment, retraining and time. Include the effect on workers and on flexibility in any evaluation.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *state* the methods, *describe* them with examples, *compare* labour-intensive and capital-intensive production and *discuss* kaizen. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 use *identify*, *explain*, *analyse* and *assess*. Always link the method to the product and to the volume of demand in the case. In an evaluation, state the condition: "this depends on whether output is large and steady enough to repay the investment".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Job production makes one-off products to order; batch makes groups of identical items; flow is continuous; mass customisation combines mass output with individual choice.
- Job production gives quality and flexibility at a high unit cost; flow gives low unit cost but needs high demand and large investment.
- Capital-intensive production has high fixed costs and low unit costs at high output; labour-intensive production is flexible and cheaper to start.
- Changing the method needs equipment, retraining and time, and may lose flexibility.
- Kaizen is continuous improvement through small steps made by workers (ZIMSEC).
:::
