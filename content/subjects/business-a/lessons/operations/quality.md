## Introduction

Nyasha runs a fictional bakery in Mutare that supplies bread to schools. One week a batch of loaves goes out under-baked, and three schools complain. She loses an order, throws away dough, and has to apologise in person. Quality problems cost money and customers. **Quality management** is the set of methods a business uses to make sure that its goods and services meet the standard that customers expect, at a cost the business can afford.

This lesson covers ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 6) and Cambridge **A Level** only (Papers 3 and 4). You should know the production methods, the idea of unit cost and the role of workers in motivation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Quality"}
The degree to which a good or service meets customers' expectations and is fit for its purpose.
:::

:::definition{term="Quality control"}
Checking products or inputs against a standard, usually by inspection or testing, to find and remove faults.
:::

:::definition{term="Quality assurance"}
A system that prevents faults by setting standards and organising every stage of production, involving all workers, so that quality is built in rather than inspected in.
:::

:::definition{term="Total quality management (TQM)"}
A philosophy in which every employee is responsible for quality at every stage of production, aiming for zero defects and customer satisfaction.
:::

:::definition{term="Benchmarking"}
Comparing a firm's products, processes or performance with those of the best firm (competitor or other industry) in order to find ways to improve.
:::

## Why quality matters

Good quality: builds the brand and customer loyalty; allows higher prices; reduces returns, waste and rework; reduces complaints and legal claims; helps the firm to win contracts. Poor quality raises costs and damages the reputation. Quality means **fit for purpose** (does the job it was made for), and **meets customer expectations** (price and promise). A cheap product may be of high quality for its price.

## Quality control

| Type | When | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feed-forward control | Before production: check inputs and plans | Testing flour before it is used |
| Concurrent control | During production: check as work goes on | A supervisor checks loaves as they leave the oven |
| Feedback control | After production: check finished goods and learn from faults | Inspecting finished loaves and customer complaints |

**Methods**: inspection of every item (100% inspection) or **random sampling** of a few items to estimate the quality of a batch; testing (including destructive testing, where the item is destroyed, so only a sample can be tested).

**Impact.** Quality control finds faults before the customer does and protects the brand. But it adds cost (inspectors, testing), finds faults after they have been made (so waste has already happened), may cause conflict between workers and inspectors, and does not stop the fault happening again.

## Quality assurance

Quality assurance focuses on **prevention**: setting standards for suppliers, training workers, maintaining machines, and designing processes so that faults do not occur. Workers check their own work. It may follow an agreed external standard and a written system. **Impact**: fewer defects and less waste, lower inspection costs, and greater customer trust; but it needs planning, training and time to introduce, and it relies on the commitment of all staff.

## TQM, kaizen groups, quality circles and benchmarking

- **TQM** makes quality everyone's job. The aims are zero defects and meeting customers' needs. Features: staff empowerment, training, teamwork, a focus on customers (including internal customers: the next department), and continuous improvement.
- **Kaizen groups / quality circles** are small groups of workers who meet regularly to find and solve quality and efficiency problems. They improve morale and ideas, but need time for meetings, training, support from managers and patience.
- **Benchmarking** compares the firm with the best in a particular area, for example delivery time. The firm sets targets, copies good practice and sees where it falls behind. It requires information, may be costly, and copying a rival's methods may not suit the firm.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: sampling and the cost of inspection]
**Context.** Nyasha's bakery produces batches of 5,000 rolls. An inspector checks a random sample of 200 rolls and finds 9 under-baked. A defective roll costs US\$4 to replace and deal with. Inspecting every roll would cost US\$0.30 a roll.

1. Defect rate in the sample = 9 ÷ 200 × 100 = **4.5%**.
2. Expected defective rolls in the batch = 4.5% × 5,000 = **225**.
3. Expected cost of failure if none are found = 225 × US\$4 = US\$900.
4. Cost of 100% inspection = 5,000 × 0.30 = US\$1,500, which is more than the US\$900 expected failure cost.
5. **Interpretation.** Inspecting every roll costs more than the faults it would find, so sampling plus action to **prevent** under-baking (checking the oven temperature) is better. The sample is only an estimate; a different sample could give a different rate, so the bakery should sample regularly.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, from control to assurance]
**Context.** The bakery spends the following each year on quality, before and after it introduces quality assurance (training and oven maintenance).

| Quality cost (US\$ a year) | Before | After |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Prevention (training, maintenance) | 0 | 8,000 |
| Inspection and testing | 6,000 | 3,000 |
| Failure: rework, returns, scrap | 30,000 | 12,000 |
| **Total** | 36,000 | 23,000 |

**Point.** Spending on prevention reduces the larger cost of failure.

**Application.** Prevention rose from 0 to US\$8,000, but failure costs fell by US\$18,000 and inspection by US\$3,000, so total quality cost fell by 36,000 − 23,000 = US\$13,000, or 36.1%.

**Analysis.** The bakery saves more than it spends because faults are avoided instead of found and repaired. Customers receive better bread, so complaints fall and orders may grow. However, the figures after the change are partly estimates, and the savings depend on workers keeping to the new standards.

**Judgement.** Quality assurance is clearly worthwhile on these figures. To keep the gain, Nyasha must keep training up to date, involve the bakers (a quality circle can help) and check the results each quarter against a benchmark such as a leading bakery's rate of returns.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating quality control and quality assurance as the same"}
Quality control **finds** faults after or during production by inspection. Quality assurance **prevents** faults by organising the whole system. TQM goes further by making every worker responsible.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that quality means luxury"}
Quality means meeting the customer's expectations for the price. A low-priced product can be of good quality if it does what it promises.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the cost of quality systems"}
Training, inspectors and meetings cost money. A good answer weighs these costs against the savings from fewer faults and returns.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge A Level Papers 3 and 4 use *analyse*, *evaluate* and *advise* on quality control, quality assurance, TQM and benchmarking. ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *state* the meaning of quality, *describe* inspection, TQM and kaizen groups and *discuss* their usefulness. Always apply a method to the product and workforce in the case, and compare cost with benefit. A judgement should depend on a condition, for example "quality assurance suits the firm if its workers are trained and committed".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Quality means fit for purpose and meeting customers' expectations; good quality builds loyalty and cuts waste, while poor quality raises costs.
- Quality control checks products by inspection or sampling (feed-forward, concurrent or feedback); it finds faults but adds cost.
- Quality assurance prevents faults by setting standards, training workers and organising every stage.
- TQM makes every employee responsible for quality; kaizen groups and quality circles involve workers in solving problems.
- Benchmarking compares performance with the best to set targets for improvement.
:::
