## Introduction

Farai runs a clothing factory in Gweru with forty workers. Rolls of cloth sit for weeks in the store, half-finished shirts wait in piles between the cutting and sewing sections, and about one shirt in twenty has to be mended or thrown away. Customers get their orders late, and cash is tied up in cloth that is not yet earning anything. Farai is trying to **lean** the factory: to use less of every resource, including time, materials, space, stock and labour, to produce the same or a better product.

Lean production is a Form 6 topic in ZIMSEC Business Studies and an A Level topic in Cambridge (Papers 3 and 4); it is not in the Business Enterprise Skills syllabus. You should already understand inventory control, quality and productivity.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Lean production"}
A way of producing goods or services that aims to cut waste of every kind - materials, time, stock, space, effort and defects - so that output of the required quality is made at lower cost and more quickly.
:::

:::definition{term="Kaizen"}
A Japanese word for continuous improvement: a policy in which every employee looks constantly for small improvements to the way work is done, so that costs and defects fall step by step.
:::

:::definition{term="Just-in-time (JIT) manufacturing"}
A system in which materials and components arrive only when they are needed for production and finished goods are made only to meet orders, so that very little stock is held.
:::

## Aims of lean production

The main aims are to lower unit costs, to improve quality, to shorten the time between an order and delivery, to use the workforce's ideas, and to respond quickly to changes in demand. The purpose is not just to cut costs: a business that saves money by making a worse product is not lean. Lean thinking treats anything the customer would not pay for as **waste**.

Commonly listed forms of waste are overproduction (making more than is wanted), waiting time, unnecessary transport or movement, excess stock, over-processing, defects and under-used skills. **Waste management** is the routine of identifying, measuring and removing these wastes - for example by reorganising the layout, by recording defects and tracing their causes, or by recycling off-cuts.

## The techniques

- **Kaizen.** Small improvements are suggested by the workers who do the job and put into practice at once. Many small gains add up, and workers feel ownership of the process.
- **Quality circles.** Small groups of workers from one area meet regularly, often voluntarily, to find the causes of quality problems and to suggest solutions to management. They use workers' knowledge of the job.
- **Simultaneous engineering.** Design, production, marketing and purchasing work on a new product **at the same time** instead of one after the other, so problems are found early and the product reaches the market sooner.
- **Cell production.** Instead of a long line where each worker repeats one task, a small team (a cell) makes a whole product or a complete part. Teams are flexible, find faults sooner and take responsibility for quality.
- **JIT manufacturing.** Stock of materials and finished goods is kept to a minimum. This frees cash and space but needs reliable suppliers and accurate forecasts.

## Links to other parts of operations

| Area | Effect of lean production |
| --- | --- |
| Inventory control | Much lower buffer stock; stock holding and storage costs fall; the business depends on suppliers delivering on time |
| Quality | Quality is built in at each stage, not checked at the end; defects are traced to their cause quickly |
| Employees' roles | Workers are multi-skilled, work in teams and make suggestions; they need training and more responsibility |
| Capacity management | Capacity is used for orders, not stock; spare capacity is cut, so a sudden rise in demand is harder to meet |
| Efficiency | Lower waste, higher productivity and faster delivery |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: measuring the effect of lean production]
Farai introduces cell production, JIT deliveries of cloth and a weekly kaizen meeting. The factory keeps the same 40 workers. Figures are monthly averages before and after the change.

| Measure | Before | After |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Output (garments a month) | 12,000 | 14,400 |
| Workers | 40 | 40 |
| Output per worker | 300 | 360 |
| Defective garments a month | 600 | 288 |
| Defect rate (%) | 5.0 | 2.0 |
| Average stock held (US\$) | 24,000 | 9,000 |

1. Output per worker = output ÷ workers: 12,000 ÷ 40 = 300 before and 14,400 ÷ 40 = 360 after, a rise of 20%.
2. Defect rate = defective ÷ output × 100: 600 ÷ 12,000 = 5.0% before and 288 ÷ 14,400 = 2.0% after.
3. Stock held falls by US\$15,000. If holding stock costs 20% of its value a year (an assumption), the saving is 15,000 × 20% = US\$3,000 a year, and US\$15,000 of cash is released.
4. All three measures improve, but the figures do not show the costs of the change (training, reorganising the floor) or how supplier problems might affect the risk of stopping production.
:::

## Limitations and risks

Lean production has costs and risks. It needs **reliable suppliers**: a late delivery stops production. Workers must be trained and motivated, and an improvement scheme can fail if suggestions are ignored. **Spare capacity and stock buffers** are removed, so the business may not meet a sudden order. Reorganising layouts and training costs money before the savings arrive. Changing to cells or JIT may meet resistance from workers who fear job losses. Constant pressure to improve can cause stress. Simultaneous engineering requires good communication between departments.

## When is lean production appropriate?

Lean methods suit a business that makes products with **steady, predictable demand**, has **dependable suppliers nearby**, can train a stable workforce and has a quality or cost problem to solve. They are less suitable where demand is highly seasonal or unpredictable, where roads, power or imports make deliveries unreliable, or where the product is made one at a time to a customer's design. Even then, parts such as kaizen, quality circles and waste reduction can be used without full JIT.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, should a bakery adopt JIT?]
**Context.** Tendai owns a bakery in Mutare that buys flour from one supplier 200 km away. Deliveries are sometimes a week late because of fuel shortages. Demand rises sharply before school holidays.

**Point.** JIT would cut her flour stock and the cash tied up in it.

**Application.** Her stock of flour is a safety net against late deliveries. With JIT she would run out when the supplier is late, and she could not bake enough bread for the holiday peak.

**Analysis.** The saving from a lower stock is smaller than the loss from lost sales and idle ovens. The unreliable supplier and the seasonal demand are the two conditions that make JIT risky.

**Judgement.** Tendai should not adopt full JIT. She can still use lean ideas that do not depend on supply: kaizen suggestions from bakers, a quality circle to cut spoiled loaves and a layout that reduces walking. She could revisit JIT if she found a second, reliable supplier nearby.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying lean production simply means cutting costs"}
Lean production aims to remove waste while keeping or improving quality and customer service. Cost cutting that harms quality is not lean.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing JIT with lean production"}
JIT is one technique within lean production. Kaizen, cells, quality circles and simultaneous engineering are others.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the context in evaluation"}
A benefit is not credit-worthy on its own: say whether supplier reliability, seasonal demand or the workforce make it work or fail for **this** business.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking kaizen means a big one-off change"}
Kaizen is continuous and small; the improvements are made constantly by the workforce.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (state or outline techniques, short cases) and Paper 2 (discuss and evaluate in essays); Cambridge Paper 3 (analyse the techniques in a case, with a calculation of productivity or stock costs) and Paper 4 (evaluate the use of lean production).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline/define*: name or define a technique accurately. *Describe/explain*: say how the technique works and apply it to the business. *Analyse*: give a chain, for example JIT leads to lower stock, then lower storage cost and more cash, but exposes the business to supplier delay. *Discuss/evaluate/assess*: weigh benefits and risks for this business, test them against supplier reliability and demand, and reach a justified judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Lean production aims to cut every kind of waste while keeping quality and service.
- Techniques: kaizen, quality circles, simultaneous engineering, cell production, JIT and waste management.
- It reduces stock, improves quality, makes workers multi-skilled and raises efficiency, but leaves little spare capacity.
- Risks: unreliable suppliers, unpredictable demand, training cost, resistance and stress.
- Whether it is appropriate depends on the business: stable demand and reliable suppliers favour it.
:::
