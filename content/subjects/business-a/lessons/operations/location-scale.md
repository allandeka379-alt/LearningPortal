## Introduction

A fictional clothing company, Mutare Garments, is growing. Its workshop in the city is too small, rent is rising, and its main customers are in Harare, 260 km away. The owner is considering three choices: stay and add a floor, move to a cheaper town with room to expand, or have the shirts made in another country and imported. Each is a **location decision**, and each also affects the **scale** of operations: how big the business should be. These decisions are costly and hard to reverse, so they are examined at A Level.

This lesson covers ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **A Level** only (Papers 3 and 4). You should know fixed and variable costs, unit cost, and the effects of globalisation on trade.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |

Offshoring and reshoring are named in the Cambridge syllabus. All boards examine economies and diseconomies of scale.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Location"}
The place where a business carries out its operations, such as its factory, shop or office.
:::

:::definition{term="Offshoring"}
Moving a business process or production to another country, usually to lower costs, while still serving the original market.
:::

:::definition{term="Reshoring"}
Bringing production or business processes that were moved overseas back to the home country.
:::

:::definition{term="Economies of scale"}
Reductions in the average (unit) cost of production that occur as the scale of operations increases.
:::

:::definition{term="Diseconomies of scale"}
Increases in the average (unit) cost that occur when the scale of operations becomes too large.
:::

## Factors affecting location and relocation

| Type | Factors |
| --- | --- |
| Quantitative (can be costed) | Cost of land and rent; labour cost; transport cost to suppliers and customers; utilities; taxes and grants; cost of relocation |
| Qualitative (judgement) | Availability and skills of labour; closeness to customers and suppliers; roads, power, water and internet; the law and political stability; the owner's preference and family; reputation and image; the climate and the environment; room to expand |

A business **relocates** when the present site no longer suits it: rents rise, the market moves, the site is too small, or a better site has come available. Relocation has costs (moving, retraining, lost output, loss of experienced staff who will not move), and benefits (lower costs, better access to customers, room to grow).

**Local, national and international location.** A local firm (a bakery) serves a nearby market and needs a site near its customers. A national firm needs a location with good transport links to the whole country. An international firm must consider exchange rates, trade barriers, law, culture and distance, and may choose between exporting and producing abroad.

**Offshoring and reshoring.** Firms offshore to reach lower labour costs, new markets or skills. Problems include longer supply chains, language and quality control, risk of delay and the effect on jobs at home. Reasons for reshoring include rising overseas wages, transport costs and delays, a desire for quality and control, customers' preference for local products, and the risk of disruption.

**Globalisation and location.** The growth of world trade, easier transport and communications have allowed firms to choose sites anywhere, and many serve world markets from a few locations. Firms may be attracted by trade agreements and special zones, but face stronger competition from imports.

## Scale of operations

The **scale** is the size of a business's operations, measured by output, capital or workers. Factors influencing the right scale are the size of the market, the amount of capital available, the nature of the product (a bridge needs a larger scale than a handmade basket), the owner's objectives and the ability to manage a large organisation.

### Economies and diseconomies of scale

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Internal economies | Technical (larger and faster machines); purchasing (bulk discounts); financial (cheaper loans); managerial (specialist managers); marketing (promotion spread over more units) |
| External economies | A skilled labour force in the area; supplier firms nearby; good roads and services; a reputation for a type of product in the area |
| Internal diseconomies | Poor communication; slow decisions; lower morale and motivation; weak coordination |
| External diseconomies | Traffic and congestion; rising land and wage costs when many firms compete for the same resources |

The link to cost: as output rises, unit cost falls while economies dominate, reaches a low point, and then rises when diseconomies dominate. The best scale is where unit cost is lowest.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: choosing a location by cost]
**Context.** A fictional furniture maker is choosing between three sites. The yearly costs are in the table.

| Site | Rent (US\$) | Labour (US\$) | Transport (US\$) | Total yearly cost (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Site A (Harare) | 54,000 | 120,000 | 38,000 | 212,000 |
| Site B (Gweru) | 30,000 | 126,000 | 52,000 | 208,000 |
| Site C (Chinhoyi) | 24,000 | 132,000 | 46,000 | 202,000 |

1. Totals: A = 54,000 + 120,000 + 38,000 = 212,000; B = 208,000; C = 202,000.
2. Site C is the cheapest, US\$10,000 a year cheaper than Site A (4.7% less).
3. **Qualitative factors.** Site A is nearest the main customers and has the best infrastructure. Site C has the lowest rent but the highest labour cost, which suggests it may be harder to find skilled workers there.
4. **Judgement.** The US\$10,000 gap is small compared with total costs, so a manager may prefer Site A if closeness to customers brings more sales, and Site C only if its costs stay low as the business grows. The final choice should weigh costs against sales, and cost of relocation.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, the right scale]
**Context.** A fictional bottler produces more each year. The cost of production at different outputs is shown.

| Output (units) | Total cost (US\$) | Cost per unit (US\$) | Change in unit cost |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 10,000 | 90,000 | 9.00 | - |
| 20,000 | 150,000 | 7.50 | -16.7% |
| 40,000 | 240,000 | 6.00 | -20.0% |
| 80,000 | 520,000 | 6.50 | +8.3% |

**Point.** The unit cost falls up to 40,000 units, then rises.

**Application.** From 10,000 to 40,000 units the cost per unit falls from US\$9.00 to US\$6.00. At 80,000 units it rises to US\$6.50.

**Analysis.** Economies of scale (bulk buying of bottles, larger machines, spreading advertising) explain the fall. Diseconomies (slower decisions, difficult supervision, tired machines) explain the rise: doubling output from 40,000 to 80,000 more than doubles total cost (240,000 to 520,000, an increase of 117%).

**Judgement.** The best scale on these figures is about 40,000 units, where cost per unit is lowest. Before expanding beyond it, the bottler should find ways to manage a larger firm better. If demand exceeds 40,000 units, a second plant might be better than stretching the first.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Only listing factors"}
A location answer must **apply** the factors to the business: its market, product, suppliers and labour. Say which factors matter most and why.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing internal and external economies"}
Internal economies arise from the firm's own size (bulk buying). External economies arise from the size of the **industry or area** (skilled labour nearby).
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming growth always cuts unit cost"}
Beyond a certain size, diseconomies raise unit cost. Say what the firm should do about them (better communication, delegation, splitting the firm).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge A Level Papers 3 and 4 use *analyse*, *evaluate* and *advise* on location, offshoring and scale. ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *state* factors, *describe* economies and diseconomies and *discuss* a relocation. Apply every factor to the business in the case, use figures where they are given, and weigh quantitative against qualitative factors. A judgement should depend on a condition, for example "relocation is justified if the cost saving exceeds the cost of moving within a few years".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Location depends on quantitative factors (rent, labour, transport) and qualitative factors (skills, infrastructure, law, closeness to customers).
- Relocation brings lower costs or better access but has costs of moving, retraining and lost staff.
- Offshoring moves work abroad for lower cost; reshoring brings it back for quality, control and reliability.
- Economies of scale (internal and external) reduce unit cost as output rises; diseconomies raise it when the firm is too large.
- The best scale is where unit cost is lowest, but demand and management ability limit it.
:::
