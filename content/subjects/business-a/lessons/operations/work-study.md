## Introduction

Rudo runs a small bakery in Masvingo. Every morning her workers carry loaves from the oven room to a packing table thirty metres away, then to an inspection table, then to the counter. Some workers finish packing in half the time of others, and Rudo cannot tell whether the slow ones are lazy or whether the job is badly arranged. **Work study** gives her a way to find out. It looks at how a job is done and at how long it should take, so that she can improve the method and set fair targets.

Work study is a **ZIMSEC-only** topic (Business Studies Form 6 and Business Enterprise Skills Form 6). It is not in the Cambridge syllabus, although Cambridge candidates meet similar ideas as productivity and efficiency. You should already understand productivity and the main methods of production.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Work study"}
The systematic examination of the way work is done, in order to find the best method and to measure how long the work should take, so as to improve efficiency and productivity.
:::

:::definition{term="Method study"}
The recording and critical examination of how a job is done at present, in order to develop a simpler, quicker or cheaper way of doing it.
:::

:::definition{term="Work measurement"}
Finding the time a qualified worker should take to carry out a job at a defined level of performance, by using techniques such as time study.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Standard time"}
Average observed time × rating ÷ 100 = **basic time**. Basic time × (1 + allowance) = **standard time**. The allowance (for rest, personal needs and unavoidable delay) is a percentage of the basic time. Rating compares the worker's pace with a normal pace of 100.
:::

## Method study

Method study has three steps.

1. **Record.** Describe the existing method, usually by listing each step in order and showing it as an operation, a transport (movement), an inspection, a delay or a storage. Charts, process tables and sketches of the layout are used.
2. **Examine.** Question every step: What is done? Why is it done? Where is it done? When? Who does it? Is there a better way? The aim is to remove, combine, re-arrange or simplify steps.
3. **Improve.** Develop a better method, put it into practice and check that it works.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: method study at a bakery]
Rudo records how loaves are packed (distances are in metres).

| Step | Activity (before) | Type | Distance (m) |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| 1 | Carry loaves from oven room to packing table | Transport | 30 |
| 2 | Wait for loaves to cool | Delay | 0 |
| 3 | Slice loaves | Operation | 0 |
| 4 | Bag loaves | Operation | 0 |
| 5 | Carry bags to inspection table | Transport | 12 |
| 6 | Check bags | Inspection | 0 |
| 7 | Carry bags to counter | Transport | 18 |
| 8 | Label and shelve | Operation | 0 |

She asks why the packing table is far from the oven room and why the check is done at a separate table. She moves the packing table next door and checks the bags where they are packed.

| Step | Activity (after) | Type | Distance (m) |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| 1 | Carry loaves from oven room to packing table next door | Transport | 8 |
| 2 | Wait for loaves to cool | Delay | 0 |
| 3 | Slice loaves | Operation | 0 |
| 4 | Bag loaves and check on the same table | Operation | 0 |
| 5 | Carry bags to counter | Transport | 18 |
| 6 | Label and shelve | Operation | 0 |

1. Before: 8 steps, 3 transports covering 30 + 12 + 18 = 60 metres.
2. After: 6 steps, 2 transports covering 8 + 18 = 26 metres.
3. Distance saved per batch = 60 − 26 = 34 metres. At a walking pace of 60 metres a minute (an assumption) that saves 34 ÷ 60 = 0.57 minutes a batch, or about 14.2 minutes over 25 batches a day.
4. The saving is small for one worker, but it is free: no new equipment is needed.
:::

## Work measurement and time study

In a **time study** the job is broken into elements and a trained observer times several repetitions with a stopwatch. The observer also gives a **rating**: if the worker is judged to be working faster than a normal pace the rating is above 100 (for example 120), and if slower, below 100. The average observed time is converted into a **basic time**, which is the time a worker at normal pace would take, and then an **allowance** is added to give the **standard time**. The standard time is used to set output targets, plan how many workers are needed, cost jobs and compare workers fairly.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a time study]
An observer times eight repetitions of bagging a batch of loaves for Rudo's bakery. The worker is rated at 120. Rudo allows 15% of the basic time for rest and delay.

| Observation | Time (minutes) |
| --- | ---: |
| 1 | 2.4 |
| 2 | 2.6 |
| 3 | 2.5 |
| 4 | 2.3 |
| 5 | 2.7 |
| 6 | 2.5 |
| 7 | 2.4 |
| 8 | 2.6 |

| Step | Working | Result |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Average observed time | 20.0 ÷ 8 | 2.50 min |
| Basic time | 2.50 × 120 ÷ 100 | 3.00 min |
| Standard time | 3.00 × 1.15 | 3.45 min |

1. The eight times total 20.0 minutes, so the average is 2.50 minutes.
2. Because the worker was working faster than normal (rating 120), the basic time is larger than the observed time: 2.50 × 1.20 = 3.00 minutes.
3. Adding the 15% allowance gives a standard time of 3.45 minutes a batch.
4. In an 8-hour day (480 minutes) one worker should complete 480 ÷ 3.45 = 139 batches (rounding down to whole batches). For 1,000 batches a day Rudo needs 1,000 × 3.45 ÷ 480 = 7.2 worker-days, so 8 workers.
:::

## Carrying out a simple study

A simple study of a local venture (for example a tuck shop, a bakery or a sewing group) follows these steps: choose the job; tell the workers what is being done and why; record the present method; time the job using a watch; examine the steps and suggest improvements; try them out; and check the results.

## How useful are the results?

**Benefits.** Work study can raise productivity and cut costs without buying equipment, and gives objective standards for targets, pay systems and costing. It can identify wasteful movement, delay and unnecessary steps.

**Limitations.** Workers may feel watched and resist, or slow down on purpose while being timed. Rating is a matter of judgement and can be unfair. It applies best to repetitive jobs, less to creative work. The study itself takes time and skill, and the results are only useful if management puts the changes into practice.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, how far should Rudo rely on the study?]
**Context.** Rudo's study gives a standard time of 3.45 minutes a batch for bagging, but her best bagger, Sipho, thinks the rating of 120 is unfair because he was nervous.

**Point.** The study gives a clear target but its accuracy depends on the rating.

**Application.** If the rating had been 110 the basic time would be 2.75 minutes and the standard time 3.16 minutes, a difference of about 0.29 minutes a batch, or roughly 8%. In an 8-hour shift one worker would be expected to complete 151 batches instead of 139, a difference of 12 batches.

**Analysis.** A small error in the rating changes the targets noticeably, and a target that is too high demotivates workers while one that is too low wastes labour.

**Judgement.** Rudo should repeat the timing on several days and with several workers before using the standard for pay or targets, but she should use the method study results at once, as moving the packing table is cheap and clearly saves effort.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing method study and work measurement"}
Method study asks **how** the job should be done; work measurement asks **how long** it should take. Time study is a technique of work measurement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Applying the allowance to the observed time"}
The allowance is added to the **basic** time (after the rating), not to the average observed time.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the rating the wrong way round"}
A rating above 100 means the worker was faster than normal, so the basic time is **longer** than the observed time.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only advantages"}
An evaluation of work study needs limits such as worker resistance, subjective rating and cost of the study.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (calculate a standard time, state the steps of method study) and Paper 2 (essays on improving efficiency and productivity); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2. It is not tested by Cambridge.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline*: name the steps of method study or the parts of the standard time. *Describe/explain*: say what each step does for a local venture. *Calculate*: show the average, rating and allowance in order. *Discuss/evaluate*: weigh increased productivity against worker resistance, cost and the reliability of the rating, and reach a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Work study examines how work is done and how long it should take, to raise efficiency and productivity.
- Method study: record the present method, examine each step critically, and develop and install an improvement.
- Work measurement (time study) gives a standard time: average observed time × rating ÷ 100, then add the allowance.
- A simple study of a local venture records, times, examines and tests improvements.
- Benefits: lower cost, higher productivity, fair standards. Limits: resistance, subjective rating, time and skill needed.
:::
