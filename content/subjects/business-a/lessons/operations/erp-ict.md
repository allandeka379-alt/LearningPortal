## Introduction

Tatenda owns a furniture factory in Bulawayo with a sales office, a store, a workshop and an accounts office. For years each section kept its own records: the sales clerk wrote orders in a book, the storekeeper counted timber on cards and the accountant typed costs into a spreadsheet. Orders were lost, the store ran out of varnish while the accounts office thought it had plenty, and nobody could say how much a wardrobe really cost to make. Tatenda is considering an **enterprise resource planning (ERP)** system, which joins all these records in one program.

ERP and the use of ICT in operations are tested in ZIMSEC Business Studies Form 6 and in Cambridge A Level (Papers 3 and 4). They are not in the Business Enterprise Skills syllabus. You should already understand inventory control, costing, capacity and productivity.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC (Business Studies Form 6) | Cambridge A Level |
| --- | --- |
| Impact of ICT on operations: computer-aided design (CAD), computer-aided manufacturing (CAM), computerised inventory (stock) control, computerised costing | Enterprise resource planning (ERP): features and effect on inventory control, costing and pricing, capacity utilisation, response to change, workforce flexibility, management information |

**CAD and CAM are ZIMSEC detail** and ERP is the Cambridge A Level focus; ZIMSEC candidates can use ERP as an example of integrated ICT, and Cambridge candidates may use CAD and CAM as examples of technology in operations.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Enterprise resource planning (ERP)"}
Software that links the main functions of a business - such as sales, purchasing, inventory, production, finance and human resources - through a single database, so that every department works with the same up-to-date information.
:::

:::definition{term="Computer-aided design (CAD) and computer-aided manufacturing (CAM)"}
CAD uses computers to create, test and change designs of products; CAM uses computers to control machines and the process of making them. When linked, a design can pass straight to the machines that produce it.
:::

:::definition{term="Management information system"}
A system that collects and processes data from across the business and presents it in the form of reports, so that managers can plan, monitor and decide.
:::

## Features of an ERP system

An ERP system has **modules** (sales, inventory, production planning, accounting, human resources) which all read and write to **one shared database**. When a sales clerk records an order, the stock module shows whether materials are available, the production module schedules the work, and the accounts module raises the invoice. Information is entered once and is available at once. Reports can be produced instantly for any manager. Access is controlled by passwords so that people see only what they need.

## How ERP improves efficiency

| Area | Effect of an ERP system |
| --- | --- |
| Inventory control | Real-time stock levels; automatic reorder points; fewer stock-outs and less excess stock |
| Costing and pricing | Materials, labour and overheads are recorded against each job, giving accurate costs and better prices |
| Capacity utilisation | Production schedules show spare capacity and bottlenecks, so machines and workers are used more fully |
| Response to change | A change in an order or in a supplier's delivery is seen by all departments at once and plans can be updated |
| Workforce flexibility | Staff can be rostered across tasks using shared data on workloads and skills; less time is lost passing paper between sections |
| Management information | Up-to-date reports on sales, costs and stock support decisions, replacing guesses with data |

The **ZIMSEC ICT tools** work in the same way for smaller areas. CAD cuts the time to design and change products and allows designs to be tested on screen. CAM gives accuracy, repeatability and less waste. Computerised stock control scans items in and out and warns when stock is low. Computerised costing records costs for each job as they happen.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: is an ERP system worth buying?]
Tatenda is offered an ERP system. It costs US\$24,000 to buy and US\$6,000 to install and train staff, so US\$30,000 in all, and a support fee of US\$4,000 a year. She estimates the annual benefits below (all figures are assumptions for the example).

| Annual benefit | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Lower stock held: (60,000 − 45,000) × 20% holding cost | 3,000 |
| Fewer stock-outs: lost profit avoided | 5,400 |
| Admin time saved: 1,500 hours × US\$3.00 | 4,500 |
| Fewer invoice and costing errors | 2,700 |
| **Total annual benefit** | 15,600 |
| Less annual support fee | (4,000) |
| **Net annual benefit** | 11,600 |

1. Stock held falls by US\$15,000; at a holding cost of 20% a year this saves US\$3,000.
2. Total benefits = 3,000 + 5,400 + 4,500 + 2,700 = US\$15,600; less the support fee of 4,000 gives a net benefit of US\$11,600 a year.
3. Payback = outlay ÷ net annual benefit = 30,000 ÷ 11,600 = 2.59 years (to two decimal places), so the system pays for itself in about two and a half years.
4. The figures are estimates. The system needs good data, trained users and reliable electricity, and benefits such as better decisions are not counted.
:::

## Costs, risks and limitations

- **Cost.** Software, hardware, installation, data transfer and training can be expensive for a small business, and support fees continue.
- **Disruption and resistance.** Staff must learn new methods, and some resist. Mistakes in the early months can cause errors in stock and invoices.
- **Data quality.** The system is only as good as the information entered. Wrong stock counts produce wrong decisions.
- **Dependence and security.** If the system or the electricity fails, the whole business is affected. Shared data must be protected from misuse and loss.
- **Fit.** A standard package may not match the way the business works, and customising it is expensive.

A small business may use only some modules, or a simple computerised stock and costing program, before moving to a full ERP system.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, ERP for a small factory]
**Context.** Tatenda's factory has 35 workers and unreliable power. Her accounts are accurate but separate from stock records, and customers often complain about late delivery.

**Point.** An ERP system would link orders, stock and production so that late deliveries fall.

**Application.** With real-time stock, the store sees that varnish is low when an order for 40 chairs is entered, and orders it at once. The shared schedule shows that the workshop is busy in March, so delivery dates are set realistically. The accounts module shows what each wardrobe costs, so she can adjust her prices.

**Analysis.** Better information reduces stock-outs and delays, and accurate costs prevent underpricing. But the system costs US\$30,000 and needs trained staff. With unreliable power, a failure could stop all recording unless she has a back-up generator and regular back-ups of the data.

**Judgement.** Tatenda should introduce the stock and costing modules first, because they address her main problems, and extend to the others once staff are confident and a back-up supply of power is in place.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing ERP as just 'a computer'"}
ERP is software that integrates the functions of the business through one database. Say what is shared and what it changes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing benefits without applying them"}
'Better management information' earns little. Say which decision is improved, for example a price set from an accurate job cost.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the risks"}
Evaluation needs cost, training, data quality and dependence on the system, as well as benefits.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up CAD and CAM"}
CAD is for **design**; CAM is for **making**. Both are ZIMSEC terms for ICT in operations.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (state or outline ICT tools) and Paper 2 (discuss the impact of ICT on operations); Cambridge Paper 3 (analyse how ERP affects a named area) and Paper 4 (evaluate whether a business should introduce ERP).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline* (ZIMSEC): name the tool and one use. *Explain/analyse*: link the feature to an area of operations (stock, costing, capacity) in the case. *Evaluate/assess/discuss*: weigh the benefits against cost, training and risk, test the answer against the size of the business and its data, and make a recommendation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- ERP links sales, inventory, production, finance and HR through one database.
- It improves inventory control, costing and pricing, capacity use, response to change, flexibility and management information.
- ZIMSEC also names CAD, CAM and computerised stock control and costing.
- Costs: purchase, installation, training, support, disruption, data errors and dependence on the system.
- Judge ERP by comparing benefits and cost for the size and circumstances of the business.
:::
