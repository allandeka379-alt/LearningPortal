## Introduction

Nyasha owns a bakery in Gaborone. For most of the year she bakes about 2,000 loaves a week, but before the festive season orders jump to 2,600. A hotel client has asked for a gluten-free loaf, and another customer wants delivery by 6 a.m. instead of 9 a.m. Can the bakery respond? That depends on her **operations strategy**: the long-term plan for how the business will use its resources to produce goods and services so that it meets its overall aims. It also depends on how **flexible** her operations are.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Operations strategy"}
A long-term plan for how a business will organise and use its resources - people, equipment, materials, money and technology - to produce its goods or services in a way that supports its overall objectives.
:::

:::definition{term="Flexibility"}
The ability of operations to change quickly and cheaply in response to changes in what customers want, in terms of the volume produced, the time of delivery or the specification of the product.
:::

:::definition{term="Process innovation"}
Changing the way goods or services are produced or delivered, either by improving current processes or by adopting new ways of working, in order to cut cost, raise quality or speed.
:::

## How resource availability shapes operations decisions

Operations decisions are never made in isolation. Three resource areas set the limits.

- **Human resources.** The number of workers, their skills and the cost of hiring or training them decide what the business can make. A firm with few skilled workers may not take a specialist order. A multi-skilled workforce makes it easier to switch between products.
- **Marketing.** Customer demand, the product range and the promotion budget shape how much has to be produced and how quickly. A business that promises fast delivery in its marketing must have operations able to deliver it.
- **Finance.** The cash available limits investment in machines and stock. A business short of funds may choose to lease equipment, outsource or accept a lower level of capacity. Lenders will also expect operations to repay what is borrowed.

Weak links between these areas cause problems, for example a marketing campaign that brings orders the factory cannot meet.

## Information technology and artificial intelligence in operations

Information technology has changed operations management. **Computer-aided design and manufacturing** speed up the creation of new products and the setting up of machines. Computerised systems record stock and orders in real time, so the manager can see what is needed. **Artificial intelligence (AI)** extends this: it can analyse past sales and conditions to forecast demand, plan routes for deliveries, predict when a machine will fail so that it can be mended before it breaks and check products for faults by image.

The benefits are faster response, fewer errors, lower waste and better use of capacity. The costs are the price of equipment and software, the need for skilled staff, the risk of system failure or poor data, and possible job losses or retraining needs. Small businesses may use simple tools such as spreadsheets or mobile apps before adopting advanced systems.

## The need for flexibility

| Type of flexibility | Meaning | Ways to achieve it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Volume | Change the quantity produced up or down | Temporary or part-time staff, overtime, subcontracting, spare capacity, leasing equipment |
| Delivery time | Respond faster or slower to orders | Stock of finished goods, extra shifts, multi-skilled staff, local suppliers, quicker transport |
| Specification | Change the features of the product | Flexible machines, modular designs, skilled workers, computer-controlled equipment |

Flexibility is valuable when demand is seasonal or changing, when customers want tailor-made goods and when competitors respond quickly. Its cost is that equipment and staff that are not fully used raise the cost of each unit, and multi-skilled labour is paid more.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: choosing how to meet a peak]
Nyasha's bakery has a capacity of 2,000 loaves a week. In the festive weeks demand is 2,600 loaves, so the bakery would be at 130% of capacity (2,600 ÷ 2,000 × 100), 600 loaves above what it can make. Normal variable cost is US\$0.60 a loaf (US\$0.30 labour and US\$0.30 ingredients). She sells each loaf for US\$1.20. She compares three ways to supply the extra 600 loaves (all figures are assumptions).

| Option | Extra loaves | Extra cost (US\$) | Extra profit (US\$) | Cost per loaf (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Overtime for current staff | 600 | 450.00 | 270.00 | 0.75 |
| Hire temporary staff for the peak | 600 | 480.00 | 240.00 | 0.80 |
| Buy bread from a partner bakery | 600 | 510.00 | 210.00 | 0.85 |

1. Overtime: labour is paid at one and a half times, so 0.30 × 1.5 = 0.45, plus 0.30 for ingredients, = 0.75 a loaf; 600 × 0.75 = US\$450.
2. Temporary staff: 0.60 a loaf plus a fixed US\$120 for recruitment and training: 600 × 0.60 + 120 = US\$480.
3. Buying in: 600 × 0.85 = US\$510.
4. Extra profit = 600 × 1.20 − extra cost: US\$270, US\$240 and US\$210. Overtime is the cheapest, but workers may tire and quality may fall.
:::

## Process innovation

Process innovation can mean making small improvements to current processes (a better layout, new scheduling) or adopting a new way of producing or delivering altogether (for example robots, 3D printing or online ordering with home delivery). It can lower costs, raise quality and speed up delivery, but it needs investment, may disturb production while it is introduced, and may meet resistance from staff. It should be judged against its cost and the risk that the new process does not work as expected.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, how flexible should the bakery be?]
**Context.** Nyasha's customers include hotels that now ask for special loaves at short notice, and orders are seasonal. Her cash reserves are low.

**Point.** Greater flexibility in specification and delivery time would let the bakery win these orders.

**Application.** She could train bakers to work on several recipes (human resources), buy a programmable oven that can switch quickly between products (finance) and promise early delivery in her marketing. The hotels pay more for special loaves, but the oven would cost a large sum when her cash is limited.

**Analysis.** More flexibility means higher revenue from special orders and less waste from unsold standard loaves, but spare capacity and a more expensive oven raise unit cost. Because her cash is low, a large purchase would put the business at risk if hotel orders fell.

**Judgement.** Nyasha should first gain flexibility cheaply, by training bakers and hiring temporary staff for peaks, and should lease the oven rather than buy it. She can then judge from the hotel orders whether more investment is worthwhile.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating flexibility as always good"}
Flexibility costs money. Idle capacity and multi-skilled staff raise costs, so it pays only where demand or specifications really vary.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing technology without application"}
Saying 'AI improves efficiency' earns little. Say what it does in the business (forecasts bread demand, schedules deliveries) and what it costs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the three resource areas"}
A good answer links an operations decision to people, marketing and finance, not to machines alone.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the three types of flexibility"}
Volume is how much, delivery time is how fast, specification is what features. Name the type being discussed.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
Cambridge Paper 3 (a calculation of cost or capacity use followed by analysis) and Paper 4 (extended evaluation of operations strategy, technology or flexibility in a case). It is not a separate ZIMSEC topic.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain/analyse*: link the type of flexibility or technology to the business's customers, workforce and cost. *Assess/evaluate/advise*: weigh the gain in responsiveness against the cost and the risk, say what the answer depends on (demand, cash, skills) and make a recommendation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Operations strategy uses people, marketing and finance resources to meet the business's long-term aims.
- IT and AI improve forecasting, scheduling, design and quality control but cost money and skills.
- Flexibility is needed in volume, delivery time and specification; each has cost and benefit.
- Process innovation changes how work is done, with investment, disruption and resistance as costs.
- Judge flexibility against demand, cash and the skills of the workforce.
:::
