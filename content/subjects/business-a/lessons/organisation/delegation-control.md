## Introduction

Rudo owns a fictional chain of three hardware shops in Gweru, Kwekwe and Masvingo. For years she checked every invoice, approved every order and visited each shop twice a week. Her business grew, but so did the delays: customers waited for her signature and she had no time to plan for a fourth shop. Rudo decides to hand some decisions to her shop managers, such as approving orders up to US\$500. A month later one manager orders too much cement, and Rudo asks, "Who is to blame?" The answer depends on what delegation does and does not pass on.

This lesson applies to ZIMSEC Business Studies (Form 6), Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **A Level** (Papers 3 and 4). It builds on the lesson on structure features, where authority, responsibility and span of control are introduced.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Delegation"}
Passing authority to a subordinate to carry out a task or make a decision, while the manager remains accountable for the outcome.
:::

:::definition{term="Accountability"}
The obligation to report on and justify the results of tasks to a superior. A person who is accountable can be called to account for success or failure.
:::

:::definition{term="Authority"}
The right to give instructions, make decisions and use resources.
:::

:::definition{term="Responsibility"}
The duty to complete assigned tasks to a satisfactory standard.
:::

:::definition{term="Centralised control"}
A situation in which a manager keeps most decisions and monitors closely, in contrast to delegating them. (See also the lesson on centralisation.)
:::

## Delegation and accountability

When a manager delegates, three things are passed to the subordinate: the **task** (responsibility), the **authority** to do it and the **right to make decisions** within limits. What is **not** passed on is the manager's own accountability to a superior. If a shop manager orders too much stock, Rudo is still accountable to herself as owner (and, in a company, to the directors and shareholders). The shop manager is accountable to Rudo. This is sometimes expressed as: *authority and responsibility can be delegated; ultimate accountability cannot*.

For delegation to work, **authority must match responsibility**. A manager who is responsible for sales but cannot change prices or hire staff cannot succeed.

## Processes of accountability

| Process | What it involves |
| --- | --- |
| Clear targets and standards | Subordinates know what is expected and by when |
| Regular reporting | Written or oral reports on progress, costs and problems |
| Monitoring and review meetings | Manager and subordinate compare results with targets |
| Performance appraisal | Formal assessment of results, with feedback |
| Audit or checks | Independent inspection of records and quality |
| Consequences | Recognition or reward for success; corrective action or discipline for failure |

Accountability should be designed so that managers can check results without removing the subordinate's freedom to act.

## The impact of delegation on a business

| Benefits | Costs and risks |
| --- | --- |
| Frees the senior manager's time for strategy | Subordinates may make mistakes |
| Decisions are made faster, by people closer to the customer | The delegator may lose control or be unaware of problems |
| Develops skills and prepares future managers | Training and supervision cost time and money |
| Motivates staff by giving them responsibility and recognition | Staff may be overloaded or unwilling to accept responsibility |
| Uses specialist knowledge of subordinates | Inconsistent decisions between subordinates |

Delegation is more successful when staff are skilled, the task is clear and limits are set, and when the culture accepts that people learn from mistakes.

## Span of control and levels of hierarchy

A business with a fixed number of employees can be organised with **narrow spans and many levels** or **wide spans and few levels**. Delegation allows spans to widen because subordinates are trusted to work without close supervision. As a result:

- the **chain of command is shorter**, so communication is faster;
- fewer managers are needed, so costs fall;
- each manager supervises more people, which is possible only if staff are capable and the work is not too complex.

If a manager's span is too wide for the work, supervision suffers and mistakes increase. If it is too narrow, managers are underused and may supervise too closely, which can discourage staff.

## Control versus trust

The central dilemma of delegation is the conflict between **control** and **trust**. A manager who checks everything keeps control but shows distrust, which reduces motivation, wastes time and defeats the purpose of delegating. A manager who trusts completely may not notice problems until too late. The balance depends on the **ability and experience** of the subordinate, the **importance and risk** of the task, the **cost of an error**, the **time available** and the manager's own **attitude to risk**. A common solution is to set limits (such as an order limit of US\$500) and to check results at agreed intervals, reducing checks as trust grows.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how many levels does a given span allow?]
**Context.** A fictional factory near Mutare has one managing director. Every manager at every level has a span of control of 4.

| Level | Working | People at that level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 (managing director) | 1 | 1 |
| 2 | 1 x 4 | 4 |
| 3 | 4 x 4 | 16 |
| 4 | 16 x 4 | 64 |
| Total employees in four levels | 1 + 4 + 16 + 64 | 85 |

1. With a span of 4, four levels give 64 operatives at the bottom, supported by 1 + 4 + 16 = 21 managers (everyone who has subordinates).
2. Suppose the factory keeps the 64 operatives but each manager can handle a span of 8. Then 64 / 8 = 8 supervisors report to one director: only three levels and 1 + 8 = 9 managers, a total of 73 employees.
3. Widening the span from 4 to 8 removes a level and cuts the number of managers from 21 to 9, a saving of 12 managers. A shorter chain of command speeds messages, but a span of 8 needs capable staff and delegation.
4. Judgement: if the work is routine and the staff well trained, the wider span saves management cost. If the work is complex or risky, the narrower span is safer.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, delegation in a hardware chain]
**Context.** After Rudo delegates order approval up to US\$500 to her shop managers, the Kwekwe manager orders a large quantity of cement worth US\$480 because a supplier discount is offered, and sales do not rise. A month later, the Masvingo manager uses the freedom to negotiate a better price from a supplier and saves US\$300.

**Point.** Delegation brings benefits and risks at the same time.

**Application.** The Masvingo manager used authority and local knowledge to cut costs. The Kwekwe manager made a poor decision within the limit, but the loss is capped at US\$500, because Rudo set a limit.

**Analysis.** Rudo remains accountable to herself as owner for the overall profit of the chain, so she should not blame the manager alone. Accountability processes such as a monthly review of orders would show the poor decision and let her coach the manager. Delegation has also freed her time to plan the fourth shop.

**Judgement.** Delegation is worthwhile because the potential loss is limited and the gains in time and speed are large. The limit should rise as managers show good judgement, and Rudo should review orders monthly instead of approving each one.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that delegating passes on all accountability"}
The manager delegates tasks and authority, but remains accountable to a superior for the result.
:::

:::mistake{title="Seeing delegation only as an advantage"}
Delegation carries risks such as errors, loss of control and overloading staff. Always consider both sides and the conditions that make it work.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up authority and responsibility"}
Authority is the right to decide; responsibility is the duty to complete the task. Authority must match responsibility.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Delegation passes tasks and authority to subordinates; the manager remains accountable to a superior.
- Authority must match responsibility for delegation to work.
- Accountability is enforced through targets, reporting, reviews, appraisal and audits.
- Delegation saves time, speeds decisions and develops staff, but risks mistakes and loss of control.
- Delegation allows wider spans, fewer levels, a shorter chain of command and lower management cost.
- Control and trust conflict: the balance depends on the subordinate's ability, the risk of the task and the cost of an error.
:::
