## Introduction

Thandeka's fictional company, Kwekwe Agri-Supplies, began as one shop selling seed and fertiliser, with a manager for sales and a manager for stock. Ten years later it also sells irrigation equipment and animal feed, and operates in Harare, Bulawayo and Mutare. The old structure no longer works: the sales manager does not understand irrigation, the Mutare customers wait for Harare to approve prices, and the feed team is competing with the fertiliser team for the same delivery vehicles. The directors must decide how to reorganise. There are several standard types of structure, each with its own advantages and disadvantages.

This lesson applies to ZIMSEC Business Studies (Form 6), Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **A Level** (Papers 3 and 4). You should already know span of control, levels of hierarchy and the chain of command.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |
| Functional, divisional, matrix; tall and flat hierarchical structures | Functional, hierarchical (tall and flat), matrix, product-based and geographic structures |

"Divisional" in ZIMSEC covers divisions by product or by region, which Cambridge names separately as product-based and geographic.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Functional structure"}
A structure in which employees are grouped by the work they do, such as production, marketing, finance and human resources, each department headed by a specialist manager.
:::

:::definition{term="Matrix structure"}
A structure in which employees report to two managers at once: a functional manager and a project or product manager. Teams are formed across the functions for particular projects.
:::

:::definition{term="Product-based (divisional) structure"}
A structure in which the business is divided into divisions, each responsible for a product or product group, with its own functions such as production and marketing.
:::

:::definition{term="Geographic structure"}
A structure in which the business is divided by region or country, each region managed as a unit with its own staff.
:::

## The main types

| Type | How it is organised | Advantages | Disadvantages | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Functional | By function: production, marketing, finance, HR | Specialist skills; clear responsibilities; economies of scale; simple | Departments may focus on their own goals; slow cross-department decisions; poor fit for many products | Small and medium businesses with one product or a few similar ones |
| Tall hierarchy | Many levels; narrow spans | Close supervision; clear promotion path | Slow communication; high management cost; staff feel little freedom | Work needing tight control or safety |
| Flat hierarchy | Few levels; wide spans | Faster communication; lower cost; more delegation and motivation | Managers may be overloaded; fewer promotion steps | Skilled staff; small or creative firms |
| Matrix | Two reporting lines: function and project | Flexible; skills shared across projects; good teamwork | Confusion over who is the boss; conflict; higher management cost | Project-based work such as construction or consultancy |
| Product-based | A division for each product group | Focus on each market; accountability for profit; specialist knowledge | Duplicated functions; competition between divisions for resources | Businesses with many different products |
| Geographic | A unit for each region or country | Local knowledge; fast local decisions; customers served nearby | Duplication; inconsistent standards; control from the centre is harder | Businesses with customers spread across many regions |

### Showing the structures as lists

A **functional** structure for a small manufacturer:

- Managing director
  - Production manager
  - Marketing manager
  - Finance manager
  - Human resources manager

A **product-based** structure for a larger firm:

- Managing director
  - Seed division (production, sales, finance)
  - Irrigation division (production, sales, finance)
  - Feed division (production, sales, finance)

A **matrix** structure has the same functional managers as the first list, plus project managers (for example Project A, Project B), and a team member reports to both their functional manager and the project manager.

## Why and how businesses change structure

| Reason | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Growth in size | A business with more staff and branches needs more managers and clearer lines |
| New strategy or products | A move into new markets may need product or geographic divisions |
| Technology | Digital communication allows fewer managers and wider spans |
| Cost reduction | Removing layers of management (delayering) lowers costs |
| Competition or customer needs | A flatter, faster structure responds more quickly |
| Poor performance | Conflict or slow decisions show that the old structure no longer works |

**Ways of changing:** **delayering** (removing levels, giving wider spans), **decentralising** (passing decisions to lower levels), **creating divisions or project teams**, **restructuring by function, product or region** and **mergers and acquisitions** which require integrating two structures.

**Effects of changes.** Communication usually becomes quicker in a flatter structure but managers carry more work. Cost falls when layers are removed but staff may lose promotion routes and morale. Motivation may rise with more responsibility, or fall if people fear for their jobs. Change needs to be planned and communicated carefully (see the lesson on force field analysis and managing change).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: choosing a structure]
**Context.** Kwekwe Agri-Supplies sells three product groups in three cities. The directors consider functional, product-based and geographic structures.

| Option | Advantages for Kwekwe | Disadvantages for Kwekwe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Functional | Cheap and simple; specialists in sales and stock | Sales manager must know all three product groups; slow decisions between cities |
| Product-based | Each division knows its products and is responsible for its profit | Three sets of managers and delivery vehicles raise cost |
| Geographic | Each city manager understands local customers and decides prices locally | Product expertise is spread thinly across three regions |

1. The main problem described is a lack of product knowledge and slow decisions in Mutare.
2. The geographic structure solves the Mutare problem, but spreads product skills across cities.
3. The product-based structure solves the product knowledge problem but not local decisions.
4. Judgement: a product-based structure with regional sales managers within each division would address both problems, although the cost of duplicate staff is higher. If cash is limited, the directors might begin with the geographic structure for Mutare and keep a single product specialist for each product at head office.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, a matrix structure in a construction firm]
**Context.** A fictional construction company in Gaborone runs three building projects at once. Each employee belongs to a department (design, engineering or finance) and is assigned to a project team. The project manager controls the schedule, and the department head controls the employee's professional standards and pay.

**Point.** The firm uses a matrix structure.

**Application.** An engineer reports to the head of engineering for professional standards and to the project manager for day-to-day work. Staff move from project to project as skills are needed.

**Analysis.** Staff can be used where they are most needed, so skills are shared and ideas move between projects. But an engineer may receive conflicting instructions if the project manager wants speed and the department head wants thorough checks. A manager's authority is therefore shared and conflict is likely to occur unless roles are made clear. Management cost is higher because both department heads and project managers are needed.

**Judgement.** A matrix structure suits a firm with several projects needing a mix of skills, provided that roles and priorities are clearly defined and communicated. For a firm with a single project, a functional structure would be simpler.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing a structure without saying when it suits"}
Listing advantages is not enough. Say which kind of business the structure suits (size, products, regions) and link it to the case.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing matrix with product-based"}
In a matrix structure staff have two bosses. In a product-based structure each division is a separate unit and staff report to one divisional manager.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating delayering as only a cost saving"}
Delayering also speeds communication and changes spans, but can overload managers and reduce promotion routes. Weigh both sides.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (state and outline types; short cases) and Paper 2 (essays: discuss a change of structure); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Paper 3 (short case questions) and Paper 4 (essays: evaluate a structure or a change in structure).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/identify*: name the type of structure. *Explain/describe*: say how it works for this business. *Analyse/examine*: link the structure to communication, cost or motivation. *Evaluate/discuss/justify*: weigh the structure against the size, products and locations of the business and give a reasoned judgement or recommendation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A functional structure groups staff by task; it suits small firms with few products.
- Tall hierarchies have many levels and narrow spans; flat hierarchies have few levels and wide spans.
- A matrix structure has two reporting lines (function and project) and suits project work but can cause conflict.
- Product-based and geographic structures divide the business by product or region; they bring focus and local knowledge but duplicate costs.
- Structure changes because of growth, new strategy, technology, cost or competition; delayering and decentralisation are common methods.
- Always say which structure suits the business in the case and why.
:::
