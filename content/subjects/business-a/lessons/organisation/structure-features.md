## Introduction

Nyasha started a fictional furniture workshop in Mutare with two carpenters and a part-time helper. Orders came from friends and she managed everything herself. Five years later the workshop has 35 employees, three showrooms and an export order to Johannesburg. Orders are lost, two supervisors give conflicting instructions, and nobody is sure who may approve a discount. The business has outgrown its informal arrangements and needs an **organisational structure**: a clear picture of who does what, who reports to whom and who may decide what.

This lesson applies to ZIMSEC Business Studies (Form 6), Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **A Level** (Paper 3 and Paper 4). You should know the functions of management and the main leadership styles.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Organisational structure"}
The formal arrangement of jobs, authority and reporting relationships in a business, showing how work is divided and coordinated.
:::

:::definition{term="Organisational chart"}
A diagram which shows the levels of hierarchy, the jobs and the lines of authority and communication in a business.
:::

:::definition{term="Chain of command"}
The line of authority along which orders pass downwards and reports pass upwards, from the top of the business to the lowest level.
:::

:::definition{term="Span of control"}
The number of subordinates who report directly to one manager.
:::

:::definition{term="Levels of hierarchy"}
The number of layers of authority in a business, from the most senior to the lowest level.
:::

:::definition{term="Authority and responsibility"}
Authority is the right to give orders and make decisions; responsibility is the duty to carry out tasks and to answer for the results.
:::

## Why a business needs a structure

A structure that fits the business helps it to:

- **meet its needs and objectives**: for example, a structure organised by product helps a firm that sells different products to different customers;
- **avoid confusion and duplication**: everyone knows their role, who to report to and who may decide;
- **allow growth**: more people can be added without losing coordination;
- **provide flexibility**: the ability to respond to change, for example by forming project teams;
- **encourage intrapreneurship**: employees given freedom and authority to develop new ideas inside the firm;
- **make accountability clear**: problems are traced to a person or department.

A poor structure causes slow decisions, conflict between departments, duplication of work and frustration among staff. The best structure depends on the size, the products and the objectives of the business, and should be reviewed as these change.

## Reading an organisational chart

Organisational charts are drawn as boxes joined by lines. In text they can be shown as an indented list. This one is for Nyasha's workshop.

- Managing director (Nyasha)
  - Production manager
    - Supervisor, cutting (4 carpenters)
    - Supervisor, assembly (6 workers)
    - Supervisor, finishing (4 workers)
  - Sales manager
    - Showroom managers, 3 (each with 3 sales staff)
  - Finance manager
    - Accountant (1 clerk)

![Organisational chart of Nyasha's workshop: the managing director has a span of control of 3 (production, sales and finance managers); the production manager also has a span of 3 supervisors; a cutting carpenter's chain of command is carpenter (level 4), supervisor, production manager, managing director; the workers sit one level below the supervisors.](media:diagrams/org-chart-workshop.png "Figure: the organisational chart of Nyasha's workshop, with spans of control and levels of hierarchy")

From such a chart we read: **who reports to whom** (assembly workers report to the assembly supervisor); the **chain of command** (assembly worker, supervisor, production manager, managing director); the **levels of hierarchy** (the managing director is at the top, then managers, then supervisors or showroom managers, then workers); the **span of control** (the production manager supervises three supervisors, so a span of 3); and the **lines of communication**, which normally follow the chain of command.

## Features of a formal structure

| Feature | Meaning | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Levels of hierarchy | The number of layers from top to bottom | Many levels (tall) slow communication; few levels (flat) speed it |
| Chain of command | The route for orders and reports | Gives clarity, but long chains slow decisions |
| Span of control | Number of subordinates under a manager | Narrow spans give close supervision but need more managers; wide spans save cost but reduce control |
| Authority | Right to decide and instruct | Must match responsibility, or the manager cannot do the job |
| Responsibility | Duty to complete tasks and answer for results | Cannot be passed to someone else in full: a manager remains accountable |
| Unity of command | Each employee has one direct boss | Avoids conflicting orders |

**Span of control and levels of hierarchy are linked.** In a business with a fixed number of staff, a wide span means fewer managers and fewer levels (a flatter structure); a narrow span means more managers and more levels (a taller structure).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: reading span of control and levels from a chart]
**Context.** Use Nyasha's chart above.

| Question | Working | Answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Span of control of the managing director | 3 managers report directly (production, sales, finance) | 3 |
| Span of control of the production manager | 3 supervisors | 3 |
| Span of control of the assembly supervisor | 6 workers | 6 |
| Levels of hierarchy in the production branch | MD, production manager, supervisor, worker | 4 levels |
| Chain of command for a cutting carpenter | Carpenter, supervisor (cutting), production manager, MD | 4 positions |

1. The assembly supervisor has the widest span (6), so controls most closely the largest team but has the most direct staff to manage.
2. The sales branch has the same four levels: MD, sales manager, showroom manager, sales staff.
3. A carpenter who needs a decision about a discount must go through three managers, which shows how a long chain of command can slow a decision.
4. Judgement: the structure is clear, but Nyasha should consider whether a showroom manager needs more authority to agree small discounts, to avoid delay.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, a structure that has not kept pace]
**Context.** In the opening case, the workshop's two supervisors give conflicting instructions and nobody knows who may approve a discount.

**Point.** The structure has not kept up with growth.

**Application.** Conflicting instructions suggest that employees have more than one boss (a breach of unity of command). Nobody knows who may approve a discount, so authority is not clearly defined.

**Analysis.** Conflicting instructions waste time and cause mistakes, which delays orders. Unclear authority leads to slow decisions as staff pass questions up to Nyasha. She remains overloaded and cannot plan for the export order.

**Judgement.** Nyasha should draw an organisational chart showing one supervisor for each team, define the authority of each manager (for example discounts up to a limit) and communicate it to all. She should review the chart each year; too many rules may reduce flexibility and the freedom of staff to suggest new ideas.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing span of control with levels of hierarchy"}
Span of control is the number of people reporting to one manager. Levels of hierarchy are the layers from top to bottom. A wide span usually means fewer levels.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that responsibility can be fully delegated"}
A manager can delegate tasks and authority, but remains accountable for the results. The lesson on delegation covers this in detail.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading the chart upside down"}
Authority flows down the chain of command, and reports flow up. Count levels from the top of the chart.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (read or draw a chart; define terms) and Paper 2 (essays on structures); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Paper 3 (calculations and short case questions) and Paper 4 (essays on organisational structure).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/identify*: read a value from an organisation chart. *Explain/describe*: say what the feature means for the business. *Analyse/examine*: trace the effect of a feature on communication, cost or motivation. *Evaluate/discuss/justify*: weigh a structure against the needs and size of the business and give a reasoned judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A structure shows how jobs, authority and reporting relationships are arranged; it should fit the business's needs and objectives.
- An organisational chart shows levels of hierarchy, the chain of command, spans of control and lines of communication.
- Span of control is the number of subordinates reporting to one manager; wide spans lead to flatter structures with fewer levels.
- Authority must match responsibility, and each employee should have one direct boss (unity of command).
- A good structure supports growth, flexibility and intrapreneurship; a poor one causes confusion, conflict and delay.
- Review the structure as the business changes in size, products and objectives.
:::
