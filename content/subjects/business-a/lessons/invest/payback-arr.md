## Introduction

Tatenda Moyo runs a fictional poultry farm near Chinhoyi. A supplier offers him a new automatic feeding and egg-collection system for US\$60,000. The system will not pay for itself immediately, and the money could be used for something else, so Tatenda needs a way to judge whether the project is **worth the outlay** before he commits. Large businesses face the same question about machines, shops and new products. **Investment appraisal** is the set of techniques used to answer it.

This lesson covers the need for appraisal and the two basic methods: the **payback period** and the **accounting rate of return (ARR)**. It is tested in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 6) and in Cambridge **A Level** Paper 3 and Paper 4. The next lesson adds net present value. You should know how to read a cash flow forecast and how profit differs from cash.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |
| Capital budgeting; investment appraisal; payback period; accounting rate of return (ARR) | Investment appraisal; payback period; accounting rate of return (ARR) |

Both boards use the same methods. In **ARR the formula must be stated in the question** because the syllabi do not fix how the average investment is found. This bank uses: ARR = average annual profit ÷ initial investment × 100 ("initial basis"), and says so whenever an answer depends on it. A second version divides by half the outlay ("average basis"). Always use the formula in the question.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Investment appraisal"}
The process of evaluating the likely costs and benefits of a long-term project, using quantitative techniques, to decide whether to go ahead or to choose between projects.
:::

:::definition{term="Payback period"}
The length of time it takes for the net cash inflows from a project to repay the initial outlay.
:::

:::definition{term="Accounting rate of return (ARR)"}
The average annual profit from a project expressed as a percentage of the investment in it.
:::

:::definition{term="Net cash flow"}
The cash coming in from a project less the cash going out in the same period.
:::

## The need for investment appraisal

Investments are usually large, last for years and are hard to reverse. Appraisal is needed because:

- **the sums are big**, so mistakes are costly and the business may not have enough finance for every project;
- **capital is scarce**, so projects must be ranked and the best chosen;
- **benefits arrive in the future**, and forecasts can be tested for different assumptions;
- lenders and investors, such as banks, usually ask for the figures before they provide finance.

Appraisal does not remove risk. All forecasts of future cash flow are **estimates**.

## The payback period

:::keyfact{title="Payback"}
- If the net cash inflows are equal each year: payback = initial outlay ÷ annual net cash inflow.
- If they are unequal: add the cash flows year by year until the total reaches the outlay. In the final year, payback = years completed + (amount still to recover ÷ that year's cash flow).
- Convert the decimal part of a year to months by multiplying by 12.
:::

Cash is assumed to arrive evenly through each year. Payback is stated in **years and months**, for example 2 years 8 months (2.69 years).

**Advantages:** it is simple and quick; it focuses on **liquidity**, because early cash can be used elsewhere; it suits risky projects and fast-changing markets (the sooner the money is back, the less time for things to go wrong).

**Disadvantages:** it ignores cash flows **after** the payback date, so it ignores the profitability of the whole project; it ignores the **timing** within the payback period and the time value of money; it gives no decision rule unless the business sets a target.

## Accounting rate of return (ARR)

:::keyfact{title="ARR (initial basis)"}
Average annual profit = (total net cash inflows − initial outlay) ÷ number of years.

ARR (%) = average annual profit ÷ initial investment × 100.

If a question defines the denominator as the *average investment* (half the outlay), divide by half the outlay instead. The answer then doubles.
:::

A project is acceptable if its ARR exceeds the **target rate** (or the cost of borrowing, or the return on other uses of the money). **Advantages:** it uses all the project's cash flows over its whole life; it gives a percentage that can be compared with other returns and targets; it is simple to understand. **Disadvantages:** it ignores the **timing** of the flows (a dollar in year 5 counts as much as a dollar in year 1); the result depends on the basis used; it is based on forecasts, which become less reliable the further ahead they look.

## Choosing between projects

Rank the projects on each measure and compare with the targets. Where the methods disagree, the choice depends on what the business needs most: **cash back quickly** (payback) or **the highest return overall** (ARR). Quantitative results must then be combined with qualitative factors such as risk, the business's finance and the strategic fit, which the next lesson develops.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: payback and ARR for a poultry project]
**Context.** Tatenda's feeding system costs US\$72,000 and is expected to give the net cash inflows below.

| Year | Net cash flow (US\$) | Cumulative cash flow (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Year 0 | (72,000) | (72,000) |
| Year 1 | 21,000 | (51,000) |
| Year 2 | 27,000 | (24,000) |
| Year 3 | 29,000 | 5,000 |
| Year 4 | 25,000 | 30,000 |
| Year 5 | 22,000 | 52,000 |

1. After year 2 the cumulative cash flow is −US\$24,000, so US\$24,000 is still to be recovered.
2. In year 3 the inflow is US\$29,000, so the fraction of year 3 needed = 24,000 ÷ 29,000 = 0.83.
3. **Payback = 2 + 0.83 = 2.83 years**, which is 2 years 10 months (0.83 × 12 = 9.9 months).
4. Total net cash inflows = 21,000 + 27,000 + 29,000 + 25,000 + 22,000 = US\$124,000. Total profit = 124,000 − 72,000 = US\$52,000. Average annual profit = 52,000 ÷ 5 = US\$10,400.
5. **ARR (initial basis)** = 10,400 ÷ 72,000 × 100 = **14.4%**. On the average-investment basis the answer is 10,400 ÷ 36,000 × 100 = 28.9%.
6. **Judgement.** If Tatenda's bank charges 14% on loans and he wants payback within three years, the project passes both tests. The cash flows in years 4 and 5 are only forecasts, however, and a drop in egg prices would reduce the profit.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, choosing between two projects]
**Context.** A fictional bakery in Mutare can buy only one of two ovens. Both cost US\$100,000.

|  | Project A | Project B |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Initial outlay (US\$) | 100,000 | 100,000 |
| Year 1 | 34,000 | 14,000 |
| Year 2 | 34,000 | 24,000 |
| Year 3 | 34,000 | 44,000 |
| Year 4 | 34,000 | 64,000 |
| Total net cash inflows (US\$) | 136,000 | 146,000 |
| Average annual profit (US\$) | 9,000 | 11,500 |
| Payback period | 2 years 11 months | 3 years 3 months |
| ARR (%), on initial investment | 9.0 | 11.5 |

**Point.** The two measures rank the projects differently.

**Application.** Project A returns the cash faster: payback is 2 years 11 months against 3 years 3 months for B. Project B earns more in total (US\$146,000 against US\$136,000) and has the higher ARR of 11.5% against 9.0%.

**Analysis.** A produces steady cash of US\$34,000 from year 1, which the bakery can reinvest or use to repay a loan. B's cash flows are small at first and large later, so its higher return depends on the demand in years 3 and 4 being as forecast, and the money is tied up for longer.

**Judgement.** If the bakery has limited cash or fears that demand may weaken, Project A is the safer choice. If it has other finance and is confident about growth, Project B gives a higher return. Neither measure considers the time value of money, so the bakery should also use net present value, and consider the risk of the later forecasts.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the formula basis for ARR"}
ARR can be calculated on the initial or the average investment. Use the formula in the question, and state it in your answer if the question leaves it open.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using total cash inflows instead of average profit"}
ARR uses the **average annual profit**: total inflows less the outlay, divided by the number of years. Dividing total inflows by the outlay overstates the return.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading payback as a whole number of years"}
If the outlay is recovered part-way through a year, include the fraction. Payback in the example is 2.69 years, not 3 years.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *calculate*, *state* and *discuss* payback and ARR; Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 ask you to *calculate*, *analyse* and *evaluate* or *advise* on a project. Show a cumulative cash flow line for payback, write the ARR formula and give the answer in years and months or as a percentage. For *evaluate*, compare the projects on both measures, name what each ignores (cash after payback, timing, risk) and make a recommendation that depends on the business's situation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Investment appraisal helps businesses to decide whether large, long-term and hard-to-reverse projects are worthwhile.
- Payback is the time to recover the outlay; use cumulative cash flows and convert fractions of a year to months.
- ARR = average annual profit ÷ initial investment × 100, or on half the outlay if the question says average investment.
- Payback favours liquidity but ignores later cash; ARR uses all the cash flows but ignores timing.
- Different methods can rank projects differently; the decision depends on the business's need for cash, its risk and its finance.
- Forecast cash flows are estimates, and qualitative factors must also be considered.
:::
