## Introduction

A fictional tourism company in Victoria Falls needs 70 hours of labour a week for most of the year but 130 hours a week for the three months of peak season. It could employ enough full-time staff to cover the peak, and pay them all year, or it could use staff on flexible contracts who work more in the peak and less when it is quiet. The first approach is safe for workers but costly for the firm. The second is cheaper but less secure for workers. Behind the choice is a view about how people should be managed: as a cost to be controlled, or as an asset to be developed.

This lesson is for Cambridge A Level only (Papers 3 and 4). ZIMSEC does not set it as a separate topic, though ZIMSEC candidates will meet part-time and temporary work in HRM. You should already know the functions of HRM, recruitment, training and motivation from the earlier lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Hard HRM"}
An approach that treats employees as a resource to be used as efficiently as possible, like any other input, and manages them mainly through tight control, targets and flexible numbers, to minimise labour cost.
:::

:::definition{term="Soft HRM"}
An approach that treats employees as valuable assets whose commitment must be won, and manages them through communication, training, development, participation and reward, to raise motivation and loyalty.
:::

:::definition{term="Flexible working contract"}
An agreement in which the hours, place or length of employment can vary, so that the business can match labour to changing demand.
:::

## Hard and soft HRM compared

| | Hard HRM | Soft HRM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| View of employees | A cost and a resource | An asset and a source of ideas |
| Main tools | Targets, close monitoring, flexible contracts, performance pay | Training, communication, participation, development and reward |
| Aim | Lower labour cost and high output | Commitment, motivation and loyalty |
| Likely result | Efficient in the short term; may cause insecurity, high turnover and low morale | Higher morale and retention; more costly and slower to produce results |
| Suits | Low-skilled, routine work and tight margins | Skilled work where creativity and service matter |

Most businesses use a mix. A hard approach is easier to defend when work is routine and labour is easy to replace; a soft approach is more effective when skills are scarce and customer service is the main source of advantage.

## Types of flexible contract

| Type | What it is | Main advantage to the employer | Main disadvantage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Full-time | Standard weekly hours, permanent | Commitment and skill | Fixed cost even when demand is low |
| Part-time | Fewer hours than full-time | Matches busy periods; wider recruitment pool | Less commitment; more people to manage |
| Temporary / fixed-term | Employment for a set period | Covers peaks or projects at low long-term cost | Less loyalty; training is lost |
| Zero-hours | No guaranteed hours; paid for hours worked | Maximum flexibility | Insecure for workers; high turnover |
| Annualised hours | A total number of hours over the year, scheduled by demand | Fewer overtime payments; matches busy seasons | Hard to plan; long hours in peaks can cause stress |
| Flexi-time | Workers choose start and finish times within limits | Higher morale; less absence | Needs organising; cover may be thin |
| Compressed hours | Full hours worked in fewer, longer days | Satisfies workers; lower travel costs | Tiredness; cover on the days off |
| Job sharing | Two workers share one full-time job | Skills of two people; cover for absence | Communication; needs two sets of benefits |
| Home working | Work done from home | Lower office costs; wider recruitment | Less supervision and teamwork |
| Shift working | Work spread over time slots, such as day, evening, night | Longer opening hours; machines used fully | Disrupts workers' lives; costs of premiums |
| Gig economy | Self-employed workers paid per task, often through a digital platform | Very low fixed cost and instant supply | No employment rights or loyalty; reputation risk |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: choosing a contract for a seasonal business]
The tourism company needs 70 hours a week for 40 weeks and 130 hours a week for 12 weeks. A full-time week is 40 hours and the year contains 52 weeks, so a full-time employee works 2,080 hours. Standard pay is US\$6 an hour; zero-hours workers receive a 20% premium (US\$7.20 an hour) but are paid only for hours worked.

| Option | Hours paid | Rate (US\$ per hour) | Annual labour cost (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A: four full-time staff (2,080 hours each) | 8,320 | 6.00 | 49,920 |
| B: two staff on annualised hours | 4,360 | 6.00 | 26,160 |
| C: zero-hours workers at a 20% premium | 4,360 | 7.20 | 31,392 |

1. Hours needed = (40 × 70) + (12 × 130) = 2,800 + 1,560 = 4,360 a year.
2. To cover the peak of 130 hours with full-time staff the firm needs 130 ÷ 40 = 3.25, so four people: 4 × 2,080 = 8,320 hours paid, at a cost of 8,320 × 6 = US\$49,920.
3. Annualised hours pay only for the 4,360 hours needed: 4,360 × 6 = US\$26,160, which saves 49,920 − 26,160 = US\$23,760.
4. Zero-hours work pays a premium: 4,360 × 7.20 = US\$31,392, which still saves US\$18,528 on option A, but costs US\$5,232 more than option B.
5. Judgement: annualised hours give the lowest cost and some security; zero hours give more flexibility but higher cost, higher turnover and a weaker commitment. A hard approach would favour C; a soft approach B.
:::

## Information technology and artificial intelligence in HRM

| Area | Use | Benefit | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recruitment | Online applications; software that screens CVs; chatbots that answer candidates | Faster, cheaper, wider reach | Bias built into the software; qualified candidates screened out; loss of personal contact |
| Monitoring | Software that records output, time online or location | Easier performance management; fraud detection | Loss of privacy and trust; stress; legal limits on data use |
| Training | E-learning, simulations, recorded courses | Flexible, lower cost per trainee, consistent content | Less personal; needs devices and connectivity |
| Workforce planning | Systems that forecast demand and match staff to rosters | More accurate, less manual work | Forecasts depend on data quality; staff may feel treated as numbers |

Technology can give a business a cost advantage, but employees' trust and the rules on privacy must be protected. A soft HRM approach would use it to support people; a hard approach may use it mainly to control them.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis of AI in recruitment]
**Context.** A fictional call centre in Harare receives 2,000 applications a year. It considers using software to screen CVs and rank candidates before managers see them.

**Point.** The software would cut costs and time, but it may also introduce bias.

**Application.** Managers now spend about 300 hours a year on screening. The software could cut this to 40 hours.

**Analysis.** The saving of about 260 hours is a clear benefit. However, the software learns from past hiring; if the company has favoured candidates from certain schools or areas, the software will repeat that, and good candidates with unusual CVs may be rejected. Candidates may also feel the process is impersonal.

**Judgement.** The call centre should use the software only for a first sort, check a sample of rejected applications by hand, and review the results each year for bias. This captures most of the time saving while limiting the risk.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating hard and soft HRM as right and wrong"}
Neither is always better. Say which suits the business in the case, considering the type of work, skills, margins and customers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing contracts without judging them"}
"Zero hours is flexible" is not enough. State who gains and who loses, and what the cost is to the firm in turnover and commitment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating AI as only a benefit or only a risk"}
Weigh the saving against the risks of bias, privacy and loss of trust, and say what safeguards would reduce the risks.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
Cambridge Paper 3 (calculation of the cost of different contracts; short analysis) and Paper 4 (the 20-mark essay: assess hard or soft HRM, flexible contracts or the use of AI). A Level only, so not in Papers 1 and 2.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define/identify*: give a precise meaning or name a contract type. *Explain/analyse*: link a contract or technology to cost, flexibility, motivation or turnover in the case. *Assess/evaluate/advise*: weigh the benefits against the costs and risks, link to the type of business and recommend with a condition.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Hard HRM treats employees as a resource to be used efficiently; soft HRM treats them as assets whose commitment is developed.
- Flexible contracts (part-time, temporary, zero-hours, annualised hours, flexi-time, compressed hours, job sharing, home and shift working, gig work) match labour to demand.
- Flexibility lowers cost and raises responsiveness but can reduce commitment and security.
- IT and AI help with recruitment, monitoring, training and planning, but bring risks of bias, privacy loss and mistrust.
- The best approach depends on the work, skills, margins and customers; most firms use a mix.
- Support any judgement with the numbers and a condition.
:::
