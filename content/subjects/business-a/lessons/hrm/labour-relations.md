## Introduction

The workers at a fictional furniture factory in Bulawayo have asked for a 10% pay rise. The managers say the factory can afford 4%. The union representative says that if there is no agreement, workers will stop overtime. A stoppage would delay a large order for a hotel. Both sides want the factory to survive, and both can lose if the dispute grows. How management and workers deal with a disagreement, and how they prevent one in the first place, is the subject of this lesson.

Labour relations are tested in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 6) and in Cambridge AS Level Papers 1 and 2. Questions ask for the benefits of cooperation, the role of unions, and how disputes are settled. You should know how workers are motivated and how a contract and a disciplinary procedure work (earlier lessons).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Trade union"}
An organisation of workers formed to protect and improve their pay, conditions and job security by representing them to employers.
:::

:::definition{term="Collective bargaining"}
Negotiation between employers (or their association) and representatives of the workers, usually a union, over pay, hours and conditions, which leads to an agreement covering a group of workers.
:::

:::definition{term="Conciliation"}
A process in which a neutral third party helps management and workers to talk and to reach their own agreement; the third party has no power to impose a solution.
:::

:::definition{term="Arbitration"}
A process in which an independent person or body hears both sides and gives a decision that both sides have agreed in advance to accept.
:::

## Cooperation between management and workers

Good relations benefit both sides.

- **For the business:** fewer strikes and disputes; higher productivity and quality; lower absence and labour turnover; flexibility in accepting change; a good reputation that attracts employees and customers.
- **For employees:** fair pay and conditions; job security; a say in decisions; less stress.

Ways to promote cooperation: regular, two-way communication; consultation before decisions that affect staff; workers' representatives on joint committees or works councils; suggestion schemes; fair, consistent discipline; recognition and profit sharing; and honest information about the business's finances.

## Trade unions and collective bargaining

A union gains strength from numbers: an individual worker has little power in a pay discussion, but a union that represents most workers can act together. Its roles include negotiating pay and conditions, representing members in disciplinary matters, giving legal and social support, and lobbying on behalf of workers.

| | Effects on workers | Effects on employers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Positive | Higher pay and better conditions; protection against unfair treatment; a voice | One channel for negotiation, which is quicker than dealing with each worker; a representative workforce; agreed procedures for dispute |
| Negative | Pay rises may cost jobs if the firm cannot afford them; the cost of union subscriptions; strikes cost workers pay | Higher wage costs; less freedom to change working practices; risk of strikes or other industrial action |

**Industrial action** can include working to rule, banning overtime, go-slow and strikes. It is costly for both sides and is normally used when negotiation fails.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the cost of different settlements]
The furniture factory has 50 workers who are paid an average of US\$280 a month. Annual wage bill = 50 × 280 × 12 = US\$168,000. The union claims 10%; management offers 4%.

| Settlement | Annual increase (US\$) | New annual wage bill (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Union claim: 10% | 16,800 | 184,800 |
| Management offer: 4% | 6,720 | 174,720 |
| Compromise: 7% | 11,760 | 179,760 |

1. 10% of 168,000 = 16,800; 4% = 6,720; 7% = 11,760.
2. The gap between the claim and the offer is 16,800 − 6,720 = US\$10,080 a year.
3. A compromise of 7% is US\$5,040 less than the claim and US\$5,040 more than the offer, exactly halfway.
4. The best settlement depends on what the factory can afford, the risk of lost output from industrial action, and whether workers accept it. If a stoppage would lose a hotel order worth more than US\$10,080 in profit, management has a reason to move towards the union claim.
:::

## Settling disputes

1. **Negotiation** between the two sides directly.
2. **Conciliation**: a neutral conciliator helps them to talk; no decision is imposed.
3. **Mediation** (sometimes used with conciliation): the neutral party suggests terms, but the sides can refuse them.
4. **Arbitration**: an arbitrator gives a decision that both have agreed to accept (binding). This is final, but neither side controls the outcome.
5. **Industrial action or lock-out** if the law permits and other steps have failed.

Labour legislation sets the rights of workers and employers, the way unions can be recognised, rules on strikes and the procedures for disputes. Refer to it in general terms in your answers.

## Workers' representation

Workers can take part in decisions through **works councils** or workers' committees, union representatives, joint consultative committees, suggestion schemes and, in some businesses, **worker directors**. Participation gives managers better information and gives workers a voice; it also slows decisions and may raise conflict if managers do not act on the advice.

## Negotiation (BES detail)

A good negotiation passes through stages. Each can be learned and rehearsed.

1. **Preparation:** know your aims, the other side's likely aims, the facts and figures, and what you will accept at the least.
2. **Establishing the environment:** create a calm, respectful setting and agree the rules and the agenda.
3. **Exploring needs:** each side explains its interests and listens, to find what really matters.
4. **Testing understanding:** repeat back what you have heard to check that you understand.
5. **Negotiating a position:** make offers and counter-offers, trading what is less important for what matters more.
6. **Reaching agreement:** record the deal clearly and confirm what each side must do.

Negotiation is needed because the interests of managers and workers overlap but differ; agreement is better than conflict if both can gain.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis of a pay dispute]
**Context.** The factory's workers want a 10% rise; management offers 4%. Workers threaten to stop overtime. The factory has a large hotel order due in six weeks.

**Point.** Both sides have a reason to settle, and a settlement between 4% and 10% is likely.

**Application.** The union has the stronger position because of the order deadline. Management has the stronger position if the factory's profit is thin and cannot cover a high rise.

**Analysis.** A ban on overtime would delay the order and risk the hotel contract, costing more than the difference between the offers. Management should share its financial information to show what it can afford, so that workers understand the limits. A conciliator could help if direct talks fail.

**Judgement.** Management should move towards about 7% and offer something non-financial, such as a bonus linked to meeting the order, which costs nothing unless output is delivered. The settlement should be reviewed next year. This protects the contract and avoids a precedent of giving in to every threat.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing conciliation with arbitration"}
A conciliator helps the sides agree and cannot impose a decision. An arbitrator gives a decision that the sides have agreed to accept.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting unions as only a problem for employers"}
Unions also help employers by giving a single channel for negotiation and by reducing disputes. A balanced answer gives both sides.
:::

:::mistake{title="Quoting laws by name"}
Refer to labour legislation in general terms unless the question gives the law.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (state, outline; role of a union), Paper 2 (essays on labour relations and negotiation) and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 (including the stages of negotiation); Cambridge Paper 1 (define, explain) and Paper 2 (analyse and assess collective bargaining and cooperation in a data response).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline*: name the process or the stages. *Describe/explain*: say how it works and its effects in the case. *Analyse/examine*: follow the chain from the dispute to cost, output and morale. *Discuss/evaluate/assess*: weigh both sides' interests, say which settlement is best and why, and give a justified recommendation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Cooperation between management and workers reduces disputes and raises productivity, quality and retention.
- Trade unions represent workers; collective bargaining sets pay and conditions for a group; both have benefits and costs for workers and employers.
- Disputes are settled by negotiation, conciliation, mediation or binding arbitration; industrial action is a last resort.
- Works councils, committees, suggestion schemes and worker directors give workers a voice.
- BES: the stages of a negotiation are preparation, establishing the environment, exploring needs, testing understanding, negotiating a position and reaching agreement.
- Refer to labour legislation in general terms.
:::
