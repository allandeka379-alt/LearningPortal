## Introduction

Rudo manages a fictional hotel in Victoria Falls. In January the hotel had 78 employees; by December it had 82, but twelve people had left during the year, and each had to be replaced, trained and brought up to speed. Guests noticed when the restaurant was short-staffed in the busy season. Rudo needs to know how many staff the hotel will need, where they will come from and why people leave. That is the work of **human resource management** (HRM), and its main planning tool is the **workforce plan**.

This topic is tested in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 6) and in Cambridge AS Level Papers 1 and 2, usually as a short calculation of labour turnover followed by an explanation of what the figure means. You should already know the stakeholders of a business and the difference between a job and a person specification, which the next lesson develops.

Both boards examine the role of HRM, workforce planning and labour turnover. **ZIMSEC detail:** the functions of HRM (planning, recruitment, selection, training and development, remuneration, welfare, discipline and dismissal, and advisory and guidance roles) are listed in the ZIMSEC syllabi. ## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Human resource management (HRM)"}
The part of the business responsible for planning, recruiting, developing, rewarding and looking after its workforce so that the people available meet the needs of the business.
:::

:::definition{term="Workforce plan"}
A forecast of the number and skills of employees the business will need, compared with the number and skills it will have, setting out how any gap will be closed.
:::

:::definition{term="Labour turnover"}
The rate at which employees leave a business, measured as the number who leave in a year as a percentage of the average number of employees.
:::

:::definition{term="Labour retention"}
The proportion of employees who stay with the business over a period; it is 100% minus labour turnover.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Formulae"}
Labour turnover (%) = number of employees leaving in the year ÷ average number of employees × 100.
Labour retention (%) = 100 − labour turnover.
Average number of employees = (opening number + closing number) ÷ 2, unless the question gives the average.
:::

## The role of HRM

HRM helps the business to reach its objectives by making sure it has enough people with the right skills, who are motivated and treated fairly. The ZIMSEC syllabi list these functions:

- **Planning** the workforce; **recruitment** and **selection** of new staff;
- **Training and development**;
- **Remuneration**: deciding pay and benefits;
- **Welfare, health and safety** of employees;
- **Discipline and dismissal**, and **advice and guidance** to managers and workers.

In a small business the owner does all of these; in a large business a separate HR department does them and advises line managers. HRM links to every other function: operations need skilled workers, marketing needs a trained sales force, and finance wants to control labour cost.

## Workforce planning

A workforce plan has four steps.

1. **Forecast the need** for labour from the business objectives: planned sales, new products, new locations, new technology.
2. **Assess the supply**: the staff already employed, their skills, and how many are expected to leave, retire or move.
3. **Compare** need with supply to find the gap, in numbers and in skills.
4. **Close the gap** by recruiting, training, promoting from within, using overtime or part-time staff, or, where there is a surplus, by reducing numbers through natural wastage, early retirement or redundancy.

A good plan reduces the risks of being short of staff (lost sales, overworked staff, delays) and of having too many (idle workers and wasted wages). Its weakness is that forecasts of sales and labour supply may be wrong, so the plan must be updated.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: labour turnover and retention]
Rudo's hotel had 78 employees on 1 January and 82 on 31 December. Twelve employees left during the year.

1. Average number of employees = (78 + 82) ÷ 2 = 80.
2. Labour turnover = 12 ÷ 80 × 100 = 15%.
3. Labour retention = 100 − 15 = 85%.
4. Interpretation: 15% means that about one employee in seven left during the year. Whether that is high depends on the industry (hotels and restaurants usually have higher turnover than offices), on the hotel's earlier figures and on competitors' figures, so it must be compared before conclusions are drawn.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a workforce plan for a bakery]
A fictional bakery in Gweru has 20 staff. A forecast of next year's output shows that it will need 24. Past labour turnover has been 15%, and the owner assumes the same.

| Item | Number of staff |
| --- | ---: |
| Staff needed next year (forecast of output) | 24 |
| Staff now | 20 |
| Expected leavers (15% labour turnover) | 3 |
| Expected supply at end of year | 17 |
| Gap to be filled by recruitment | 7 |

1. Expected leavers = 20 × 0.15 = 3.
2. Expected supply = 20 − 3 = 17.
3. Gap = 24 − 17 = 7 people, not 4 (24 − 20), because three leavers must also be replaced.
4. Closing the gap: recruit some new bakers, train two existing sales staff for the production line, and offer overtime during the busiest months. The owner should review the plan if sales differ from the forecast.
:::

## High and low labour turnover

| | High turnover | Low turnover |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Costs | Recruitment, selection and training costs; lost output while new staff learn | Lower recruitment and training costs |
| Quality | Loss of experience and skills; inconsistent service | Experienced, skilled workforce; consistent quality |
| Other effects | Lower morale among those who remain; may signal low pay or poor management | May bring fresh ideas less often; staff may become complacent or resist change; older staff may be costly |

Some turnover is healthy: it brings new ideas and lets the business change the skill mix. Possible causes of high turnover include low pay, poor management, lack of promotion, an unsafe workplace, or a booming local labour market where rivals are hiring.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis of high turnover]
**Context.** A fictional call centre in Harare has labour turnover of 40%. Staff say supervisors give little feedback and that pay is below that of two nearby rivals.

**Point.** High turnover is raising costs and lowering quality.

**Application.** With 40% of staff leaving each year, the centre constantly recruits and trains, and customers deal with inexperienced agents.

**Analysis.** The causes are in the centre's control: low pay and weak supervision. Fixing pay would raise costs, but the savings on recruitment and training, and better customer satisfaction, might outweigh them. Morale among remaining staff is likely to be low because they have to cover for leavers.

**Judgement.** The centre should survey leavers to find the main causes before acting, then improve supervision first (cheap) and review pay if the survey shows it is the main cause.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using closing employees instead of the average"}
Labour turnover divides by the average number of employees. If the question gives only the opening and closing numbers, find their average first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring leavers in the workforce plan"}
The gap between need and supply must allow for staff who will leave. Comparing the forecast need with today's number understates the recruitment needed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that all turnover is bad"}
Some turnover is useful. Judge the figure against the industry, the business's own history and its causes before deciding whether it is a problem.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (state the functions of HRM; calculate labour turnover) and Paper 2 (essays on HRM and workforce planning); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Paper 1 (define, calculate, explain) and Paper 2 (data response with turnover figures and a final judgement).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline*: list the functions of HRM or the steps of a plan. *Calculate*: write the formula, use the average, show the percentage. *Explain/analyse*: link the figure to costs, quality or morale in the case. *Discuss/evaluate/assess*: compare the turnover with the industry or past figures, consider causes, and give a justified judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- HRM makes sure the business has enough people with the right skills, who are motivated and fairly treated.
- A workforce plan compares the forecast need for labour with the expected supply and sets out how to close the gap.
- Labour turnover (%) = leavers ÷ average employees × 100; retention = 100 − turnover.
- The recruitment gap must allow for expected leavers.
- High turnover raises recruitment and training costs and lowers experience and morale; low turnover can bring complacency.
- Judge turnover against the industry, past figures and the causes.
:::
