## Introduction

At a fictional canning factory near Mutare the production line runs at full speed, and Tendai, the HR manager, notices that absence has doubled in two months, two operators have had hand injuries, and the best supervisors are looking at other jobs. Tendai suspects that the cause is not pay but the working environment: long unplanned shifts, worn guards on machines, and a sense that managers do not listen. Employee morale, welfare and safety directly affect output, cost and reputation, which is why they are an HRM responsibility.

This lesson is tested in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 6) and in Cambridge AS Level Papers 1 and 2. Questions ask how HRM raises morale, why diversity and work-life balance matter, and how a business should respond to a health and safety problem. You should already know how motivation, labour turnover and contracts affect staff (the earlier lessons).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Employee morale"}
The overall attitude, confidence and satisfaction of workers towards their job and their employer; it shows in effort, attendance and willingness to cooperate.
:::

:::definition{term="Employee welfare"}
The provision of facilities and services that look after workers' physical and social wellbeing at work, such as rest rooms, canteens, clean water, first aid and counselling.
:::

:::definition{term="Work-life balance"}
The balance an employee achieves between the time and energy given to work and those given to family, rest and other interests.
:::

:::definition{term="Diversity and equality"}
Diversity means having a workforce of people with different backgrounds, ages, genders, abilities and beliefs; equality means treating every person fairly and giving equal opportunity, whatever those differences.
:::

## Morale and welfare

**Signs of low morale:** high labour turnover, absence, lateness, complaints and grievances, low output and poor quality. **Causes:** poor pay or conditions, job insecurity, weak management, no recognition, unfair treatment, heavy workloads.

**How HRM improves morale and welfare:** fair pay, recognition and promotion; two-way communication and listening to complaints; welfare facilities; fair and consistent discipline; safe conditions; support for stress and ill health; and reasonable hours. Higher morale usually raises productivity and reduces turnover and absence, though it costs money, and results are not immediate.

**Absence rate** is a simple indicator: absence rate (%) = working days lost to absence ÷ working days available × 100.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the cost of absence]
The canning factory has 60 workers who are each expected to work 25 days in a month, so 1,500 working days are available. In the month, 120 days were lost to absence. The average daily wage is US\$12.

1. Absence rate = 120 ÷ 1,500 × 100 = 8.0%.
2. Wages paid for days of absence (if workers are paid sick leave) = 120 × 12 = US\$1,440.
3. If absence falls to 4%, days lost = 0.04 × 1,500 = 60, saving 60 × 12 = US\$720 a month in wages for unproductive days, before counting the lost output.
4. The saving suggests that measures to improve welfare and safety, such as repairing the machine guards, can pay for themselves, provided absence really falls.
:::

## Work-life balance

Long, unpredictable or rotating hours cause stress and tiredness, which lead to errors and sickness. Employers can help by agreeing fixed hours, flexible start times, shift swaps, home working where possible, and reasonable notice of rosters. The benefits are loyalty, lower absence and the ability to attract and keep staff; the costs are the difficulty of covering flexible hours and possible loss of supervision. Work-life balance matters most to staff with young children or long journeys to work.

## Diversity and equality

Diverse teams bring a wider range of ideas and skills, understand a varied customer base, and widen the pool from which the business can recruit. Equal treatment reduces grievances and legal claims and improves morale. Difficulties include communication problems, prejudice or conflict that must be managed, and the cost of adapting premises or training. Businesses promote equality through fair recruitment and promotion, equal pay for equal work, training on respect, and a clear complaints procedure.

## Health and safety at work

**The law (in outline).** Employers have a legal duty to provide a safe workplace, safe equipment and procedures, information and training, protective clothing where needed, first aid, and to record and report accidents. Workers must follow safety rules and report hazards. Employers who break the law may face fines, compensation claims and closure.

**The role of HRM:**

- **Training and education** in safe working, fire drills and first aid, including induction;
- **Protective equipment and facilities:** gloves, helmets, ear protection, guards on machines, clean water, washrooms, first aid kits;
- **Policies and records:** written rules, accident books, regular inspections, safety committees;
- **Dealing with problems:** investigate accidents, correct hazards, retrain workers and discipline those who ignore the rules.

**Costs and benefits.** Safety costs money (equipment, training, time). But accidents cost more through injury, absence, lost output, higher insurance, compensation, damaged morale and reputation.

**Investigating a local organisation (ZIMSEC detail).** Visit the workplace, list the hazards observed (for example uncovered wires, blocked fire exits, poor lighting), interview workers and managers about accidents, review accident records, compare the findings with the law and with good practice, and recommend actions in order of risk and cost.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis of a safety problem]
**Context.** At the canning factory two operators injured their hands on a machine whose guard had worn away. Production managers say that stopping the line to repair it would cost a day of output.

**Point.** The factory should repair the guard immediately, even at the cost of lost output.

**Application.** The injuries show a real hazard, and further accidents are likely because the machine is used every day.

**Analysis.** A day of lost output is a known, limited cost. A further accident could mean medical bills and compensation, absence, a legal investigation and a fall in morale, as workers see that managers value output above safety. The two injuries have already made the workers uneasy, which may explain the rise in absence.

**Judgement.** The repair should be done at once, with the HR manager investigating how the guard was allowed to wear away, retraining operators and introducing a weekly inspection. The short-term cost is small compared with the cost and damage of a serious accident.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating safety as a cost only"}
Safety measures cost money, but accidents cost more. A strong answer weighs both sides.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing morale with motivation theory"}
Morale is the overall attitude of the workforce and shows in attendance and output. Describe the signs and causes in the case rather than listing theorists.
:::

:::mistake{title="Quoting laws by name"}
Refer to health and safety law, or labour legislation, generally unless the question names the law.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (state duties of employers; describe health and safety measures) and Paper 2 (essays on welfare and safety; investigation of a local organisation); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Paper 1 (explain how work-life balance or diversity affects a business) and Paper 2 (analyse and assess in a data response).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline*: name measures or duties. *Describe/explain*: say what each does and apply it to the business. *Analyse/examine*: follow the chain from the measure to morale, cost and output. *Discuss/evaluate/assess*: weigh cost against benefit, say which measure matters most in the case and justify a recommendation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Morale is the attitude of the workforce; low morale shows in absence, turnover, complaints and poor quality.
- Welfare facilities, fair treatment, communication and good conditions raise morale and productivity.
- Work-life balance reduces stress and absence but flexible hours can be hard to manage.
- Diversity widens ideas and the labour pool; equality reduces grievances; both need active management.
- Employers must provide safe equipment, training, protective clothing and first aid; HRM plans, trains, inspects and investigates.
- Safety costs money but accidents cost more.
:::
