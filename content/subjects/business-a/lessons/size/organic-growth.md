## Introduction

Nyasha's Fresh Bakery in Chinhoyi began with one oven and one shop. Over five years she added a second oven, opened shops in two nearby towns, launched a range of cakes and bought a delivery van, all paid for from her profits and a bank loan. She never bought or merged with another bakery. This steady, internally financed expansion is **organic growth**, and it is the commonest route for small firms that become large ones.

Growth questions are tested in ZIMSEC Business Enterprise Skills Form 6 (Paper 1 structured parts, Paper 2 essays on survival and growth strategies and managing a growing enterprise) and in Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS Level: reasons for growth, internal growth methods, advantages and limits). You should know how size is measured, the forms of ownership and what economies of scale mean (lower average cost as output rises). External growth (mergers and takeovers) is a separate lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Organic (internal) growth"}
The expansion of a business by its own efforts, using its own resources: selling more, launching new products, entering new markets or opening new outlets, rather than by buying or merging with another firm.
:::

:::definition{term="Economies of scale"}
Falling average cost per unit as the scale of output increases, for example from bulk buying or spreading fixed costs over more units.
:::

:::definition{term="Overtrading"}
Expanding sales faster than the business can finance, so that stock, debtors and costs rise and cash runs out even though the business may be profitable.
:::

## Why businesses grow

- **Economies of scale**: lower average costs and higher profit margins.
- **Greater market share**: more customers and more influence over prices.
- **Security**: a larger firm is less likely to be forced out by competitors or hurt by one customer leaving.
- **Profit**: more sales can mean more total profit.
- **Owners' ambition** and the wish to provide jobs or a legacy.

## Ways of growing organically

| Method | Example |
| --- | --- |
| New products | A bakery adds cakes and biscuits |
| New markets | Selling in a nearby town or by delivery |
| More outlets | Opening a second shop |
| Investment in capacity | Buying a larger oven or a second machine |
| Franchising | Allowing others to open outlets under the brand without the firm financing each one |

## Advantages and limits of organic growth

| Advantages | Limits |
| --- | --- |
| Less risky than a takeover: growth is gradual and can be controlled | Slow: it may take years |
| Financed from profits, so less debt | Limited by the profits and finance available |
| Keeps the culture and control of the owner | Market may be saturated, or rivals may respond |
| Business learns as it grows | Cannot gain a rival's assets or market share instantly |

## ZIMSEC (BES) detail: survival and growth strategies, and managing growth

**Survival strategies** in a hard period include cutting costs, widening the product range, finding new customers, controlling credit and building loyalty. **Growth strategies** include market penetration (selling more of the present product), new products, new markets and franchising.

**Managing a growing enterprise** needs:
- **Delegation**: the owner cannot do everything, so authority is given to managers.
- **Controls**: budgets, stock records and cash checks.
- **Finance**: a plan for funding growth from profits, loans or new owners, with cash forecasts.
- **Staffing**: recruiting and training skilled staff.

## Problems of rapid growth

Growth can strain a business: cash is needed before sales are received; quality and service may fall; owners may lose touch with customers and staff; diseconomies of scale (rising average costs from poor communication and coordination) may appear. **Overtrading** is a particular danger when a firm grows its sales faster than its finance.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: economies of scale from growth]
A fictional soap maker in Gweru produces at different scales as it grows.

| Output (units) | Total cost (US\$) | Average cost per unit (US\$) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1,000 | 8,000 | 8.00 |
| 2,500 | 17,500 | 7.00 |
| 4,000 | 26,000 | 6.50 |

1. Average cost = total cost ÷ output: 8,000 ÷ 1,000 = 8.00; 17,500 ÷ 2,500 = 7.00; 26,000 ÷ 4,000 = 6.50.
2. From 1,000 to 2,500 units, average cost falls by (8.00 − 7.00) ÷ 8.00 × 100 = 12.5%. From 1,000 to 4,000 units it falls by (8.00 − 6.50) ÷ 8.00 × 100 = 18.75%.
3. The fall shows economies of scale, from bulk buying and spreading fixed costs, but the fall slows (US\$1.00 saved between the first two stages, US\$0.50 between the last two).
4. If the soap sells for US\$9.00, profit per unit rises from US\$1.00 to US\$2.50, so growth raises profitability as long as demand exists for the extra output.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: overtrading]
A fictional garment business sells US\$150,000 a year (US\$12,500 a month) and plans to raise sales by 60% to US\$240,000 (US\$20,000 a month). Customers pay after 1.5 months, so receivables equal 1.5 months of sales. Extra stock of US\$9,000 will be needed. The business has US\$15,000 of cash.

1. Growth in sales = (240,000 − 150,000) ÷ 150,000 × 100 = 60%.
2. Receivables before = 1.5 × 12,500 = US\$18,750; after = 1.5 × 20,000 = US\$30,000. Extra receivables = US\$11,250.
3. Extra working capital needed = 11,250 + 9,000 = US\$20,250.
4. Cash available = US\$15,000, so there is a shortfall of 20,250 − 15,000 = US\$5,250 before any profit from the extra sales is received.
5. The business is profitable but cannot finance its growth: this is overtrading. Remedies are to arrange an overdraft or loan in advance, grow more slowly, or collect from customers faster.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, how fast to grow?]
**Context.** Rudo, a fictional owner of a juice business in Mutare, has a chance to supply a supermarket chain, which would double her sales. She has no spare machine capacity and no manager besides herself.

**Point.** The order offers growth but threatens overtrading and loss of quality.

**Application.** She would need to buy a second machine, hire two workers and buy more fruit before the chain pays 60 days after delivery.

**Analysis.** Without a cash forecast, the extra costs may run ahead of cash. If she tries to supervise everything alone, quality may fall and the chain may drop her. Delegating production to a trained supervisor would let her concentrate on finance and customers.

**Judgement.** She should accept the order only with finance arranged in advance and a supervisor in place; if finance is not available, she should accept a smaller first order, since losing the supermarket account through poor service would damage her more than growing slowly.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up organic and external growth"}
Organic growth comes from the firm's own expansion. Buying or merging with another firm is external growth.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming growth always lowers costs"}
Economies of scale can be followed by diseconomies. Growth helps only if the business is well managed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating overtrading as the same as making a loss"}
Overtrading is a shortage of cash caused by growing too fast. The firm may be profitable on paper.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 (survival and growth strategies; managing a growing enterprise); Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 at AS Level (reasons for growth, methods of internal growth, advantages and limits). It is not a Business Studies topic in this form.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline*: name a reason or method. *Explain/describe*: add the effect in the case (for example "a second shop gives access to a new market"). *Analyse*: follow a chain such as growth → bigger orders → lower unit costs → higher margin. *Evaluate/assess/discuss*: weigh speed and risk against benefits and judge, stating the condition (for example enough finance and management).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Organic growth comes from a firm's own efforts: new products, new markets, more outlets, more capacity or franchising.
- Reasons for growth include economies of scale, market share, security and profit.
- Organic growth is slower but less risky and easier to control than external growth.
- Managing growth needs delegation, controls, finance planning and staffing (BES).
- Overtrading is a cash shortage caused by growing faster than finance allows.
:::
