## Introduction

Walk through a high-density suburb in Harare and you will find hairdressers working from containers, welders under shade, tailors with a single sewing machine and women selling vegetables from stalls. Most of the businesses in Zimbabwe, as in most countries, are small. A hardware store with five staff cannot buy cement as cheaply as the national chain, yet it knows its customers by name and can deliver on a Saturday afternoon.

Small business questions are common: in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Paper 1 structured parts, Paper 2 essays on the role of SMEs and the support given to them) and in Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (advantages and disadvantages of being small, the importance of small firms, family businesses). You should know the methods of measuring size from the previous lesson and the forms of ownership from the structure lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Small business"}
A business that is small compared with others in its industry, judged by measures such as number of employees, revenue or capital employed, and often owned and managed by a single person or family.
:::

:::definition{term="Small and medium-sized enterprise (SME)"}
A business below set limits of size (for example in employees or turnover) that governments treat as a group when designing support and rules. The limits differ between countries and over time, so exam questions state them.
:::

:::definition{term="Family business"}
A business owned and run mainly by members of one family, with decisions often shaped by family relationships as well as business aims.
:::

## Advantages and disadvantages of being small

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| **Flexibility**: quick decisions and quick changes of product | **Limited finance**: reliance on savings and small loans |
| **Personal service**: close contact with customers | **Weak buying power**: no bulk discounts, so higher unit costs |
| **Owner control** and motivation | **Dependence on the owner**: ill health or absence can stop the business |
| Low overheads and simple organisation | Few resources to survive a bad period or fight price competition |
| Serving niche or local markets that big firms ignore | Limited skills and few chances to specialise |

## Family businesses

**Strengths:** trust and commitment among relatives; flexible hours; a long-term outlook, such as building the business for the next generation; lower labour costs when relatives accept low pay.

**Weaknesses:** family disputes can spill into the business; promotions may go to relatives rather than the most capable; difficulty in separating family and business money; problems of succession when the owner retires.

## Importance of small firms

- **Employment**: small firms employ a large share of workers, including many in the informal sector.
- **Innovation**: new ideas often come from small firms or start-ups.
- **Competition**: they keep prices down and quality up by challenging larger firms.
- **Serving local needs**: they supply goods and services that suit local demand.
- **Supply to large firms**: they supply parts and services to bigger firms.
- **Starting point of growth**: many large firms began as small ones.

## ZIMSEC detail: SMEs in Zimbabwe

SMEs matter in Zimbabwe because they create jobs, provide incomes to households, use local materials and skills and supply goods in places large firms do not serve. They face problems:

| Problem | Example | Possible solution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Finance | Banks demand security that small owners lack | Microfinance, savings groups, loans designed for SMEs |
| Markets | Few customers, competition from imports | Joining associations, selling to larger firms, better marketing |
| Skills | Weak record keeping and management | Training, mentoring, short courses |
| Infrastructure | Unreliable power, poor roads, lack of workspace | Shared workspaces, alternative energy, planned business sites |
| Regulation | Complicated registration and licences | Simplified registration, advice centres |

**State assistance:** the state may assist through training, advice, easier finance, workspace and simplified registration; it may also constrain through taxes, licensing, health and safety rules and planning rules. Compliance costs fall more heavily on small firms. Describe the types of help and constraint rather than quoting any agency or programme.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the cost of weak buying power]
A small hardware shop buys 150 bags of cement a month at US\$12.00 a bag. A national chain buys 3,000 bags and gets a 15% discount. Both sell at US\$15.00 a bag.

1. Chain's price per bag = 12.00 × (1 − 0.15) = US\$10.20.
2. Small shop's profit per bag = 15.00 − 12.00 = US\$3.00, a margin of 3.00 ÷ 15.00 × 100 = 20%.
3. Chain's profit per bag = 15.00 − 10.20 = US\$4.80, a margin of 4.80 ÷ 15.00 × 100 = 32%.
4. The chain earns US\$1.80 more per bag, so it can cut prices below the small shop's cost price (US\$12.00) and still make a profit; the small shop cannot. The shop's answer is to compete on service, delivery and credit to regular customers rather than on price.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: the value of cheaper finance]
A fictional tailor in Gweru needs a loan of US\$5,000. A commercial bank would charge 14% interest a year. A support scheme for SMEs offers the same loan at 8% a year. (The rates are assumptions for this example.)

1. Interest at 14% = 5,000 × 0.14 = US\$700 a year.
2. Interest at 8% = 5,000 × 0.08 = US\$400 a year.
3. Saving = 700 − 400 = US\$300 a year, which is 300 ÷ 700 × 100 = 42.9% of the commercial interest.
4. For a business earning a profit of perhaps US\$2,000 a year, US\$300 is a significant part. This shows why cheaper finance for SMEs can improve survival, though it is useful only if the tailor has demand to repay the loan.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, should a small shop try to grow?]
**Context.** Rudo's Grocery in Masvingo is a family business with four staff. A large supermarket has opened a street away, and Rudo's sales have fallen. She can compete on price by buying in bulk with other shops, or focus on personal service and credit for regular customers.

**Point.** As a small firm, Rudo cannot win on price alone, but she has strengths in service.

**Application.** Buying alone, she lacks the volume for discounts. Joining other small shops to buy in bulk could cut costs. Her staff know the customers' needs and she can give credit, which the supermarket does not.

**Analysis.** Competing on price against a supermarket with greater buying power would shrink her margin. Focusing on credit and delivery may keep loyal customers but risks bad debts if she lends too much.

**Judgement.** A mix is best: join a buying group to reduce costs while concentrating on service and carefully controlled credit. The strategy works only if enough customers value service over the lowest price.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying small businesses are always weaker"}
Small firms have real strengths (flexibility, personal service, niche markets). Discuss both sides.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing problems without solutions"}
Questions on SMEs often ask for solutions. Link each problem to a realistic remedy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming specific agencies or programmes"}
Names and programmes change. Describe the type of help (training, finance, workspace) instead.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 (importance, problems, solutions and state assistance); Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (advantages and disadvantages, family businesses, importance of small firms).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline*: name the problem or benefit. *Describe/explain*: say what it is and why it matters in the case. *Discuss/evaluate/assess*: weigh strengths against problems, or compare solutions, and conclude with a judgement stating the condition on which success depends.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Small firms are flexible and personal but have limited finance, weak buying power and depend on the owner.
- Family businesses have trust and commitment but can suffer from disputes and succession problems.
- Small firms and SMEs provide jobs, innovation, competition and local service.
- SME problems in Zimbabwe include finance, markets, skills, infrastructure and regulation, each with possible solutions.
- The state may both assist (training, finance, workspace) and constrain (taxes, licences, rules).
:::
