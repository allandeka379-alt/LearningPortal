## Introduction

Sunrise Bakery and Crust & Co (both fictional) are two of the larger bakeries in Harare. Each has grown by opening new outlets from its own profits, which is slow. When the owners of Crust & Co grow tired of competing on price, Sunrise proposes to join the two firms into one. Joining with an existing business is called **external growth**, and it is a faster, riskier route than **internal (organic) growth**, which was covered earlier in this unit.

The topic is examined in Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS Level), and in ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 and 2 and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2. You should already know what makes a business small or large and why a firm wants to grow. Here you learn the types of integration, who gains and loses, and why many mergers disappoint their owners.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Merger"}
Two firms agree to join together and form one new business, usually by exchanging shares. The owners of both firms normally agree to it.
:::

:::definition{term="Takeover (acquisition)"}
One firm buys enough of the shares of another firm to gain control of it. If the board of the target agrees, the takeover is **friendly**; if the target resists and the bid goes straight to the shareholders, it is **hostile**.
:::

:::definition{term="Horizontal, vertical and conglomerate integration"}
**Horizontal**: joining a firm at the same stage of production in the same industry (bakery with bakery). **Vertical**: joining a firm at a different stage of the same supply chain: **backward** (towards suppliers, such as a bakery buying a flour mill) or **forward** (towards customers, such as the bakery buying shops). **Conglomerate**: joining firms in unrelated industries.
:::

:::definition{term="Joint venture and strategic alliance"}
A **joint venture** is a separate project or business set up and owned by two or more firms, which share its costs, risks and profits. A **strategic alliance** is an agreement to cooperate on a specific aim, such as sharing distribution, without forming a new company or merging.
:::

## Types of integration

| Type | Joins | Example | Main aim |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Horizontal | Same industry, same stage | Sunrise Bakery buys Crust & Co | Bigger market share, fewer rivals, economies of scale |
| Backward vertical | A supplier | A bakery buys a flour mill | Secure and cheaper supplies, control of quality |
| Forward vertical | A distributor or retailer | A bakery buys a chain of tuck shops | Secure outlets, keep the retailer's profit |
| Conglomerate | An unrelated industry | A bakery buys a bus company | Spread risk across different markets |

## Why firms integrate

- **Growth and market share**: a horizontal merger removes a competitor at once.
- **Economies of scale**: bulk buying, shared overheads and one set of managers for two firms lower the cost per unit.
- **Spreading risk**: a conglomerate is less hurt when one market weakens.
- **Control of supply or sales**: vertical integration reduces the risk of a supplier raising prices or a retailer dropping the product.
- **Buying skills, brands or technology** that would take years to build.

## Effects on stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Possible gains | Possible losses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shareholders | Higher share price, bigger profits | Overpaying; control diluted |
| Workers | More promotion posts in a bigger firm | Redundancies where jobs duplicate; changed conditions |
| Customers | Lower prices from economies of scale; wider range | Less choice and higher prices if competition falls |
| Suppliers | A larger, steadier customer | Pressure to cut prices; loss of the contract if the merged firm has its own supplier |
| Competitors | None directly | A stronger rival; loss of market share |

## Why integration may fail

Many mergers do not deliver the benefits promised. The usual causes are:

- **Clash of cultures**: different management styles and ways of working cause friction and loss of staff.
- **Diseconomies of scale**: a larger organisation is harder to manage and communicate within, so unit costs may rise.
- **Overpaying**: a premium over the share price must be earned back.
- **Integration problems**: two computer systems, brands and pay structures must be combined, which takes time and money.
- **Loss of key people**, and distraction of managers from the core business.

## Joint ventures and strategic alliances

These give some benefits of growth without full merger. Two firms may form a joint venture to bid for a large contract that neither could win alone, sharing the cost and risk. The drawbacks are disagreement over control, sharing profit and the risk that a partner gains the other's know-how. An alliance is looser and easier to leave, but offers less control.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: market share and cost saving in a horizontal merger]
Sunrise Bakery and Crust & Co sell bread in a Harare market worth US\$2,000,000 a year. Each firm has its own administration and distribution overheads.

| | Sales (US\$ a year) | Share of the Harare bread market (%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Sunrise Bakery | 480,000 | 24 |
| Crust & Co | 360,000 | 18 |
| Combined firm | 840,000 | 42 |
| All other bakeries | 1,160,000 | 58 |
| Whole market | 2,000,000 | 100 |

| Administration and distribution overheads | US\$ a year |
| --- | ---: |
| Sunrise Bakery | 90,000 |
| Crust & Co | 70,000 |
| Total before the merger | 160,000 |
| Combined firm after the merger | 125,000 |
| Annual saving | 35,000 |

**Working.** Market share = firm's sales ÷ market sales × 100. The shares are 480,000 ÷ 2,000,000 × 100 = 24% and 360,000 ÷ 2,000,000 × 100 = 18%, so the merged firm holds 24% + 18% = 42%. The overhead saving is 160,000 − 125,000 = US\$35,000 a year, which is 35,000 ÷ 160,000 × 100 = 21.9% of the old overheads (to one decimal place).

**Interpretation.** A 42% share makes the merged firm the market leader and may give it pricing power, but it also invites attention from the competition authorities, and the saving is only realised if staff and depots are successfully combined.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, a hostile takeover]
**Context.** Tatenda Mills (a fictional maize miller in Gweru) makes a bid for Midlands Feeds, a fictional animal-feed producer, offering a price 30% above the current share price. The Midlands board rejects the offer and tells shareholders to refuse it, but many shareholders are tempted.

**Point.** The bid is a hostile takeover and is also an example of vertical integration if Tatenda Mills supplies the maize that Midlands uses.

**Application.** Tatenda Mills would secure a customer for its grain, and shareholders in Midlands would receive a premium. The board resists because it fears the loss of its jobs and independence.

**Analysis.** If the bid succeeds, Tatenda Mills must integrate a firm whose managers opposed it. Resentful managers may leave, and the premium paid means that profits must rise substantially for shareholders of the buyer to gain. Workers in duplicated departments such as accounts may be made redundant, which can lower morale and productivity in the short run.

**Judgement.** The takeover is worthwhile only if the savings and the guaranteed sales exceed the premium and the integration costs. Tatenda Mills should assess the culture of Midlands before bidding, and a negotiated (friendly) deal would reduce the risk of losing key staff.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing horizontal and vertical integration"}
Horizontal means the same stage of production in the same industry. A bakery buying a flour mill is vertical (backward) integration, because the mill is a supplier.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing every merger as a success"}
Many mergers fail to meet their objectives. For a judgement, name the conditions (similar cultures, a fair price, a clear integration plan) under which the merger would work.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing stakeholders without saying how they are affected"}
Do not just list shareholders, workers and customers. State the effect on each and whether it is a gain or a loss for that group.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS Level): definitions, types of integration in a case, effects on stakeholders, and an assessment of whether a merger will meet its objectives. ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2: structured questions on the types and reasons, and essays that ask you to discuss the advantages and disadvantages of external growth.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/identify*: name the type of integration from the case. *Explain/outline*: give a reason and apply it to the firms named. *Analyse*: build a chain such as "bigger orders, lower cost per unit, lower prices, more customers". *Assess/evaluate/discuss*: weigh gains against clash of cultures and overpaying and give a judgement that depends on a stated condition.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- External growth joins firms by merger, takeover, joint venture or alliance; it is faster than organic growth but riskier.
- Horizontal joins the same stage of the same industry; vertical joins another stage of the supply chain (backward or forward); conglomerate joins unrelated industries.
- A takeover can be friendly or hostile, depending on whether the target's board agrees.
- Shareholders, workers, customers, suppliers and competitors are each affected in different ways.
- Mergers fail through clashes of culture, diseconomies of scale, overpaying and poor integration.
- Joint ventures and alliances share cost and risk but reduce control.
:::
