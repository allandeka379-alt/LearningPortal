## Introduction

Zuva Breads has 120 workers and 21 shops. Chiedza Foods has 14 workers and five shops but sells more per worker because its factory is highly automated. Which is the bigger business? A bank deciding who may need a large loan, a government deciding who qualifies for support for small firms and a competition authority worried about one firm dominating the market might each give a different answer, because each uses a different measure of size.

You will be asked to name measures, to calculate market share and to explain why one measure may mislead. The topic is examined in ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 and 2 (Form 5), Business Enterprise Skills (Form 6) and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS Level). You should know how revenue and profit differ (see the first lesson) and have met the idea of capital employed as the money invested in the business.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Revenue (turnover, sales revenue)"}
The money received from sales in a period: selling price × quantity sold.
:::

:::definition{term="Capital employed"}
The total long-term finance invested in a business: the money put in by owners and by long-term lenders, used to buy its assets.
:::

:::definition{term="Market share"}
The proportion of the total sales of a market that is made by one business: firm's sales ÷ total market sales × 100.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge 9609 |
| --- | --- |
| Turnover; sales | Revenue |
| Number of employees | Number of employees |
| Capital employed | Capital employed |
| Market share | Market share |

Profit is not a measure of size in ZIMSEC; Cambridge lists it among measures but treats it with caution (see below).
:::

## Methods of measuring size

| Measure | How it is found | Strength | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue | Value of sales in a year | Easy to find; shows scale of trading | Includes goods bought in; high-price products inflate it; varies with prices |
| Number of employees | Count the staff | Simple; useful for the labour market and for rules based on staff numbers | Misleading for capital-intensive firms with few staff; part-time and casual workers complicate counting |
| Capital employed | Owners' capital plus long-term loans | Shows the investment in the business | Valuation of assets varies; hard to compare firms with different financing |
| Profit | Revenue minus costs | Shows performance | Depends on efficiency, not size; a large firm can make a loss |
| Market share | Sales ÷ total market sales × 100 | Shows position against rivals | Depends on how the market is defined; hard to compare across markets |
| Number of outlets | Count the shops, branches or sites | Easy to see | Outlets differ greatly in size |

Because each measure can mislead, use **more than one** and choose the one that suits the purpose: a lender may focus on capital employed, a union on employees, an advertiser on market share.

## Calculating market share

Market share (%) = firm's sales ÷ total sales in the market × 100.

Market share can change even when sales rise. If the whole market grows faster than the firm's sales, the firm's share falls. Market definition matters: a bakery has a large share of "bread sold in a small town", but a tiny share of "all food sold nationally".

:::worked-solution[Example 1: comparing three firms by different measures]
Three bakeries sell in a market worth US\$2,400,000 a year.

| Firm | Annual sales (US\$) | Employees | Outlets | Market share |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Mapfura Bakery | 600,000 | 38 | 9 | 25.0% |
| Zuva Breads | 840,000 | 120 | 21 | 35.0% |
| Chiedza Foods | 276,000 | 14 | 5 | 11.5% |

1. Market share = sales ÷ 2,400,000 × 100: Mapfura 600,000 ÷ 2,400,000 = 25.0%; Zuva 840,000 ÷ 2,400,000 = 35.0%; Chiedza 276,000 ÷ 2,400,000 = 11.5%.
2. By revenue, employees, outlets and market share, Zuva is largest and Chiedza smallest.
3. Sales per employee: Mapfura 600,000 ÷ 38 = US\$15,789; Zuva 840,000 ÷ 120 = US\$7,000; Chiedza 276,000 ÷ 14 = US\$19,714. Chiedza gets the most sales per worker, so measuring its size by employees alone understates how much it sells.
4. Conclusion: Zuva is the largest on every direct measure, but number of employees alone would suggest Chiedza is very small even though it is efficient. Use more than one measure.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: market share when the market grows]
Next year the market grows to US\$2,640,000 and Mapfura's sales rise to US\$693,000.

1. Growth of the market = (2,640,000 − 2,400,000) ÷ 2,400,000 × 100 = 10.0%.
2. Growth of Mapfura's sales = (693,000 − 600,000) ÷ 600,000 × 100 = 15.5%.
3. New market share = 693,000 ÷ 2,640,000 × 100 = 26.25%, or 26.3% to one decimal place, up from 25.0%.
4. Because Mapfura's sales grew faster than the market, its share rose by 1.3 percentage points. Had its sales grown only 6%, to US\$636,000, its share would have fallen to 24.1% even though sales rose.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, which measure for which user?]
**Context.** The authority that supports small firms in a fictional district wants to decide whether a quarry and a software firm should receive help. The quarry has eight workers, large machines and US\$900,000 of capital employed. The software firm has 25 workers and US\$60,000 of capital employed.

**Point.** The two measures give opposite answers.

**Application.** By employees the software firm is larger (25 against 8); by capital employed the quarry is far larger (US\$900,000 against US\$60,000).

**Analysis.** A rule based on employees treats the quarry as very small, though it has a large investment and probably high revenue. A rule based on capital employed would treat the software firm as tiny, though it provides more jobs.

**Judgement.** The authority should use more than one measure, for example employees and revenue, and choose the one matched to its purpose: if the aim is job creation, employees matter most; if the aim is to support investment, capital employed matters.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using profit as a measure of size"}
Profit shows how well a business is doing, not how big it is. A large firm can make a loss; a small one can be very profitable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calculating market share from the wrong total"}
Divide by the total sales of the whole market, not by a competitor's sales.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming rising sales always mean rising share"}
Share rises only if the firm's sales grow faster than the market's.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 (calculate market share, discuss measures) and Business Enterprise Skills; Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (calculate market share, explain the limitations of a measure, choose a measure for a case).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State*: name the measure. *Calculate*: formula, substitution, answer with %. *Explain/analyse*: say why the measure can mislead in the case (for example a capital-intensive firm). *Evaluate/assess*: decide which measure is most appropriate for the purpose and justify it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Size can be measured by revenue, employees, capital employed, profit, market share or number of outlets.
- Market share (%) = firm's sales ÷ total market sales × 100.
- Every measure has weaknesses; for example employees understate capital-intensive firms.
- Market share rises only if the firm's sales grow faster than the market.
- The best measure depends on the purpose, and more than one measure should be used.
:::
