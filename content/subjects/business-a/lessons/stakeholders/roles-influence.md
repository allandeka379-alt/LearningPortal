## Introduction

Masimba Brick Works (a fictional firm in Gweru) wants to build a bigger kiln. The owners expect higher profit. The workers hope for overtime but worry about smoke. Neighbouring families complain about dust and noise, the bank wants to be sure its loan will be repaid, and the council must approve the plans. Every one of these groups has something at stake in the decision, and the firm cannot ignore any of them.

Understanding who the stakeholders are, what they want, and how much power they have is part of nearly every case study. The topic is tested in Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS Level), ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2. You should already know the types of business objective.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Stakeholder"}
An individual or group with an interest in a business and its decisions, who is affected by it or can affect it.
:::

:::definition{term="Internal and external stakeholders"}
**Internal** stakeholders are part of the organisation: owners (shareholders), managers and employees. **External** stakeholders are outside it: customers, suppliers, lenders, government, the community and pressure groups.
:::

:::definition{term="Accountability"}
The obligation of managers and directors to explain and justify their decisions and results to those with a legitimate interest, such as owners, lenders and government.
:::

:::definition{term="Shareholder and stakeholder views"}
The **shareholder view** says the business exists mainly to earn a return for its owners. The **stakeholder view** says the business must balance the interests of all the groups affected by it.
:::

## The main stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Type | Main interests | Responsibilities |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Owners / shareholders | Internal | Profit, dividends, growth in the value of shares | Provide capital; accept risk |
| Managers | Internal | Salary, job security, power, success of the business | Run the business well; act in the owners' interests |
| Employees | Internal | Wages, job security, safe conditions, training | Work properly; follow rules |
| Customers | External | Quality, fair price, safe goods, service | Pay for goods; use them correctly |
| Suppliers | External | Regular orders, prompt payment | Deliver goods of the right quality on time |
| Lenders (banks) | External | Repayment with interest, security | Provide finance on agreed terms |
| Government | External | Tax revenue, jobs, legal compliance | Make and enforce laws |
| Community | External | Jobs, a clean environment, local spending | Welcome business that serves them |
| Pressure groups | External | A specific cause such as the environment or workers' rights | Campaign responsibly |

Rights go with these interests: employees have a right to safe conditions and to be paid as agreed; customers have a right to safe goods; shareholders have a right to information and to vote.

## How decisions affect stakeholders and how they respond

A decision such as closing a branch, raising prices or relocating produces gains for some and losses for others. Stakeholders respond in different ways. Owners may sell shares or vote out the directors; workers may strike or leave; customers may switch to rivals; suppliers may demand earlier payment; the community or a pressure group may protest, lobby the council or publicise the firm's behaviour. The aims of the stakeholders also shape decisions: if shareholders insist on high dividends, the firm has less to spend on training or equipment.

## Accountability

Directors are accountable to shareholders through annual reports and meetings, to lenders through financial information, and to government through tax returns and compliance. Managers are accountable to directors. Accountability reduces the chance of dishonesty and waste, but it also takes management time.

## Conflict between stakeholders

Conflict arises because stakeholders' aims differ: shareholders want high dividends while workers want higher pay; customers want low prices while owners want high profit; the community wants a clean environment while managers want low-cost production. Conflict can be reduced by communication, consultation, compromise and by giving stakeholders a share, for example through profit-sharing or community funds. When a firm's objectives change (from survival to growth, say), the balance of power and the points of conflict change too.

## Judging power and influence

The influence of a stakeholder depends on:

- **Power**: can it make or break the business? For example, a bank holding the main loan, or the only supplier of a vital material.
- **Interest**: how much does it care about the decision?
- **Organisation and support**: a united trade union or a well-publicised pressure group has more influence than isolated individuals.
- **Alternatives**: if customers can easily switch, they have more power over the firm.
- **Legal rights**: government and shareholders hold legal powers.

Stakeholders with high power and high interest, such as major shareholders, must be managed closely. Those with high power but low interest must be kept satisfied. Those with low power but high interest, such as local residents, should be kept informed, although they can gain power by joining a pressure group.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: dividing a profit between stakeholders]
Masimba Brick Works earned a profit of US\$48,000. Two plans for sharing it are being considered.

| Use of the US\$48,000 profit | Plan 1 (%) | Plan 1 (US\$) | Plan 2 (%) | Plan 2 (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Dividends to shareholders | 60 | 28,800 | 40 | 19,200 |
| Bonus to workers | 10 | 4,800 | 25 | 12,000 |
| Community fund | 5 | 2,400 | 10 | 4,800 |
| Retained for the business | 25 | 12,000 | 25 | 12,000 |
| Total | 100 | 48,000 | 100 | 48,000 |

**Working.** Each amount is the percentage of US\$48,000. Plan 2 pays shareholders 28,800 − 19,200 = US\$9,600 less in dividends and the workers 12,000 − 4,800 = US\$7,200 more in bonuses. With 20 workers, the average bonus rises from US\$240 to US\$600 each.

**Interpretation.** The plans transfer money from owners to workers and the community while the amount kept in the business is the same. Plan 1 suits the shareholder view; Plan 2 the stakeholder view. Which is better depends on the effect on workers' effort and on whether shareholders will accept lower dividends.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, who has most influence?]
**Context.** Dube Poultry (a fictional business in Chinhoyi) plans to move its processing to a site near a river. The bank that lent for the new equipment, the 40 workers, a group of residents downstream, and the council all have views.

**Point.** The bank and the council hold the most influence, the residents the least, unless they organise.

**Application.** The bank can withdraw lending if the plan puts repayment at risk. The council can refuse planning permission or fine the firm for pollution. The workers can reduce output or leave, though they are easily replaced. The residents have a strong interest but no formal power.

**Analysis.** If the residents form a group, contact the press and appeal to the council, their influence rises, because the firm's reputation and permission to operate are at stake. The workers' interest is in keeping their jobs and may support the move if it brings overtime, which could cause conflict with the residents.

**Judgement.** Dube Poultry should consult the council and the residents before moving, and invest in waste treatment. Ignoring the residents may save time now but risks delay, fines and damage to its reputation later.
:::

![Stakeholder map for Dube Poultry's move to a river site: the bank (lender) and the council hold high power and sit in the upper half; the 40 workers have high interest but lower power; the downstream residents have strong interest but the least power until they organise. Quadrants are keep satisfied, manage closely, monitor and keep informed.](media:diagrams/stakeholder-power-interest.png "Figure: where the four stakeholders sit on a power and interest map (a judgement, not a measurement)")

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing stakeholders without their interests"}
Name the group, state what it wants, and say how the decision helps or harms it. A list alone earns little.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming all stakeholders have equal power"}
Rank them. A bank with a large loan or the only supplier has more power than a few individual customers. Explain why.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating shareholders and stakeholders as the same"}
Shareholders are owners; stakeholders include everyone affected, from workers and customers to the community.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS Level): classification, interests, conflicts and an assessment of which group has most influence. ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2: stakeholder interests in structured questions and essays on conflict between stakeholders.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/identify*: name the stakeholder group and one interest. *Explain*: link the interest to the decision in the case. *Analyse*: trace how the reaction of the group affects the firm. *Assess/discuss/evaluate*: compare the power of two groups and judge which should have priority, giving a reason and a condition.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Internal stakeholders are owners, managers and employees; external ones include customers, suppliers, lenders, government, the community and pressure groups.
- Each group has interests, rights and responsibilities, and reacts to decisions in ways that can help or harm the firm.
- Managers are accountable to owners, lenders and government.
- Conflict arises when aims differ, for instance dividends against wages.
- Influence depends on power, interest, organisation, alternatives and legal rights; strong evaluation ranks the groups in the case.
:::
