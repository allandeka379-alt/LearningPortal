## Introduction

Chipo plans to start a poultry project outside Masvingo. Before she can sell a single egg she must buy chicks, feed, a chicken house and equipment, and pay for registration. Once the hens are laying she must buy feed every week, pay a helper and keep a little money aside for the day a customer pays late. Later, if she wants to double the size of the flock, she will need more money again. Every business, large or small, needs **finance**: money to start, to run from day to day and to grow.

This topic is in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and in Cambridge AS Level. It leads into the lessons on working capital, sources of finance and cash flow forecasts. You should already know the difference between revenue, costs and profit.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |
| Start-up capital; expansion funds; finance for working capital | Start-up capital; capital for growth; working capital; business failure: bankruptcy, liquidation, administration |

Both boards examine why finance is needed and the difference between cash and profit. The legal terms bankruptcy, liquidation and administration are Cambridge terms; ZIMSEC candidates should know that a business without enough cash may fail even if it is making a profit.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Start-up capital"}
The money needed to set up a business before it earns any revenue, for example to buy premises, equipment, stock and to pay for registration and the first expenses.
:::

:::definition{term="Cash flow"}
The movement of money into and out of a business over a period. A business has a cash flow problem when it cannot pay its bills when they are due.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit"}
The surplus of revenue over costs for a period. Profit is not the same as cash: sales made on credit count as revenue before the cash arrives.
:::

## Why businesses need finance

1. **To start up.** Premises, equipment, a first stock of materials, legal and registration costs and enough cash to cover early bills are needed before the first sale. This is the start-up capital.
2. **To keep running (working capital).** Wages, rent, electricity, and materials must be paid before customers pay. The money for this is the working capital.
3. **To grow.** Expansion needs money for new machines, more premises, new products or new markets. This is expansion capital or capital for growth.
4. **To survive.** Unexpected costs, a fall in sales or a customer who does not pay can cause a shortage of cash. Finance keeps the business trading through hard times.

**Short-term needs** (up to about a year) include stock, wages, bills and a temporary shortage of cash. **Long-term needs** (several years) include land, buildings, machinery and vehicles. A good rule is to match the length of the finance to the life of what it pays for: borrowing for a few weeks to buy a truck that lasts ten years is risky.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: start-up finance for a poultry project]
Chipo lists what she needs before the first eggs are sold.

| Start-up item | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Day-old chicks (500) | 1,800 |
| Feed for the first three months | 2,700 |
| Chicken house | 4,500 |
| Drinkers, feeders and heaters | 1,200 |
| Registration and licence | 300 |
| Cash for the first month's wages and transport | 1,500 |
| **Total start-up finance** | 12,000 |

1. Long-term items (the house and the equipment) = 4,500 + 1,200 = US\$5,700.
2. Short-term items (chicks, feed, licence, cash) = 1,800 + 2,700 + 300 + 1,500 = US\$6,300. The two groups add up to the US\$12,000 total.
3. If Chipo has saved US\$5,000, she needs US\$7,000 more from other sources. The lessons that follow show how she can choose.
4. Under-estimating the total is a common cause of early failure. A sensible owner adds a margin for unforeseen costs.
:::

## Cash and profit are different

Profit is calculated by matching revenue with the costs of earning it, in the period the sale is made. Cash flow records money as it moves. A profitable business can run out of cash if customers take a long time to pay, if it buys too much stock, or if it spends on equipment. A business making a loss can still have cash for a while, perhaps because the owner has put in money.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: profit without cash]
A fictional furniture maker in Gweru sells 400 chairs at US\$50 each in a month, on 60 days' credit. It starts the month with US\$10,000 in the bank.

| Item | Profit statement (US\$) | Cash flow (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Sales: 400 × 50 | 20,000 | 0 |
| Materials | (8,000) | (8,000) |
| Wages | (6,000) | (6,000) |
| Rent | (1,500) | (1,500) |
| **Profit / net cash flow** | **4,500** | **(15,500)** |

1. Profit = 20,000 − 8,000 − 6,000 − 1,500 = US\$4,500.
2. Cash received this month is nil because customers have not paid; cash paid out is 8,000 + 6,000 + 1,500 = US\$15,500.
3. Closing bank balance = 10,000 − 15,500 = US\$(5,500): an overdraft, although the business made a profit.
4. In two months, when the customers pay, the cash will arrive. If the bank refuses an overdraft meanwhile, the business may be unable to pay its wages and could fail while profitable.
:::

## Business failure

A business fails when it cannot pay its debts and can no longer continue. Three legal terms are used:

- **Bankruptcy.** A legal process in which a person (a sole trader or partner) who cannot pay their debts has assets used to repay creditors, and loses control of them.
- **Liquidation.** A company is closed down, its assets are sold, and the money is used to pay creditors in order of priority. The business ceases to exist.
- **Administration.** An independent person (an administrator) takes control of an insolvent company to try to rescue it or to sell it as a going concern, with legal protection from creditors for a time.

The main financial reason for failure is **lack of cash**, caused by weak cash flow control, too little start-up capital, over-expansion, bad debts or falling sales.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, can a profitable business fail?]
**Context.** The furniture maker in Example 2 plans to take a new order of 1,000 chairs on the same 60 days' credit. It has no overdraft limit and its suppliers want to be paid within 30 days.

**Point.** The business may fail because of lack of cash even though the new order is profitable.

**Application.** In Example 2 the costs were US\$15,500 for 400 chairs, or US\$38.75 a chair. For 1,000 chairs the costs would be about US\$38,750, most of it paid within 30 days, while the sales of US\$50,000 would arrive only after 60 days. The business has only US\$10,000 at the start.

**Analysis.** Costs are paid in 30 days while income arrives in 60: a gap of 30 days in which the business needs finance. Without it the business cannot pay its suppliers or wages and its creditors could force it into liquidation.

**Judgement.** The furniture maker should accept the order only if it arranges finance first: an overdraft, asking the customer for part-payment in advance, or negotiating 60 days with suppliers. The profit would then be worth having.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating profit and cash as the same"}
Profit is revenue minus costs; cash is money actually received and paid. A profitable business can run out of cash.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing needs without matching the finance"}
Say whether a need is short-term (wages, stock) or long-term (machinery, buildings), because this decides which source is suitable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the terms for failure"}
Bankruptcy is for individuals; liquidation closes a company; administration tries to rescue a company.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that growth needs cash too"}
Rapid growth can cause failure if the business must pay for stock and wages before customers pay.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (state start-up needs, a cash and profit calculation) and Paper 2 (essays on finance); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (define, explain why a business needs finance, explain the difference between cash and profit).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline/define*: name the need or term precisely. *Explain/analyse*: link the need to the business (a bakery buying an oven needs long-term finance) and show the chain to the effect. *Discuss/assess/evaluate*: consider how important cash is compared with profit for this business, and say what finance should be arranged.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Finance is needed to start up, to run the business (working capital), to grow and to survive.
- Short-term needs (stock, wages) and long-term needs (machinery, buildings) require different finance.
- Profit is not cash: sales on credit give profit without cash, and a profitable business can fail through lack of cash.
- Failure terms: bankruptcy (individuals), liquidation (company closed and assets sold), administration (rescue by an administrator).
- Weak cash flow, too little capital, over-expansion and bad debts are the main financial causes of failure.
:::
