## Introduction

Tendai grows oyster mushrooms near Mutare and sells them to supermarkets and market traders. Sales are good and rising, yet in March she must pay US\$6,000 for a new drying room and wages are due every month. Will she have the money when the bill arrives? A **cash flow forecast** answers this question before it is too late. It lists the cash the business expects to receive and pay out in each month and shows the cash balance month by month.

A cash flow forecast is tested in ZIMSEC Business Studies (Form 6), Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5, where it appears as the cash budget) and Cambridge AS Level. Calculations and interpretation of forecasts are among the most common data-response items. You should already know the difference between cash and profit and what working capital is.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |
| Cash flow forecast; cash budget (Business Enterprise Skills); cash inflows and outflows; opening and closing balance | Cash flow forecast; cash inflows (receipts) and outflows (payments); net cash flow; opening and closing cash balances |

Both boards examine the same layout and calculations. In all questions in this course, brackets or a minus sign show a negative figure.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cash flow forecast"}
A statement of the cash a business expects to receive and pay out over future periods, usually months, showing the expected net cash flow and the cash balance at the end of each period.
:::

:::definition{term="Net cash flow"}
Total cash receipts (inflows) in a period less total cash payments (outflows) in the same period. It may be positive or negative.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Formulas"}
Net cash flow = total receipts − total payments. Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow. The closing balance of one month is the opening balance of the next. A negative closing balance means an overdraft or a shortage of cash.
:::

## Purpose of a forecast

A cash flow forecast helps a business to: foresee shortages of cash and arrange an overdraft or other finance in good time; decide when to buy equipment or pay large bills; show lenders and investors that it can repay a loan (banks ask for a forecast when a new business applies for finance); plan to use surplus cash productively; and control the business by comparing actual cash with the forecast. Its limitations: it is based on estimates of sales, prices and the time customers take to pay, so it can be wrong, especially for a new business or in changing conditions.

## Receipts and payments

**Receipts** include cash sales, payments from credit customers (received in a later month than the sale), loans received, the owner's capital and the sale of assets. **Payments** include materials, wages, rent, electricity, loan repayments, tax and the purchase of equipment. Only items in which cash actually moves appear. Depreciation is not a cash payment and is never included.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: building the forecast]
Tendai's sales are US\$8,000, 9,000, 10,000, 12,000, 11,000 and 9,500 from January to June, after US\$7,000 in December. Half of sales are for cash and half are on credit, paid in the following month. Materials cost 40% of each month's sales and are paid in the same month. Other monthly payments are wages US\$3,000, rent US\$800, electricity and transport US\$350 and a loan repayment US\$500. In March she also buys a drying room for US\$6,000. The opening balance on 1 January is US\$4,000.

| US\$ | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| **Receipts** | | | | | | |
| Cash sales (50%) | 4,000 | 4,500 | 5,000 | 6,000 | 5,500 | 4,750 |
| Credit sales received (50% of last month) | 3,500 | 4,000 | 4,500 | 5,000 | 6,000 | 5,500 |
| **Total receipts** | 7,500 | 8,500 | 9,500 | 11,000 | 11,500 | 10,250 |
| **Payments** | | | | | | |
| Materials (40% of sales) | 3,200 | 3,600 | 4,000 | 4,800 | 4,400 | 3,800 |
| Wages | 3,000 | 3,000 | 3,000 | 3,000 | 3,000 | 3,000 |
| Rent | 800 | 800 | 800 | 800 | 800 | 800 |
| Electricity and transport | 350 | 350 | 350 | 350 | 350 | 350 |
| Loan repayment | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 |
| New equipment | | | 6,000 | | | |
| **Total payments** | 7,850 | 8,250 | 14,650 | 9,450 | 9,050 | 8,450 |
| **Net cash flow** | (350) | 250 | (5,150) | 1,550 | 2,450 | 1,800 |
| Opening balance | 4,000 | 3,650 | 3,900 | (1,250) | 300 | 2,750 |
| **Closing balance** | 3,650 | 3,900 | (1,250) | 300 | 2,750 | 4,550 |

1. January credit receipts are half of December sales: 50% × 7,000 = 3,500. In February they are 50% × 8,000 = 4,000, and so on.
2. March: receipts 5,000 + 4,500 = 9,500; payments 4,000 + 3,000 + 800 + 350 + 500 + 6,000 = 14,650; net cash flow = 9,500 − 14,650 = (5,150).
3. Closing balance in March = 3,900 − 5,150 = (1,250): an overdraft of US\$1,250.
4. The business is profitable and cash recovers, but for March it needs short-term finance: it should arrange an overdraft of at least US\$1,250 in advance.
:::

## Interpreting and amending a forecast

Look for: months with a negative closing balance; the size and length of the shortage; large payments and when receipts will cover them; and the effect of a change in sales or in the time customers take to pay. A forecast is amended when assumptions change: if customers pay later, receipts move to later months; if costs rise, payments rise. An amended forecast shows what will happen if the assumptions prove wrong (sometimes called what-if analysis).

:::worked-solution[Example 2: customers pay later than expected]
Tendai's biggest supermarket customer starts to pay after two months instead of one, so credit receipts are 50% of the sales of two months earlier (November sales were US\$6,500). All other figures stay the same.

| US\$ | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Total receipts (amended) | 7,250 | 8,000 | 9,000 | 10,500 | 10,500 | 10,750 |
| Total payments | 7,850 | 8,250 | 14,650 | 9,450 | 9,050 | 8,450 |
| Net cash flow | (600) | (250) | (5,650) | 1,050 | 1,450 | 2,300 |
| Closing balance (amended) | 3,400 | 3,150 | (2,500) | (1,450) | 0 | 2,300 |
| Closing balance (original forecast) | 3,650 | 3,900 | (1,250) | 300 | 2,750 | 4,550 |

1. January credit receipts are 50% of November sales: 3,250; February 50% × 7,000 = 3,500.
2. The overdraft doubles to US\$2,500 in March and lasts until May (April closing is (1,450) and May closing is nil).
3. Slower payment by one customer turns a short shortage into a three-month problem, so the forecast helps Tendai see the risk.
:::

## Causes of cash flow problems and ways of improving cash flow

**Causes:** customers who pay late or not at all; over-stocking; heavy spending on equipment or expansion (overtrading); a fall in sales; rising costs; seasonal sales with steady costs; and poor forecasting.

| Method | How it helps | Cost or limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reduce the credit period or chase customers | Receipts arrive sooner | May lose customers |
| Offer discounts for prompt payment | Cash arrives sooner | Reduces revenue |
| Extend payment terms with suppliers (trade credit) | Payments are delayed | Suppliers may withdraw credit or discounts |
| Debt factoring | Immediate cash for invoices | Fee reduces profit |
| Leasing instead of buying | Spreads the cost | Total cost is higher; no ownership |
| Sale and leaseback | Large cash sum | Rent every year |
| Bank overdraft | Covers short-term shortage | Interest; can be withdrawn |
| Cut costs or delay non-essential spending | Lower payments | May harm quality or growth |

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, leasing the drying room]
**Context.** In the amended forecast of Example 2, Tendai expects an overdraft from March to May. A supplier offers to lease the drying room for US\$450 a month from March instead of selling it for US\$6,000.

**Point.** Leasing would remove the cash shortage.

**Application.** The March payment falls by 6,000 and rises by 450, so net cash flow in March is (5,650) + 6,000 − 450 = (100). Closing balances become 3,050 in March, 3,650 in April, 4,650 in May and 6,500 in June, with no overdraft at all.

**Analysis.** Leasing avoids interest on an overdraft and keeps cash for wages. But over four months the lease costs 4 × 450 = US\$1,800, and it would continue; if she keeps the drying room for years the total could exceed US\$6,000, and she would not own it.

**Judgement.** Leasing is a sensible way to avoid a cash shortage in the short run. If the forecast is reliable and the drying room will be used for many years, buying with a loan or retained profit once cash recovers would cost less overall.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting credit sales in the month of the sale"}
Cash from credit sales is received later. Put it in the month in which customers pay, not the month of the sale.
:::

:::mistake{title="Including depreciation or profit as a payment"}
Depreciation is not a cash flow. The forecast contains only receipts and payments of cash.
:::

:::mistake{title="Not carrying the closing balance forward"}
The closing balance of one month is the opening balance of the next. A mistake in one month spreads to all later months.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a negative balance as 'no problem'"}
A negative balance means the business must have an overdraft or other finance. Say when it occurs, how big it is and how it can be covered.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (complete or correct a forecast) and Paper 2 (data response and essays); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 (cash budget); Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (calculate net cash flow and balances, explain causes and solutions, and evaluate a forecast).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate*: show net cash flow and the opening and closing balance for each month asked. *State/outline* (ZIMSEC): name a cause or a method. *Explain/analyse*: link the cause to the case, for example slow payers and a negative balance. *Evaluate/discuss/advise*: compare methods of improving cash flow for the business, weigh cost against benefit, and recommend with a reason.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A cash flow forecast lists expected receipts and payments by month, with net cash flow and opening and closing balances.
- Net cash flow = receipts − payments; closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow; one month's closing balance is the next month's opening balance.
- Forecasts help foresee shortages, support loan applications and control the business, but are only estimates.
- Causes of problems include late-paying customers, over-stocking, heavy spending and seasonal sales.
- Methods to improve cash flow include chasing debts, discounts, trade credit, factoring, leasing, sale and leaseback, an overdraft and cutting costs; each has a cost.
:::
