## Introduction

Nyasha supplies bread and rolls to six tuck shops and two hotels around Gweru. She buys flour on 30 days' credit, pays wages every Friday and gives her customers 60 days to pay. Her ovens work well and her orders are growing, yet last month she could not pay the flour supplier on time because the hotels had not paid her. The bakery was profitable on paper but short of **working capital**: the money that keeps day-to-day operations going.

Working capital is tested in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and in Cambridge AS Level. You should already know what current assets and current liabilities are and the difference between cash and profit.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |
| Working capital; debtors and creditors; stock; control of working capital in a project | Working capital; trade receivables and trade payables; inventory; capital expenditure and revenue expenditure |

Both boards examine the same ideas. ZIMSEC asks about the significance, management and control of working capital in a project; Cambridge expects the distinction between capital and revenue expenditure and its effect on profit and the statement of financial position.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Working capital"}
The finance available for the day-to-day running of a business. It is calculated as current assets minus current liabilities.
:::

:::definition{term="Capital expenditure"}
Spending on non-current (fixed) assets such as machinery, vehicles and buildings that will be used in the business for more than one year.
:::

:::definition{term="Revenue expenditure"}
Spending on the day-to-day running of the business, such as wages, materials, rent and repairs, which is charged against profit in the period in which it is incurred.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Formula"}
Working capital = current assets − current liabilities. Current assets are cash, inventory (stock) and trade receivables (debtors); current liabilities are trade payables (creditors), a bank overdraft and other debts due within a year.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 1: calculating working capital]
Nyasha's bakery has these current items at the end of a month.

| | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| **Current assets** | |
| Inventory (stock) | 8,400 |
| Trade receivables (debtors) | 6,100 |
| Cash | 1,500 |
| Total current assets | 16,000 |
| **Current liabilities** | |
| Trade payables (creditors) | 5,200 |
| Bank overdraft | 3,000 |
| Tax owing | 1,000 |
| Total current liabilities | 9,200 |
| **Working capital** | 6,800 |

1. Current assets = 8,400 + 6,100 + 1,500 = US\$16,000.
2. Current liabilities = 5,200 + 3,000 + 1,000 = US\$9,200.
3. Working capital = 16,000 − 9,200 = US\$6,800.
4. The figure is positive, but only US\$1,500 of the current assets is cash and US\$3,000 is an overdraft that must be repaid on demand. Working capital that is mostly stock and receivables is not cash: this is why a business with positive working capital can still be unable to pay its bills.
:::

## Too little and too much working capital

**Too little** working capital means the business cannot pay its bills on time. Suppliers may refuse credit or demand cash, wages may be late, the business may miss chances to buy stock cheaply or accept big orders, and in the worst case it is forced into liquidation. **Overtrading** (expanding faster than the finance allows) is a common cause.

**Too much** working capital means money is tied up in idle stock, slow-paying customers or unused cash. That money could be earning a return elsewhere, and stock may be damaged or become out of date. Cash held in a bank earns little, and over-large stocks cost storage and security.

## Managing receivables and payables

**Trade receivables** are money owed by customers. The business can control them by checking credit references, setting credit limits, invoicing promptly, chasing late payers, offering **early settlement discounts**, or selling the debts to a specialist firm (**factoring**: the factor pays most of the invoice at once and keeps a fee). **Trade payables** are money owed to suppliers. The business can negotiate longer payment terms, but paying too late damages relations and may lose discounts or credit.

The aim is to receive cash as early as possible and pay as late as sensible, while balancing the cost of each action.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: an early settlement discount]
A fictional building-materials supplier in Mutare has credit sales of US\$240,000 a year, and customers take 60 days to pay on average. It considers a 2% discount for payment within 10 days. It expects that 30% of sales (by value) will take it and pay in 10 days; the rest will continue to pay in 60 days. Its overdraft costs 12% a year (an assumption). Use a 365-day year.

| Item | Working | US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Average receivables now | 240,000 × 60 ÷ 365 | 39,452 |
| Average receivables with the discount | 72,000 × 10 ÷ 365 + 168,000 × 60 ÷ 365 | 29,589 |
| Fall in receivables | 39,452 − 29,589 | 9,863 |
| Interest saved | 9,863 × 12% | 1,184 |
| Cost of discounts | 72,000 × 2% | 1,440 |
| Net effect | 1,184 − 1,440 | (256) |

1. Sales taking the discount = 240,000 × 30% = US\$72,000 and the rest = US\$168,000.
2. Receivables fall by about US\$9,863 and release that much cash, saving 12% a year in overdraft interest.
3. The discounts given cost US\$1,440, which is more than the interest saved, so the scheme loses US\$256 a year at 12%.
4. If the overdraft cost 18%, the saving would be 9,863 × 18% = US\$1,775, a net gain of US\$335. The decision depends on the cost of borrowing and on how many customers take the discount.
:::

## Capital and revenue expenditure

Capital expenditure buys assets that last (a new oven); it appears on the statement of financial position as a non-current asset and its cost is spread over its life (depreciation) in the profit calculation. Revenue expenditure is a cost of running the business (flour, wages, repairs of the oven). It is charged in full against profit in the period. Misclassifying spending changes profit: treating a repair as capital overstates profit now; treating a new machine as revenue understates profit and the assets.

| Spending | Type | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New delivery van | Capital | Lasts several years |
| Fuel for the van | Revenue | Used up in the period |
| Replacing a broken oven door | Revenue | Maintains the asset |
| Building an extension | Capital | Adds a lasting asset |

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, a working-capital squeeze]
**Context.** Nyasha's bakery wins a contract with two hotels for 500 rolls a day. Her flour supplier wants payment in 30 days, but the hotels will pay in 60 days.

**Point.** The new contract improves sales but strains working capital.

**Application.** She must pay for flour and wages 30 days before the hotels pay. Her cash is only US\$1,500 and she already has a US\$3,000 overdraft.

**Analysis.** The gap of 30 days means she needs extra funds to carry the order. If she cannot find them she may delay paying her flour supplier, who may stop supplying and risk the whole bakery.

**Judgement.** She should accept the contract only if she can reduce the gap: ask the hotels to pay in 30 days, ask the supplier for 60 days' credit, or arrange a short-term loan. The sales are worth having, but not at the risk of running out of cash.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the whole of current assets 'working capital'"}
Working capital is current assets **minus** current liabilities.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming more working capital is always better"}
Too much is also a problem, because money is idle and could be used more profitably.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up capital and revenue expenditure"}
Capital expenditure buys a lasting asset; revenue expenditure is a running cost. A repair is revenue expenditure; a new machine is capital expenditure.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the cost of a discount or of factoring"}
A discount reduces income and factoring costs a fee. Compare the cost with the saving in interest.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (calculate working capital, state methods of control) and Paper 2 (finance essays); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (define, calculate, explain the effect on cash flow, classify expenditure).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define/state*: give the formula and the meaning. *Calculate*: show current assets, current liabilities and the difference. *Explain/analyse*: link the problem to the business, for example slow-paying customers and a cash shortage. *Discuss/assess/evaluate*: compare the cost of an action with its benefit and say what the decision depends on.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Working capital = current assets − current liabilities; it funds the day-to-day running of the business.
- Too little causes inability to pay bills; too much ties up money that could earn a return.
- Receivables can be controlled with credit checks, limits, prompt invoicing, discounts and factoring; payables by negotiating terms.
- Capital expenditure buys lasting assets; revenue expenditure is a running cost charged against profit.
- Every method of improving working capital has a cost that must be compared with its benefit.
:::
