## Introduction

Chipo has US\$20,000 saved from her tuck-shop supply business, which she will not need for six months. Her neighbour Farai runs a growing bakery and wants to borrow US\$100,000 to build a factory. A financial market is where people with money to spare and people who need money are brought together. Without such markets Chipo would keep her savings at home and Farai might never find a lender.

This topic is **ZIMSEC Business Studies only** (Form 5). Cambridge AS and A Level do not examine financial markets as a topic, although they use the sources of finance that these markets provide. You should already know the internal and external sources of finance.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Financial market"}
A market in which funds are raised and lent, and financial instruments such as shares, bonds and short-term deposits are bought and sold, linking those who have spare money with those who need it.
:::

:::definition{term="Money market"}
The market for short-term borrowing and lending, usually for up to one year, in instruments such as bank deposits and treasury bills.
:::

:::definition{term="Capital market"}
The market for long-term finance, for periods longer than a year, in which shares, bonds and long-term loans are issued and traded.
:::

## Money market and capital market

| Feature | Money market | Capital market |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Period | Short term (up to one year) | Long term (over one year) |
| Purpose | Working capital, liquidity, short-term cash management | Fixed assets, expansion, long-term projects |
| Instruments | Bank deposits, treasury bills, short-term loans, commercial paper | Shares, debentures and bonds, long-term bank loans, mortgages |
| Risk | Lower | Higher, because prices change over longer periods |
| Return | Usually lower | Usually higher but less certain |

## Functions of financial markets

1. **Providing funds.** Businesses and governments borrow or raise capital by selling shares or bonds.
2. **Trading securities.** A **primary market** is where new shares and bonds are first sold; a **secondary market** is where existing ones are traded between investors. A secondary market makes shares easier to sell, which makes investors more willing to buy them in the first place.
3. **Setting prices.** The buying and selling of securities sets their prices, which show what investors think of a company and what interest rates should be.
4. **Spreading risk and encouraging saving.** Savers can invest small amounts in many ways and borrowers have more places to look for funds.

## Institutions

- **The Zimbabwe Stock Exchange (ZSE)** is the market in which shares and other securities of listed companies are bought and sold. Listing on the exchange helps a company raise capital and gives it a public standing, but listed companies must publish information and meet the exchange's rules.
- **Commercial banks** accept deposits, lend through loans and overdrafts, and provide payment services. They are the main source of short-term finance for most businesses.
- **Other institutions** include building societies (mortgages), micro-finance institutions (small loans to small businesses), insurance companies and pension funds (large investors in shares and bonds).
- **International financial institutions.** The **African Development Bank** provides loans, grants and technical support for development projects in African countries. The **World Bank** provides loans and support for development and the reduction of poverty. The **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** works to promote the stability of the international financial system and lends to countries with difficulties in paying for their imports or debts, usually with conditions about economic policy. These institutions deal mainly with governments and large projects, but their lending can affect businesses through infrastructure, interest rates and the economy.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: an investment in shares on a stock exchange]
A fictional investor buys 5,000 shares in a listed fictional company at US\$0.40 each. Brokers charge 1.5% of the value on each purchase and sale. She holds the shares for a year, receives a dividend of US\$0.03 a share and sells at US\$0.46 (all figures are assumptions for the example).

| Item | Working | US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Cost of shares | 5,000 × 0.40 | 2,000.00 |
| Broker's fee on purchase | 2,000 × 1.5% | 30.00 |
| **Total cost** | | 2,030.00 |
| Sale value | 5,000 × 0.46 | 2,300.00 |
| Broker's fee on sale | 2,300 × 1.5% | (34.50) |
| Dividends received | 5,000 × 0.03 | 150.00 |
| **Net gain** | 2,265.50 + 150 − 2,030 | 385.50 |

1. Total cost = 2,000 + 30 = US\$2,030.
2. Proceeds after the fee = 2,300 − 34.50 = US\$2,265.50.
3. Net gain = 2,265.50 + 150 − 2,030 = US\$385.50, a return of 385.50 ÷ 2,030 × 100 = 19.0% on the cost.
4. The share price could have fallen instead: the return from shares is uncertain, unlike a deposit.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: choosing a market for a surplus]
Chipo has US\$20,000 she will not need for six months. A six-month bank deposit pays 4% a year (an assumption), while shares in the capital market might earn more but their price can fall.

| Option | Working | Return (US\$) |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Six-month deposit (money market) | 20,000 × 4% × 6 ÷ 12 | 400 |
| Shares: price rises 10% in six months | 20,000 × 10% | 2,000 |
| Shares: price falls 10% in six months | −20,000 × 10% | (2,000) |

1. The deposit gives a certain return of US\$400 and she can have her money back on a known date.
2. Shares might give US\$2,000, but could also lose US\$2,000, and she may be unable to sell them at a good price when she needs the cash.
3. Money she will need within a year belongs in the money market; money she can leave for many years can go into the capital market.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, how financial institutions help a bakery]
**Context.** Farai's bakery in Bulawayo needs US\$100,000 for a new factory, repayable over ten years, and US\$10,000 of working capital for flour.

**Point.** Different institutions supply different parts of the finance.

**Application.** The commercial bank could provide a ten-year loan and an overdraft for the flour. If the bakery becomes a public company, it could raise funds by selling shares on the Zimbabwe Stock Exchange. A micro-finance institution would lend only small sums.

**Analysis.** The long-term factory needs the capital market (a loan or shares), and the short-term flour needs the money market (an overdraft). A stock exchange listing could raise a lot of capital but would bring costs, rules and publicity, which a small bakery may not want.

**Judgement.** Farai should use a bank loan for the factory and an overdraft for the flour, since the bakery is still small. He could consider a listing later if it grows large.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the money market and capital market"}
The money market is for short-term funds (up to a year); the capital market is for long-term finance. A treasury bill is a money-market instrument; shares are capital-market instruments.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the stock exchange lends money to businesses"}
A stock exchange is a market in which securities are bought and sold. A company raises money by selling new shares to investors; the exchange provides the place and the rules.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving the same role to all international institutions"}
The African Development Bank and the World Bank mainly support development projects; the IMF concentrates on international financial stability and lends to countries with payment problems.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the link to businesses"}
A good answer says how the market or institution affects a business, for example through finance for expansion or the interest rates a bank charges.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (state and distinguish money and capital markets, name the roles of institutions) and Paper 2 (essays on the role of financial markets and institutions). It is not part of Business Enterprise Skills or Cambridge.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline*: give the market or institution and its main role. *Distinguish/differentiate*: give a feature of each with a comparison word. *Describe/explain*: say how the institution helps a business or the economy. *Discuss/evaluate*: weigh the benefits to businesses against the costs and risks.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Financial markets link people with spare money to those who need it; the money market is short term, the capital market long term.
- Their functions are providing funds, trading securities (primary and secondary markets), setting prices and spreading risk.
- The Zimbabwe Stock Exchange trades shares and other securities of listed companies.
- Commercial banks supply deposits, loans, overdrafts and payment services; other institutions include building societies, micro-finance institutions, insurers and pension funds.
- The African Development Bank and the World Bank support development; the IMF promotes financial stability and lends to countries with payment problems.
:::
