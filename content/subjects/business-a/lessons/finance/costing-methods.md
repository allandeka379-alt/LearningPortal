## Introduction

Rudo's bakery in Mutare bakes bread and cakes in the same building, with the same ovens, staff and electricity. Her rent, insurance and the manager's salary cannot be traced to a loaf or a cake. To fix prices, she wants to know what each product costs. And when a hotel offers to buy cakes at a low price, she must decide whether to accept. Two methods answer these questions: **full costing** shares every cost, including overheads, among the products; **contribution costing** looks only at the extra money each product earns after its own variable costs.

This topic is in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge AS Level. You should already know fixed and variable costs, and direct and indirect costs.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |
| Absorption costing; overhead absorption; contribution costing; mark-up | Full costing; apportionment and absorption of overheads; contribution costing; cost-plus pricing |

Both boards examine the same two methods. In this course the basis for absorbing overheads (units or labour hours) is always stated in the question.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Full (absorption) costing"}
A method of costing in which all the costs of a business, direct costs and a share of the overheads, are charged to the products or cost centres, so that each product carries its share of every cost.
:::

:::definition{term="Contribution"}
Selling price per unit less variable cost per unit. Total contribution is contribution per unit multiplied by the units sold. It is the amount each product contributes towards fixed costs and profit.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Formulas"}
Overhead absorption rate = total overheads ÷ total of the chosen basis (units or labour hours). Overhead absorbed = rate × the product's units or hours. Full cost per unit = direct cost per unit + overhead absorbed per unit. Contribution per unit = price − variable cost per unit. **Profit = total contribution − fixed costs.** Price using a mark-up = cost × (1 + mark-up percentage).
:::

## Full costing

Overheads cannot be traced to a product, so they are **apportioned** (shared between departments or cost centres on a fair basis, such as floor area for rent) and **absorbed** into products on a stated basis, commonly units made or direct labour hours. The full cost per unit, plus a profit margin or mark-up, then gives a price.

**Uses:** pricing to cover all costs; valuing inventory; showing the profit of each product; and giving the long-run cost that prices must cover. **Limitations:** the choice of basis is arbitrary and changes the cost of each product; overheads are fixed, so charging them per unit can make a product appear to lose money when it adds to profit; it can lead to refusing worthwhile orders; and it relies on a forecast of output.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: full costing and the effect of the basis]
Rudo makes 4,000 loaves and 1,000 cakes a month. Overheads are US\$6,000. Direct costs are US\$0.55 a loaf and US\$2.30 a cake; selling prices are US\$1.80 and US\$6.00. Bread uses 600 labour hours and cakes 400.

| | Bread | Cakes | Total |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Units made | 4,000 | 1,000 | 5,000 |
| Revenue | 7,200 | 6,000 | 13,200 |
| Direct costs | 2,200 | 2,300 | 4,500 |
| Labour hours | 600 | 400 | 1,000 |
| Overheads absorbed (US\$6 per hour) | 3,600 | 2,400 | 6,000 |
| **Profit** | 1,400 | 1,300 | 2,700 |

1. Overhead rate = 6,000 ÷ 1,000 hours = US\$6 an hour. Bread absorbs 600 × 6 = 3,600; cakes 400 × 6 = 2,400.
2. Full cost per loaf = 0.55 + 3,600 ÷ 4,000 = 0.55 + 0.90 = US\$1.45. Full cost per cake = 2.30 + 2,400 ÷ 1,000 = US\$4.70.
3. With a 25% mark-up the prices would be 1.45 × 1.25 = US\$1.8125 (about US\$1.81) and 4.70 × 1.25 = US\$5.875 (about US\$5.88).
4. If overheads were absorbed on units made instead (6,000 ÷ 5,000 = US\$1.20 a unit), bread would absorb 4,800 and cakes 1,200, giving profits of 200 for bread and 2,500 for cakes. The total profit of US\$2,700 is the same, but the profit of each product depends on the basis.
:::

## Contribution costing

Contribution costing ignores the sharing of fixed costs. It treats each product's contribution (price − variable cost) as money towards the fixed costs and profit. Total contribution of all products less total fixed costs gives profit. **Contribution is not profit**: a product with a positive contribution helps to pay the fixed costs, even if it shows a loss under full costing.

**Uses:** special order decisions; deciding whether to keep a product; choosing between products when a resource is short; and break-even and budgeting. **Limitations:** it ignores fixed costs, which must be covered in the long run; selling everything at contribution-based prices would make a loss; and it assumes costs can be split reliably into fixed and variable.

**Special orders.** A special order at a price below full cost is worth accepting if the price is above variable cost per unit (the contribution is positive), there is **spare capacity** and it does **not affect normal sales** or the price paid by regular customers.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: contribution and a special order]
Using Rudo's figures: contribution per loaf = 1.80 − 0.55 = US\$1.25; per cake = 6.00 − 2.30 = US\$3.70. A hotel offers to buy 500 extra cakes at US\$4.00 each. Rudo has spare oven time, and the hotel's cakes will not reduce her normal sales. No extra fixed costs arise.

| | Bread | Cakes | Total |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Contribution per unit (US\$) | 1.25 | 3.70 | |
| Total contribution (US\$) | 5,000 | 3,700 | 8,700 |
| Less fixed costs (overheads) | | | (6,000) |
| **Profit** | | | 2,700 |

1. Total contribution = 4,000 × 1.25 + 1,000 × 3.70 = 5,000 + 3,700 = US\$8,700; less fixed costs of 6,000 = US\$2,700, the same profit as under full costing.
2. The special order: contribution per cake = 4.00 − 2.30 = US\$1.70; for 500 cakes = US\$850 extra profit.
3. Under full costing the hotel's price of US\$4.00 is below the full cost of US\$4.70 a cake, so it would be rejected, and Rudo would lose US\$850 of profit.
4. The order should be accepted only if the conditions hold: spare capacity, no loss of normal sales and no wider price cutting.
:::

## Cost information for decisions

Cost information is used to set **prices** (full cost plus a mark-up, or at least above the variable cost for a one-off sale), to decide on **special orders**, to **monitor performance** by comparing actual costs with expected costs, to decide whether to **keep or drop** a product and to **improve performance** by finding costs that can be reduced. A business uses full costing to ensure that, over time, all costs are covered, and contribution costing for short-term decisions where fixed costs are already committed.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, should the cakes be dropped?]
**Context.** On a full-cost basis with overheads absorbed on units (US\$1.20 a unit), the bread shows a profit of only US\$200 in Example 1 while cakes show US\$2,500. A friend suggests dropping bread because its profit is so small.

**Point.** The bread's profit looks small only because it absorbs a large share of the fixed overheads.

**Application.** Bread's contribution is US\$5,000. If bread were dropped, the fixed overheads of US\$6,000 would not disappear; Rudo would lose US\$5,000 of contribution, and the cakes' contribution of US\$3,700 would not cover the fixed costs of US\$6,000, leaving a loss of US\$2,300.

**Analysis.** A product should be dropped only if its contribution is negative or if the space and resources can earn more elsewhere, and fixed costs would actually be saved. The basis used to absorb overheads made the bread look poor.

**Judgement.** Rudo should keep the bread. The full-cost figure is useful for pricing in the long run, but the contribution figure is the right guide to this decision.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing contribution with profit"}
Contribution is price minus variable cost. Profit is total contribution minus fixed costs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Rejecting every order priced below full cost"}
If there is spare capacity and the price exceeds variable cost, a special order can add to profit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the conditions for a special order"}
Check for spare capacity, no loss of normal sales and no extra fixed costs before accepting.
:::

:::mistake{title="Not stating the basis of absorption"}
Say whether overheads are absorbed per unit or per labour hour. A different basis gives a different cost for each product.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (calculate full cost, contribution or profit) and Paper 2 (data response and essays on costing and pricing); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (calculate contribution and profit, explain a special order decision, evaluate full costing).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate*: state the basis, show the rate or contribution per unit and then the total. *State/outline*: name a use or a limitation. *Explain/analyse*: link the method to a decision, for example a special order with spare capacity. *Evaluate/discuss/advise*: weigh the extra profit against the risk to normal sales and prices and recommend with conditions.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Full costing shares overheads among products on a stated basis (units or labour hours); the basis changes each product's cost and profit.
- Contribution = price − variable cost per unit; profit = total contribution − fixed costs.
- Full costing helps long-run pricing; contribution costing guides short-run decisions such as special orders and dropping products.
- Accept a special order if the price exceeds variable cost, there is spare capacity and normal sales are not affected.
- Both methods rely on accurate cost information; neither should be used mechanically.
:::
