## Introduction

Farai owns a hardware shop in Chinhoyi that has traded well for six years. He wants US\$30,000 for a second delivery truck and a bigger stock of cement. A bank loan would cost interest and require security, so he asks first: how much of this money can the business find from itself? He could put in more of his own savings, keep part of this year's profit instead of drawing it out, sell a spare building, or reduce the stock and the money customers owe him. These are **internal sources of finance**: money raised from within the business or its owners, rather than from outside lenders or new investors.

The topic is tested in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge AS Level. You should already understand why businesses need finance and what working capital is.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |
| Owner's capital; ploughed-back profit; sale of unwanted assets; leaseback; reducing stock and debtors | Owner's investment (personal funds); retained profit; sale of assets; sale and leaseback; reducing inventory and trade receivables |

Both boards examine the same sources. Cambridge expects the sources to be linked to the form of business, cost, availability and the use of the money in analysis and evaluation.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Internal source of finance"}
Money raised from within the business or from its owners, such as the owner's own funds, profits kept in the business, and the sale or release of assets already held.
:::

:::definition{term="Retained earnings (ploughed-back profit)"}
Profit after tax that is kept in the business for use, instead of being paid out to owners as drawings or dividends.
:::

:::definition{term="Sale and leaseback"}
Selling a non-current asset, such as a building, to a finance company and immediately leasing it back, so that the business receives a lump sum of cash and keeps using the asset in return for regular rent.
:::

## The internal sources

| Source | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Owner's investment | The owner (or partners) put in more personal savings | No interest; no outsiders; control kept | Limited by the owner's wealth; risks personal savings |
| Retained earnings | Part of profit is kept instead of being paid out | No interest or repayment; always available if profitable | Only available from past profit; owners get less income; slow |
| Sale of unwanted assets | Spare vehicles, land or machines are sold | Quick cash; no cost of borrowing; saves upkeep | Only possible if there are surplus assets; may sell at a loss |
| Sale and leaseback | A building is sold to a finance firm and rented back | Large sum of cash; asset still used | Rent is a continuing cost; loses future rise in value of the asset |
| Releasing working capital | Reduce stock, collect debts faster, delay payments | Cash raised without borrowing; improves efficiency | Risk of stock-outs; customers may leave; relations with suppliers suffer |

**Cost and availability.** Internal sources usually have no interest, but they have an **opportunity cost**: the owner could have used the money elsewhere or taken it as income. They are limited by the size and profitability of the business, so a new or loss-making business has little available. Sole traders and partnerships depend heavily on the owner's funds, whereas a company can also keep profit within the business for growth.

**Short and long term.** Releasing working capital and selling spare assets help with short-term needs. Retained earnings and the owner's investment fund long-term needs, such as equipment, because they need not be repaid. Sale and leaseback gives a large amount of long-term finance, but the business pays rent for years.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: sale and leaseback]
A fictional hotel in Victoria Falls has a building with a book value of US\$100,000. It sells the building for US\$120,000 and leases it back at US\$10,800 a year. The money would otherwise be borrowed at 8% interest (an assumption).

| Item | Working | US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Cash raised | | 120,000 |
| Gain over book value | 120,000 − 100,000 | 20,000 |
| Annual rent | | 10,800 |
| Annual interest on a loan of the same size | 120,000 × 8% | 9,600 |
| Extra annual cost of leaseback compared with the loan | 10,800 − 9,600 | 1,200 |
| Total rent over 5 years | 10,800 × 5 | 54,000 |

1. The sale raises US\$120,000 at once and the building is still used.
2. Rent of US\$10,800 is US\$1,200 a year more than the interest on a loan of the same size, but a loan would also have to be repaid, whereas the leaseback needs no repayment of the US\$120,000.
3. Over 5 years the hotel pays US\$54,000 in rent and no longer owns the building. If property prices rise, the gain goes to the new owner.
4. Leaseback suits a business that needs cash and values the use of the building more than owning it.
:::

## Retained earnings and releasing working capital

:::worked-solution[Example 2: how much can be retained, and how much working capital can be released?]
Farai's shop makes a profit after tax of US\$42,000. He draws US\$18,000 for himself. His average stock is US\$60,000 and trade receivables US\$24,000. He believes he can cut stock by 15% and receivables by US\$3,000 (assumptions).

| Source | Working | US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Profit retained | 42,000 − 18,000 | 24,000 |
| Stock released | 60,000 × 15% | 9,000 |
| Receivables released | | 3,000 |
| **Total available internally** | 24,000 + 9,000 + 3,000 | 36,000 |

1. After drawings, US\$24,000 of profit stays in the business.
2. Cutting stock by 15% frees 60,000 × 0.15 = US\$9,000, and collecting US\$3,000 of debts sooner frees a further US\$3,000.
3. The total of US\$36,000 is more than the US\$30,000 he wants, so internal sources could cover the need.
4. But the profit is earned over a year, and a stock cut may mean running out of cement, so the plan may need a short-term overdraft as well.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, which internal source for the delivery truck?]
**Context.** Farai needs US\$30,000 for a truck that will last eight years. He has US\$10,000 in savings he does not need for the family, and the shop has a spare storeroom worth US\$12,000.

**Point.** The truck is a long-term need, so long-term internal finance suits it best.

**Application.** He could invest his US\$10,000, sell the storeroom for US\$12,000 and retain US\$8,000 of this year's profit: 10,000 + 12,000 + 8,000 = US\$30,000.

**Analysis.** None of this carries interest and he keeps full control. However, it takes all his spare savings and the storeroom, leaving little reserve, and retaining profit means less income for his family this year.

**Judgement.** The mix is appropriate if the shop's trading is steady. If a bad month would leave him without cash, he should keep some savings back and use a bank loan for the rest.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying internal finance is 'free'"}
Internal finance has an opportunity cost: the owner could have used the money elsewhere or taken it as income.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing retained earnings with cash in the bank"}
Retained earnings are profit kept in the business. They may already be spent on stock or equipment and may not be in cash.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that sale and leaseback adds a rent cost"}
The cash is received once, but the rent is paid every year, and future increases in the asset's value are lost.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using one source for every need"}
Match the source to the need: working capital for short-term needs, the owner's funds or retained profit for long-term ones.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 and Paper 2 and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 (state and describe sources, advantages and disadvantages); Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (identify, explain, analyse and judge which source suits a business).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline/identify*: name the source. *Explain/describe*: say how the source raises money and give one advantage or disadvantage for the business. *Analyse*: link the source to the form of business, the time period and the cost. *Discuss/assess/evaluate/justify*: compare two or more sources for the stated purpose and recommend one with a reason.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Internal sources: the owner's investment, retained earnings, sale of unwanted assets, sale and leaseback, and releasing working capital.
- They carry no interest but have an opportunity cost and are limited by the business's size and profit.
- Releasing working capital and selling spare assets suit short-term needs; the owner's funds and retained earnings suit long-term needs.
- Sale and leaseback gives a large sum but creates a continuing rent and gives up any rise in the asset's value.
- The best source depends on the purpose, the time period, the cost and how much risk the owner will accept.
:::
