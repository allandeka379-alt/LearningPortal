## Introduction

Nyasha bakes bread in Gweru. Her rent, the baker's wages and the oven loan are the same whether she bakes 500 loaves or 5,000 in a month, but flour, packaging and gas rise with every loaf. When a hotel asks her for a price on 2,000 extra loaves a month, she needs to know what the extra loaves would cost and what each loaf costs on average. Without accurate **cost information** she may agree to a price that loses money.

This topic is in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge AS Level. It prepares the ground for costing methods, break-even and budgets. You should already be able to read a table and calculate percentages.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |

Both boards examine the same classification and calculations. In this course, 'average cost' is total cost divided by output, and 'marginal cost' is the extra cost of producing one more unit.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fixed cost"}
A cost that does not change when the level of output changes in the short run, such as rent, insurance, managers' salaries and loan repayments.
:::

:::definition{term="Variable cost"}
A cost that changes directly with the level of output, such as raw materials, packaging and, often, wages paid per item.
:::

:::definition{term="Direct cost and indirect cost"}
A **direct cost** can be traced to a particular product or job (flour in a loaf). An **indirect cost** (overhead) cannot be traced to one product, because it is shared by many (factory rent, the manager's salary, electricity for the building).
:::

:::definition{term="Marginal cost"}
The extra cost of producing one more unit of output.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Formulas"}
Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost. Average (unit) cost = total cost ÷ output. Marginal cost = change in total cost ÷ change in output. Variable cost = variable cost per unit × output (if the cost per unit is constant). **Semi-variable** costs have a fixed part and a variable part (for example electricity with a fixed charge plus a charge per unit used).
:::

## Why accurate cost information matters

Accurate cost information allows a business to: set prices that cover costs and give a profit; decide whether to accept a special order; measure performance against targets; control spending; and decide whether to continue a product. Wrong cost information leads to wrong decisions: if costs are understated the business may underprice and make a loss; if overstated it may refuse profitable work.

## Classifying costs

| Cost | Fixed or variable? | Direct or indirect? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flour in the bakery | Variable | Direct |
| Baker paid per loaf | Variable | Direct |
| Bakery rent | Fixed | Indirect |
| Oven loan repayment | Fixed | Indirect |
| Packaging per loaf | Variable | Direct |
| Electricity (fixed charge plus a charge per unit) | Semi-variable | Indirect |
| Fuel for delivering one customer's order | Variable | Direct to that order |
| Supervisor's salary | Fixed | Indirect |

A cost can be classified in two ways at once. Whether a cost is fixed or variable depends on the time period: in the long run all costs are variable, because a business can move to a different building or sell its machines.

For a service business such as a hair salon, the stylist's wage per client is variable and direct; the rent, the chairs and the licence are fixed and indirect. For a small factory, raw materials are variable and direct, the factory rent is fixed and indirect, and machine maintenance is often semi-variable.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: total, average and marginal cost]
Nyasha's fixed costs are US\$1,500 a month. Flour and other variable costs are US\$0.40 a loaf for the first 3,000 loaves, and US\$0.50 a loaf for each loaf after that, because the extra loaves need overtime pay.

| Output (loaves) | Fixed cost (US\$) | Variable cost (US\$) | Total cost (US\$) | Average cost per loaf (US\$) | Marginal cost per loaf (US\$) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 1,500 | 0 | 1,500 | - | - |
| 1,000 | 1,500 | 400 | 1,900 | 1.90 | 0.40 |
| 2,000 | 1,500 | 800 | 2,300 | 1.15 | 0.40 |
| 3,000 | 1,500 | 1,200 | 2,700 | 0.90 | 0.40 |
| 4,000 | 1,500 | 1,700 | 3,200 | 0.80 | 0.50 |
| 5,000 | 1,500 | 2,200 | 3,700 | 0.74 | 0.50 |

1. At 3,000 loaves variable cost = 3,000 × 0.40 = US\$1,200 and total cost = 1,500 + 1,200 = US\$2,700.
2. At 4,000 loaves variable cost = 1,200 + 1,000 × 0.50 = US\$1,700 and total cost = US\$3,200.
3. Average cost at 4,000 = 3,200 ÷ 4,000 = US\$0.80.
4. Marginal cost between 3,000 and 4,000 loaves = (3,200 − 2,700) ÷ 1,000 = US\$0.50 a loaf.
5. Average cost falls as output rises because the fixed cost is spread over more loaves. The marginal cost rises from 0.40 to 0.50 after 3,000 loaves because of overtime.
:::

## How costs change with output

- **Fixed costs** stay the same in total, so fixed cost per unit falls as output rises (US\$1,500 ÷ 1,000 = 1.50 a loaf; US\$1,500 ÷ 5,000 = 0.30).
- **Variable costs** rise in total as output rises. Variable cost per unit stays constant until something changes (overtime, a larger discount on bulk materials).
- **Total cost** rises with output, but by less than output if fixed costs are large.
- **Fixed costs can step up**: at a certain output the business may need a second oven, which adds a new fixed cost (a 'step' in fixed cost).

:::worked-solution[Example 2: semi-variable cost and an extra order]
A fictional hair salon in Masvingo pays an electricity charge of US\$80 a month plus US\$0.15 for each unit used. In a month with 600 units, the bill is 80 + 600 × 0.15 = US\$170. The salon rents its premises for US\$300 a month and pays US\$3 of products and US\$4 of wages per client. It serves 200 clients.

| Item | Working | US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Electricity (semi-variable) | 80 + 600 × 0.15 | 170 |
| Rent (fixed) | | 300 |
| Products (variable) | 200 × 3 | 600 |
| Wages (variable) | 200 × 4 | 800 |
| **Total cost** | | 1,870 |
| **Average cost per client** | 1,870 ÷ 200 | 9.35 |

1. The electricity bill has a fixed element (80) and a variable element (90).
2. Fixed costs total 300 + 80 = US\$380 and variable costs 90 + 600 + 800 = US\$1,490, so the total is 380 + 1,490 = US\$1,870.
3. If the salon serves 10 more clients, products and wages add 10 × 7 = US\$70, and the electricity a little more. The marginal cost of a client is therefore a little over US\$7, far less than the average of US\$9.35.
4. Quoting the average cost for extra work would overstate the real extra cost.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, pricing a hotel order]
**Context.** A hotel asks Nyasha to supply 1,000 extra loaves a month at US\$0.75 each. She already bakes 3,000 loaves, and her average cost at that output is US\$0.90.

**Point.** The price is below her average cost, but that may not be the right measure.

**Application.** Moving from 3,000 to 4,000 loaves adds US\$500 of cost (marginal cost US\$0.50 a loaf) and brings in 1,000 × 0.75 = US\$750.

**Analysis.** The order adds US\$750 − US\$500 = US\$250 to profit, because the fixed costs are already covered. At average cost she would have refused it.

**Judgement.** Nyasha should accept if she has the spare capacity and the hotel does not reduce her other sales. She must check that overtime is available and that the hotel's price does not become the price all her customers expect.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the average cost as the cost of one more unit"}
The extra cost of one more unit is the marginal cost. Fixed costs are already incurred, so the average overstates the cost of extra output.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a cost fixed because it is large"}
Fixed means it does not change with output. Wages are variable if paid per item, fixed if a monthly salary.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing direct and variable"}
A direct cost can be traced to a product; a variable cost changes with output. The two usually go together but not always (a supervisor's salary is indirect and fixed).
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to add the fixed cost in total cost"}
Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost. The variable cost alone is not the total cost.
:::

:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/define/identify*: give the type of cost with a reason. *Calculate*: show total cost, then divide for the average, or take the change in total cost for the marginal cost. *Explain/analyse*: link the type of cost to a decision, for example why average cost falls as output rises. *Evaluate/discuss*: judge how reliable or useful the cost information is for a decision.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Fixed costs do not change with output in the short run; variable costs change directly with output; semi-variable costs have both parts.
- Direct costs can be traced to a product; indirect costs (overheads) are shared.
- Total cost = fixed + variable; average cost = total ÷ output; marginal cost = change in total cost ÷ change in output.
- Average cost usually falls as output rises because fixed costs are spread over more units.
- Accurate cost information supports pricing, special orders and cost control; use marginal cost for extra output.
:::
