## Introduction

Rudo wants to open a second bakery in Mutare. It will cost US\$60,000 to fit out and equip. She has some savings, but not enough, so she must look outside the business. Could she borrow from a bank, ask a friend to become a partner, find investors for shares, hire the ovens instead of buying them, or apply for a grant? Each choice has a different cost and risk, and each affects how much of the business she will still own. **External sources of finance** are those that come from outside the business and its owners.

This topic is tested in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and in Cambridge AS Level. You should already know the internal sources of finance and the difference between short-term and long-term needs.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Debt finance"}
Money borrowed from outside the business which must be repaid, normally with interest. Examples are bank loans, overdrafts, debentures, mortgages, hire purchase and leasing.
:::

:::definition{term="Equity finance"}
Money raised by selling a share in the ownership of the business. Investors expect a share of profit (dividends) and do not need to be repaid on a fixed date. Examples are share capital, new partners and venture capital.
:::

:::definition{term="Leasing"}
An arrangement in which the business pays regular rent to use an asset owned by someone else, without owning it at the end of the lease.
:::

## The external sources

| Source | How it works | Time period | Key advantages | Key disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Share capital | A company sells shares to investors | Long term | No repayment or interest; large sums possible | Dividends are paid; ownership and control are shared; legal cost |
| Debentures | Company borrows by issuing certificates that pay fixed interest | Long term | Fixed cost; no loss of control | Interest must be paid; must be repaid on a set date |
| New partners | Another person joins and puts in capital | Long term | No interest; skills may be gained | Profits and control shared; disagreement; unlimited liability |
| Venture capital | Specialist investors buy shares in a risky new business | Long term | Large sums for risky ventures; advice | Investors take a share and may want control and an exit |
| Bank overdraft | The bank allows the account to be overdrawn up to a limit | Short term | Flexible; interest only on the amount used | Can be withdrawn at short notice; interest rate often high |
| Bank loan | A fixed sum is borrowed and repaid over a set period | Medium or long term | Planned repayments; control kept | Interest and security needed; hard for new firms |
| Mortgage | A long-term loan secured on property | Long term | Large sums; lower interest because of the security | Property may be lost if repayments are missed |
| Leasing | The business rents an asset | Medium term | No large outlay; the lessor may maintain | Asset not owned; total cost may exceed buying |
| Hire purchase | The asset is paid for by a deposit and instalments; owned at the end | Medium term | Spreads cost; asset owned in the end | Total cost higher than the cash price |
| Debt factoring | Invoices are sold for immediate cash | Short term | Quick cash; the factor collects the debts | Fee reduces profit |
| Trade credit | Suppliers allow payment after delivery | Short term | Free if paid on time; improves cash flow | May lose discounts; poor relations if late |
| Micro-finance | Small loans to small businesses, often without conventional security | Short or medium term | Available to those banks refuse | High interest; small amounts |
| Crowd funding | Many people give small sums through a website | Short or medium term | Does not need a bank; tests demand | Needs a good idea and publicity; may fail to reach the target |
| Government grant | Money given for a stated purpose | Varies | Usually does not need to be repaid | Conditions attached; competition for funds |

## Debt and equity

Debt must be repaid and carries interest whether or not the business makes a profit, but it does not give up ownership. Equity does not have to be repaid and dividends can be reduced in a poor year, but the owners share profit and may lose control. A business with too much debt is exposed to a rise in interest rates and may be unable to pay in a poor year.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: hire purchase, leasing or cash?]
Rudo needs an oven that costs US\$15,000 in cash. A hire purchase deal asks a deposit of 20% and 24 monthly instalments of US\$560. A lease costs US\$400 a month for 36 months and the oven is returned at the end.

| Option | Upfront (US\$) | Monthly (US\$) | Months | Total paid (US\$) | Owns the oven at the end? |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Buy with cash | 15,000 | 0 | 0 | 15,000 | Yes |
| Hire purchase | 3,000 | 560 | 24 | 16,440 | Yes |
| Leasing | 0 | 400 | 36 | 14,400 | No |

1. Deposit = 20% × 15,000 = US\$3,000. Instalments = 560 × 24 = US\$13,440. Total hire purchase = 3,000 + 13,440 = US\$16,440.
2. Hire purchase costs 16,440 − 15,000 = US\$1,440 more than the cash price (9.6% of the cash price).
3. The lease costs US\$14,400 in total, but Rudo owns nothing at the end and must keep paying or buy a new oven.
4. Hire purchase needs only US\$3,000 at the start, so it suits a business short of cash but expecting the oven to last.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: loan or an equity partner?]
To raise US\$60,000 Rudo can borrow it at 9% interest a year, or sell a 25% share of the business to an investor who takes 25% of profit. Profit before interest is estimated for a good year and a poor year.

| Profit before interest (US\$) | Owner keeps with loan (US\$) | Owner keeps with equity partner (US\$) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 40,000 | 34,600 | 30,000 |
| 10,000 | 4,600 | 7,500 |

1. Interest = 60,000 × 9% = US\$5,400 a year, paid whatever the profit.
2. Good year: with the loan Rudo keeps 40,000 − 5,400 = 34,600; with the partner she keeps 75% × 40,000 = 30,000.
3. Poor year: with the loan she keeps 10,000 − 5,400 = 4,600; with the partner she keeps 75% × 10,000 = 7,500.
4. The two are equal when profit − 5,400 = 0.75 × profit, so profit = 5,400 ÷ 0.25 = US\$21,600. Above that the loan leaves her better off; below it the partner does. The loan also lets her keep full control.
:::

## Choosing between sources

The right source depends on the amount, how long the money is needed, the cost, the risk, the security available, the form of business and how much control the owner will give up. Sole traders cannot sell shares. Private companies sell shares to chosen people; public companies can sell to the public. Short-term needs (wages, stock) suit an overdraft, trade credit or factoring; long-term needs (property, machinery) suit loans, mortgages, share capital or leasing. Choosing between sources is covered in the next lesson.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: case analysis, microfinance or a grant?]
**Context.** Chipo runs a small sewing project in Masvingo. She needs US\$3,000 for two machines. Banks have refused a loan because she has no security. A micro-finance lender will lend at a high interest rate, and a government youth programme offers a grant but takes three months to decide.

**Point.** A grant would be the cheapest, and micro-finance is the faster.

**Application.** The micro-finance loan could be agreed in a week and does not need security, but its high interest would reduce her profit. The grant would not need to be repaid, but the machines would be delayed by three months and she might not win it.

**Analysis.** Delay costs lost orders; high interest costs profit. If orders are waiting, the lost sales of three months could exceed the interest cost, but if the loan is large compared with her income she may struggle to repay.

**Judgement.** Chipo should apply for the grant and ask the lender for a small loan to buy one machine now, so she can fulfil orders and repay quickly. If the grant is awarded she can buy the second machine.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing debt with equity"}
Debt must be repaid with interest and does not give up ownership. Equity is not repaid but gives investors a share of profit and control.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every source suitable for every purpose"}
Match the period: an overdraft for a short-term shortage, a mortgage or share capital for long-term property.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that hire purchase costs more than the cash price"}
The total paid includes the cost of spreading payments. Leasing leaves the business without the asset at the end.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the form of business"}
A sole trader or partnership cannot sell shares. New partners are used by partnerships, share capital by companies.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 (state or describe sources, advantages and disadvantages); Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (identify, explain, calculate hire purchase or interest, and evaluate the choice for a given business).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/identify*: name the source and the period. *Describe/explain*: say how the source works and give one advantage or disadvantage in the context. *Analyse*: link the source to cost, risk and control. *Discuss/assess/evaluate*: compare sources for the stated need and recommend one, with a reason.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- External sources come from outside the business: shares, debentures, partners, venture capital, loans, overdrafts, mortgages, leasing, hire purchase, factoring, trade credit, micro-finance, crowd funding and grants.
- Debt carries interest and must be repaid but does not share ownership; equity shares profit and control but needs no repayment.
- Short-term needs suit overdrafts, trade credit and factoring; long-term needs suit loans, mortgages and share capital.
- Hire purchase costs more than the cash price but gives ownership; leasing gives use without ownership.
- The right source depends on the amount, the period, the cost, the risk, the security and the business's form.
:::
