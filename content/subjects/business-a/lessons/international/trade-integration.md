## Introduction

Nhamo Solar (a fictional firm in Bulawayo) installs solar panels for schools and clinics. Most of the panels are imported, and when the government places a duty on imported panels, the firm has to decide whether to pay more, switch to a local supplier or find a trade partner in a neighbouring country with a lower duty. At the same time it hopes to sell installation services to customers in Botswana and Zambia, where regional agreements may make selling easier.

International trade creates both opportunities (bigger markets, cheaper supplies) and threats (foreign competition, barriers). It is tested in Cambridge Paper 3 and Paper 4 (A Level), in ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 and 2 (Form 6) and in Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 (Form 5). You should already know the effects of exchange rates and government policy on business.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Imports and exports"}
**Imports** are goods and services bought from other countries. **Exports** are goods and services sold to other countries. The difference between the value of exports and imports of goods is the **balance of trade**; the **balance of payments** is the wider record of all money flowing in and out of the country.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade barriers"}
Government measures that limit imports to protect home producers or for other reasons. They include **tariffs** (taxes on imports), **quotas** (limits on quantity), **embargoes** (a complete ban on trade with a country), **standards and licences** (technical or safety requirements, import licences) and **exchange controls** (limits on the foreign currency available to importers).
:::

:::definition{term="Trade agreement and economic integration"}
A **trade agreement** is a deal between countries to reduce barriers and trade more freely. **Economic integration** is the closer linking of the economies of countries. Arrangements may be **bilateral** (two countries), **multilateral** (many countries) or **regional** (neighbouring countries).
:::

## Why international trade matters to a business

- **Bigger markets**: selling abroad adds customers and can allow economies of scale.
- **Specialisation**: countries produce what they make best; firms buy cheaper or better inputs from abroad.
- **Supplies and technology**: access to raw materials and machinery not available at home.
- **Competition**: imports force home firms to improve quality and efficiency, but may also threaten them.
- **Spreading risk**: sales in several countries reduce dependence on one economy.

Risks include currency changes, delays, differences in law and culture, and dependence on foreign suppliers.

## Trade barriers

| Barrier | How it works | Effect on business |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tariff | A tax on imports | Importers' costs rise; home producers are protected; government gains revenue |
| Quota | A limit on the quantity of imports | Limits supply, raises prices; importers cannot expand |
| Embargo | Ban on trade with a country | Cuts off a market or a supplier completely |
| Standards and licences | Technical, health or safety rules; import permits | Extra cost and delay; may block products that do not comply |
| Exchange controls | Limits on foreign currency | Importers may be unable to pay suppliers abroad |

**Firm responses**: pay the cost and raise prices; find local or duty-free suppliers; change the product to meet standards; set up production in the market (a local assembly plant or joint venture); or lobby the government.

## Trade agreements and regional integration

Countries form groupings to reduce barriers. The usual levels are:

1. **Preferential trade area**: lower tariffs among members.
2. **Free trade area**: no tariffs between members, each keeping its own tariffs on outsiders.
3. **Customs union**: free trade among members plus a common external tariff.
4. **Common market**: also free movement of labour and capital.
5. **Economic union**: common economic policies, possibly a common currency.

**Benefits to enterprises**: larger markets with no or low tariffs, cheaper inputs, bigger economies of scale, easier movement of staff and capital, and stronger bargaining power in negotiations.

**Drawbacks**: more competition from firms in partner countries, loss of protection for weak industries, differences in rules that remain, delays at borders, and gains that go mainly to the bigger economies in the group.

Bilateral agreements are quicker to negotiate but cover only two countries; multilateral agreements open wide markets but take long to agree.

## The role of technology in international trade

The internet and mobile payments make it easier to find customers and pay suppliers abroad; tracking systems improve logistics; digital marketing reaches overseas buyers cheaply. Technology lowers the barriers faced by small firms, but increases competition from foreign online sellers.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the effect of a tariff and of the trade balance]
Nhamo Solar can import a solar panel from an exporter at US\$200 at the border. The government imposes a 15% tariff. A locally made panel costs US\$240. (Figures are invented for practice.)

| Imported solar panel (per panel) | No tariff (US\$) | With a 15% tariff (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Price at the border | 200 | 200 |
| Tariff | 0 | 30 |
| Cost to the importer | 200 | 230 |
| Price of a locally made panel | 240 | 240 |
| Gap in favour of the import | 40 | 10 |

**Working.** Tariff = 15% of 200 = US\$30, so the cost to the importer is 200 + 30 = US\$230. The import is still US\$10 cheaper than the local panel (240 − 230). If Nhamo imports 500 panels, the government collects 500 × 30 = US\$15,000.

A fictional country's trade in goods for a year is shown below.

| Trade in goods in a year (fictional country) | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Value of exports | 4,200,000 |
| Value of imports | 5,100,000 |
| Trade balance (exports − imports) | (900,000) |

The balance of trade is 4,200,000 − 5,100,000 = a deficit of US\$900,000.

**Interpretation.** The tariff narrows but does not remove the import's price advantage, so the local producer is only partly protected; if the tariff were raised to 20% the import would cost US\$240 and lose its advantage. A trade deficit means the country is buying more than it sells abroad, which can put pressure on its currency.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, regional integration]
**Context.** Dube Dairies (a fictional firm in Masvingo) exports yoghurt to Zambia. Zambia and Zimbabwe belong to a regional group that has cut tariffs on dairy products to zero. Dube is considering expanding exports to the group's other markets, but a competitor from a larger member country is also entering Zimbabwe.

**Point.** Integration offers Dube a bigger market but also brings more competition.

**Application.** With no tariffs, Dube's yoghurt is cheaper in Zambia than imports from outside the group. Dube can sell larger volumes and reduce the cost per unit. The competitor from the larger country, on the other hand, can now sell in Dube's home market without tariffs, with its lower costs.

**Analysis.** Dube's gain depends on its costs, quality and transport. Cold-chain transport is expensive over long distances and border delays can spoil goods. If the competitor has lower costs, Dube may lose home sales faster than it gains exports.

**Judgement.** Dube should expand exports where transport is short and a distributor can be found, and meanwhile improve quality and branding at home. Integration is beneficial for firms that are efficient and well prepared; for weaker firms it can be a threat.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the balance of trade and the balance of payments"}
The balance of trade covers only exports and imports of goods. The balance of payments is the wider record of all flows, including services, investment income and capital movements.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying tariffs help every business"}
A tariff protects home producers but raises costs for importers and for firms that use imported inputs. Name who gains and who loses.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating integration as only good news"}
Free trade areas bring competition from partner countries. Judge the effect on the specific business and its ability to compete.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
Cambridge Paper 3 and Paper 4 (A Level): short calculations or explanations on a trade barrier or agreement, and an evaluation of the effect of trade links on a firm. ZIMSEC Business Studies (Form 6) and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) Papers 1 and 2: structured questions on imports, exports and barriers and essays on regional integration.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline*: name a barrier or a benefit. *Describe/explain*: say how it works and apply it to the firm. *Analyse*: build the chain (tariff, higher import cost, higher price, lower demand). *Discuss/evaluate*: weigh benefits against drawbacks for this firm, the time period and its ability to respond.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- International trade gives access to bigger markets, supplies and technology but exposes firms to competition and risk.
- Trade barriers include tariffs, quotas, embargoes, standards and licences, and exchange controls.
- Firms respond by raising prices, changing suppliers or products, producing locally or lobbying.
- Trade agreements and regional integration (SADC, COMESA, ECOWAS) open markets but increase competition.
- Technology makes it easier for firms of all sizes to find customers and suppliers abroad.
:::
