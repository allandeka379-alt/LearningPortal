## Introduction

Farai's fictional chain of four hardware shops in Harare has traded for twelve years. Online sellers and a new mining town have both appeared in the last two years. His managers argue about whether to close the weakest shop, open in the mining town or build a website. Without a clear direction every manager decides for his own shop and the business drifts. **Strategic management** gives the whole business one direction, a method for choosing between options and a plan to carry out the choice.

This lesson is for Cambridge A Level only (Papers 3 and 4). ZIMSEC does not set a separate topic on strategic management, although its tools, such as SWOT and decision trees, are used in the same way. You should already know the difference between aims and objectives and have met SWOT analysis and Ansoff.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Strategy"}
A long-term plan of action that sets the direction of the whole business and the way its resources will be used to achieve its objectives.
:::

:::definition{term="Strategic management"}
The process of analysing the business and its environment, choosing a strategy and putting it into practice, then reviewing the results.
:::

:::definition{term="Corporate plan"}
A long-term plan for the whole business, usually covering three to five years, that sets out objectives, the strategy to achieve them and the resources needed.
:::

:::definition{term="Corporate culture"}
The shared values, attitudes and ways of working in a business, often summed up as "the way we do things here".
:::

:::definition{term="Contingency planning"}
Preparing in advance a response to a specific event that could happen, so that the business can act quickly if it does.
:::

## The strategic management process

1. **Strategic analysis.** Find out where the business is and what surrounds it: internal strengths and weaknesses, the external environment (PEST, competitors), and what stakeholders expect. SWOT is the usual summary.
2. **Strategic choice.** Generate options, such as growing, merging or specialising, and choose between them using tools such as decision trees, investment appraisal and the Ansoff matrix, and by asking whether each option fits the mission and objectives.
3. **Strategic implementation.** Turn the choice into action: set tactics, allocate budgets and people, set targets and deadlines, and manage the changes that follow.
4. **Review.** Compare results with targets and adjust. Strategy is not fixed once chosen: if the environment changes, the strategy must change.

**Strategy and tactics.** Strategy is the long-term direction for the business; tactics are the shorter-term actions that carry it out, usually the work of departmental managers, such as a price promotion or a staff roster.

## Corporate planning

A corporate plan links the mission and objectives to the actions of every department, so that marketing, operations, finance and HR pull in the same direction. Its benefits are direction, coordination, a basis for judging performance and a reason for managers to look ahead. Its limits are that it takes time and money, it can become rigid in a fast-changing environment, and it depends on forecasts that may be wrong. A plan that is reviewed each year is more useful than a five-year plan that is left alone.

## Corporate culture

Culture affects how decisions are made. Four types are commonly described (following Charles Handy's classification):

| Type | Typical features | Effect on decisions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Power | Decisions made by one dominant leader; personal loyalty | Fast, but depend on one person's judgement |
| Role | Clear rules, job titles and procedures | Consistent and careful, but slow to react to change |
| Task | Teams formed to solve problems; expertise matters | Flexible and creative, but needs skilled people |
| Person | Individuals' needs come first; common in small professional partnerships | Decisions shaped by individual views; may be slow to agree |

A strong culture can speed up decisions and unite staff, but it can also resist the strategic change a business needs. A business with a role culture that tries to adopt a fast, innovative strategy will often find that its own culture is the obstacle.

## Transformational leadership and strategic change

A **transformational leader** inspires staff with a clear vision, communicates it, involves people and encourages them to go beyond their normal duties, so that large changes can be achieved. This differs from a **transactional** leader, who rewards and punishes against agreed targets. Transformational leadership is important when a business needs deep change, for example moving from a shop-based business to online selling.

**Resistance to change** is natural. Employees may fear job loss, extra work, loss of status, or simply dislike uncertainty. Managers reduce resistance by explaining the reasons for the change early, involving staff in the plan, training them for new tasks, introducing change in stages, and showing early successes. Control of strategic change means setting milestones, checking progress against them and taking corrective action.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: case analysis of a change in strategy]
**Context.** Farai's hardware chain has a role culture: written procedures, clear job titles and slow decisions. He decides to add online ordering with home delivery. Shop managers fear that online sales will take their customers and bonuses.

**Point.** The strategy is sound, but his culture and the managers' resistance make implementation the main risk.

**Application.** The managers' fear of losing customers and bonuses is a cause of resistance. A role culture favours following existing procedures, so staff may avoid the new system.

**Analysis.** If the managers do not support online ordering, delivery will be delayed and customers will be disappointed, which wastes the investment. A transformational approach, in which Farai explains why the change protects the chain's future, shares the vision and changes the bonus scheme so that online orders count for the shop that delivers them, would reduce the fear. Introducing the service in one shop first would allow problems to be corrected.

**Judgement.** The strategy should go ahead, but on a staged timetable with changed bonuses and training. Without this, the culture is likely to hold back the change.
:::

## Contingency planning and crisis management

A **contingency plan** deals with a specific risk, such as a fire, a supplier failure or a cyber attack. It names the event, who will act, what they will do and how long it should take. A **crisis** is a sudden, serious event that threatens the business's survival or reputation, such as a food-poisoning case at a restaurant or a major accident. Crisis management uses:

- a **crisis response team** with named members and authority to act quickly;
- **communication**: prompt, honest messages to staff, customers, the media and regulators, because silence lets rumours spread;
- a **rehearsal**: plans that are practised work better than plans that sit in a file.

The cost is the time spent preparing for events that may never happen, and plans can give false confidence if they are out of date.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis of a crisis response]
**Context.** A fictional lodge near Victoria Falls has a contingency plan for a fire but not for a guest becoming seriously ill after a meal. A guest posts a complaint online; local newspapers pick it up.

**Point.** The lodge's lack of a crisis plan puts its reputation at risk.

**Application.** With no named team, managers disagree about who should speak, and the lodge says nothing for two days. Bookings fall.

**Analysis.** Silence suggests that the lodge has something to hide. A crisis team with a spokesperson could have released a short statement that first day, investigated the kitchen and told guests what was being done. Quick communication limits the damage to reputation, which is the lodge's main asset.

**Judgement.** The lodge should write a plan for food safety incidents, name the team and rehearse it, even though the event is unlikely. The cost of rehearsal is small compared with the revenue lost when bookings fall.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing strategy with tactics"}
Strategy is the long-term direction for the whole business. Tactics are shorter-term actions by departments. "Opening a branch in a new city" is strategic; "a weekend discount" is tactical.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing culture without its effect"}
Naming a culture type scores little. Say how it affects decisions in the case, and whether it helps or hinders the strategy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating resistance as irrational"}
Staff resist change for reasons that are real to them, such as job security. The best answers explain what the fear is and how management can reduce it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
Cambridge Paper 3 (short calculations and analysis from a case) and Paper 4 (the 20-mark essay on strategic management, culture, change or crisis). It is A Level only, so it does not appear in Papers 1 and 2.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define/identify*: give a precise meaning. *Explain/analyse*: link a feature (such as a role culture or a crisis plan) to its effect on this business. *Assess/evaluate/advise*: weigh the strategy or plan against the alternatives, say what matters most in the case and give a justified recommendation with a condition or time-frame.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Strategic management has three steps: analysis, choice and implementation, with review; tactics carry out strategy.
- A corporate plan links objectives and resources across the whole business; it must be reviewed to stay useful.
- Culture (power, role, task, person) affects how decisions are made and how easily change is accepted.
- Transformational leaders use vision and involvement to drive major change.
- Resistance to change is reduced by communication, involvement, training, staging and early success.
- Contingency plans and crisis teams, with fast and honest communication, protect the business when events occur.
:::
