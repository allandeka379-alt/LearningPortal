## Introduction

Thandeka's fictional dairy in Bulawayo sells fresh milk to shops in the city. Sales are steady but margins are thin. She can sell more milk to more shops, add yoghurt, take her milk to Gweru, or start something quite different, such as a farm shop. Each choice carries a different level of risk. Strategic models do not make the decision, but they give managers a structured way to describe the choices, judge their risk and prepare for change.

This lesson covers five models. They appear in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 6) and in Cambridge A Level Papers 3 and 4. Exam questions give you a case and ask you to identify the model's category for a described action and then to evaluate the choice. You should already know the SWOT and PEST tools from the lesson on environmental scanning.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ansoff matrix"}
A framework by Igor Ansoff that shows four growth strategies, formed by combining existing or new products with existing or new markets: market penetration, product development, market development and diversification.
:::

:::definition{term="Blue ocean strategy"}
A strategy of Kim and Mauborgne that creates a new, uncontested market space by offering buyers a different kind of value, instead of competing head-on with rivals in an existing crowded market (a red ocean).
:::

:::definition{term="Scenario planning"}
The development of a small number of detailed, plausible accounts of how the future might unfold, so that the business can test its strategy against each and prepare responses.
:::

:::definition{term="Core competence"}
Something a business does especially well that is valuable to customers, hard for rivals to copy and can be used in more than one product or market.
:::

:::definition{term="Force field analysis"}
Kurt Lewin's technique for managing change, in which the forces driving a change are weighed against the forces restraining it, and managers plan to strengthen the drivers or weaken the restraints.
:::

## The Ansoff matrix

| | Existing products | New products |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Existing markets** | Market penetration (lowest risk) | Product development (medium risk) |
| **New markets** | Market development (medium risk) | Diversification (highest risk) |

- **Market penetration**: sell more of the existing product to existing customers, for example by promotion or lower prices. The business knows the product and the market, so the risk is lowest, but the growth available is limited.
- **Product development**: launch new products in the existing market. The business knows its customers but not the product, so the risk comes from the product failing.
- **Market development**: sell existing products in new markets, such as a new region or a new group of customers. The risk comes from not knowing the new market.
- **Diversification**: new products in new markets. The business knows neither, so risk is highest, but it spreads the firm's dependence across more than one product.

**ZIMSEC detail.** A firm can use the matrix to review its portfolio of products: products with growth possibilities in their present market suggest penetration, and products near the end of their life suggest development of new ones.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: choosing a growth strategy with the Ansoff matrix]
**Context.** Thandeka sells fresh milk to shops in Bulawayo. She considers four moves: (1) a loyalty discount to shops that already buy; (2) a new range of yoghurt for the same shops; (3) selling fresh milk to shops in Gweru; (4) opening a farm shop for tourists selling cheese and crafts.

| Option | Product | Market | Ansoff strategy | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Existing | Existing | Market penetration | Lowest |
| 2 | New | Existing | Product development | Medium |
| 3 | Existing | New | Market development | Medium |
| 4 | New | New | Diversification | Highest |

**Point.** Penetration and development are the lowest-risk routes to growth for a business with thin margins.

**Application.** Option 1 uses customers she already serves, but growth is limited because shops can only sell so much milk. Option 4 needs a new building, new skills and new customers.

**Analysis.** Product development (yoghurt) uses her existing shops and her expertise in dairy, so the main risk is whether the new product sells.

**Judgement.** Thandeka should choose yoghurt for the existing shops, which spreads her risk into a second product while remaining within her core skills. She should delay diversification until profits are more secure.
:::

## Blue ocean strategy

In a **red ocean** many firms fight over the same customers, so prices and profits fall. In a **blue ocean** a firm creates a new market space with little competition. Kim and Mauborgne suggest doing this by changing what the product offers: removing features customers do not value, reducing costs, and adding features that existing offerings ignore. The aim is **value innovation**: higher value for buyers at lower cost for the firm.

A tuck shop that sells airtime, bread, small repairs and mobile money in one place, in a village with none, is creating new value. The weaknesses of the model are that it is hard to predict what customers will value, success may be copied once proven, and the new market can be too small.

## Scenario planning

Scenario planning does not predict one future. It asks, for example: *What if fuel prices double? What if a large mine closes? What if a competitor opens in our town?* For each plausible scenario the managers describe what would happen and prepare a response. It helps firms in uncertain surroundings by preventing surprise, but it takes time, depends on the imagination of managers and does not give probabilities, so it is not a forecast. It is related to, but different from, contingency planning, which prepares a specific response to a specific event.

## Core competence

A core competence meets three tests: it is **valuable** to customers, **hard to copy** and **extendable** to other products or markets. A bakery's skill at making a distinctive bread that people queue for is a candidate. A shop's choice of location is not, if rivals can easily rent a place next door. A firm should build on its core competences when choosing a strategy, and avoid markets where they do not help. The weakness of the idea is that a competence that is valuable today may become less so if customers' needs or technology change, so the firm can become locked into a skill it no longer needs.

## Force field analysis

Lewin pictured a change as a balance of forces. **Driving forces** push for the change; **restraining forces** resist it. Managers list each force, give it a score (for example from 1 for weak to 5 for strong), compare the totals and decide how to tip the balance, which is usually most effective by reducing restraining forces, such as fear or lack of training, instead of simply pushing harder.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: force field scores]
A fictional school production unit near Masvingo wants to take orders by mobile phone. The headteacher and bursar score the forces from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong).

| Driving forces | Score | Restraining forces | Score |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: |
| Parents want to order from home | 4 | Staff fear extra work | 3 |
| Saves time on paper orders | 3 | Poor network at school | 4 |
| Head supports the idea | 2 | Cost of phones | 2 |
| **Total** | **9** | **Total** | **9** |

1. Driving total = 4 + 3 + 2 = 9. Restraining total = 3 + 4 + 2 = 9.
2. The forces are balanced, so the change will stall unless the balance is changed.
3. Raising the drivers (more pressure) tends to increase resistance as well. Reducing a restraint is more effective: for example, offering training to ease staff fears, and using a tablet over the school's wireless network instead of buying phones.
4. If staff fears fall from 3 to 1 and the cost of phones from 2 to 1, the restraining total falls to 1 + 4 + 1 = 6 against 9 and the change can go ahead.
:::

![Force field analysis for the school's phone ordering: driving forces score 4, 3 and 2 (total 9) against restraining forces of 3, 4 and 2 (total 9), so the change stalls; cutting staff fears to 1 and phone cost to 1 reduces the restraining total to 6 and the change can go ahead.](media:diagrams/force-field-scores.png "Figure: force field scores before and after reducing two restraining forces")

## Choosing between the models

| Model | Best used to | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ansoff | Compare the risk of growth routes | Does not say how to do it or if it is profitable |
| Blue ocean | Find new demand away from rivals | Hard to predict what buyers value |
| Scenario planning | Prepare for uncertainty | Time-consuming; no probabilities |
| Core competence | Decide where the firm can beat rivals | May lock the firm into a skill that becomes less valuable |
| Force field | Plan the introduction of a change | Scores are subjective; ignores how forces interact |

A firm usually uses several together: scenarios to see the possible future, core competences to see what it can do, Ansoff to choose a route and force field analysis to carry it out.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the Ansoff boxes"}
Check two things: is the product new or existing, and is the market new or existing? "Existing product to a new region" is market development, not diversification.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a blue ocean as simply 'a new product'"}
A blue ocean changes the basis of competition and creates new demand. A slightly different version of a rival's product in the same market is still a red ocean.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using scenarios as predictions"}
Scenarios are possible futures used to test plans. They are not forecasts, and none of them need be the one that happens.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (state the four Ansoff strategies; classify actions), Paper 2 (data response and essays: discuss the choice of strategy) and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 (analyse and evaluate the use of the models for a firm in a case).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline*: name the model or its categories. *Explain/describe*: say how it works for this business. *Analyse/examine*: link the model to risk, cost or growth. *Evaluate/discuss/justify/advise*: choose a route, weigh its risk against the possible gain, and state the conditions under which the choice would change.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Ansoff: market penetration, product development, market development and diversification, with risk rising from penetration to diversification.
- Blue ocean strategy creates new demand in an uncontested market; a red ocean is a crowded existing market.
- Scenario planning tests strategies against several plausible futures but does not forecast one.
- A core competence is valuable, hard to copy and extendable to other products or markets.
- Force field analysis weighs driving forces against restraining forces (Lewin); reducing restraints is usually more effective than increasing pressure.
- Choose the model that matches the question, and always apply it to the case and judge it.
:::
