## Introduction

Chipo is thinking of opening a fuel-efficient minibus service between Masvingo and Mutare. Before she signs a loan, she asks what she can control (her buses, her drivers, her cash) and what she cannot (fuel prices, new regulations, rival operators, customers' incomes). Gathering and analysing that information is **environmental scanning**, and a handful of tools make it systematic. A business that scans its environment is less likely to be surprised and more likely to choose a strategy that fits.

These tools are tested in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and at Cambridge A Level. They appear in data response and case study questions, where the marks go to applying the tool to the business in the stimulus, not to listing its headings. You should already know the stakeholders of a business and the idea of competition.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |
| Internal and external environment; SWOT; PEST; Porter's five forces | SWOT; PEST and PESTLE (PESTEL); Porter's five forces |

Both boards examine the same tools. Cambridge examines them at A Level only (Papers 3 and 4). ZIMSEC examines them in Form 5 of both syllabi. The extra E (environmental) and L (legal) of PESTEL are used by Cambridge; ZIMSEC questions often use PEST and add legal and environmental factors in the answer.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Environmental scanning"}
The continuous collection and analysis of information about the internal and external environment of a business, so that managers can spot changes early and plan a response.
:::

:::definition{term="Internal environment"}
The factors inside the business that managers can control, such as its finance, staff, skills, products, equipment and reputation.
:::

:::definition{term="External environment"}
The factors outside the business that it cannot control but must respond to, such as the economy, the law, technology, competitors and customers' tastes.
:::

:::definition{term="SWOT analysis"}
A framework that sorts what a business knows about itself and its surroundings into internal **strengths** and **weaknesses** and external **opportunities** and **threats**.
:::

:::definition{term="PEST analysis"}
A checklist of external factors: **political**, **economic**, **social** and **technological**. PESTEL adds **environmental** and **legal**.
:::

## Internal and external factors

The **macro** (general) external environment affects every business: the economy, the law, society and technology. The **micro** (task) environment is the immediate setting of one industry: customers, suppliers and rivals. Managers can influence the micro environment a little, for example by choosing suppliers, but they can only respond to the macro environment.

## SWOT analysis

| | Helpful | Harmful |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Internal** | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| **External** | Opportunities | Threats |

A SWOT is useful only when it leads to a decision. The usual steps are to match strengths to opportunities, to use strengths to reduce threats, to improve weaknesses that block an opportunity, and to avoid situations where weaknesses meet threats. Each entry must be specific and, where possible, evidence-based: "good reputation" is weak, "ninety per cent of orders come from repeat customers" is useful (in an exam, a figure must come from the case, not from your own imagination).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: from SWOT to strategy]
**Context.** Nyasha owns a fictional bakery in Gweru with a loyal customer base, a modern oven and little cash. A supermarket plans to open nearby. Demand for wholesale bread from small shops is rising.

| | Helpful | Harmful |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Internal** | Strength: loyal customers; modern oven | Weakness: little cash for expansion |
| **External** | Opportunity: rising demand from small shops | Threat: supermarket opening nearby |

**Strategy from the SWOT.**

1. Match a strength to an opportunity: the modern oven can produce the larger volumes wholesale customers need.
2. Use a strength against a threat: loyal customers are less likely to switch to the supermarket, so the shop front is protected for now.
3. Fix a weakness that blocks the opportunity: little cash means wholesale expansion must be financed carefully, for example by asking shops for deposits or paying for the extra flour through supplier credit.
4. Judgement: Nyasha should enter wholesale in stages, because the supermarket threat makes the shop front less secure in future, and the lack of cash rules out a fast expansion.
:::

## PEST and PESTEL analysis

| Factor | Examples of questions to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Political | Government policy, taxes, trade restrictions, political stability |
| Economic | Incomes, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, unemployment |
| Social | Population, tastes, attitudes, lifestyles, education |
| Technological | New equipment, mobile payments, automation, internet access |
| Environmental (PESTEL) | Climate, pollution rules, energy supply, waste |
| Legal (PESTEL) | Labour, consumer, health and safety and competition law |

For each factor decide whether it is an **opportunity** or a **threat** to this business and how soon it will matter. The same change can be both: a rise in fuel prices is a threat to a transport operator and an opportunity to a firm that sells solar lighting.

## Porter's five forces

Michael Porter's model explains why some industries are more profitable than others. It looks at the strength of five forces; the stronger they are, the lower the profit available to firms in the industry.

| Force | Strong when | Effect on profit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rivalry among existing firms | Many equal-sized firms, slow growth, similar products, high fixed costs | Price cutting and high marketing costs reduce margins |
| Threat of new entrants | Low start-up costs, no brand loyalty, easy access to customers | New firms take sales and push prices down |
| Threat of substitutes | Other products meet the same need at a similar price | Firms cannot raise prices |
| Bargaining power of buyers | Few large customers, easy to switch, can buy elsewhere | Customers force prices down or demand more service |
| Bargaining power of suppliers | Few suppliers, no alternative inputs, high switching costs | Suppliers raise input prices |

:::worked-solution[Example 2: five forces for a minibus service]
**Context.** Chipo's fictional minibus route between Masvingo and Mutare has several competing operators, low start-up costs for a second-hand vehicle, a rail service that is slow but cheap, a few fuel suppliers and thousands of travellers who can choose any minibus.

**Point.** Profit in this industry is likely to be low.

**Application and analysis.**

1. Rivalry is strong: many operators sell the same service, so fares are competitive.
2. New entrants are a threat: a second-hand vehicle is cheap, so more operators can join the route and share the passengers.
3. Substitutes are a moderate threat: the slower rail service attracts travellers who care most about price.
4. Buyer power is individually weak because each traveller buys one ticket, but passengers can switch operator at no cost, which limits fares in effect.
5. Supplier power is strong: with few fuel suppliers Chipo cannot bargain, and fuel is a major cost.

**Judgement.** The industry is not attractive on price alone. Chipo should compete on reliability, safety and a regular timetable, because these are things rivals cannot copy as quickly as a lower fare, and she should check fuel costs closely before borrowing.
:::

## Using the tools, and their limits

The tools work best together. PEST finds the external changes, five forces shows how they affect the industry, and SWOT combines the results with the business's own position.

| Tool | Main limitation |
| --- | --- |
| SWOT | Subjective; a list is not a plan; can be out of date quickly |
| PEST/PESTEL | A checklist: it does not show which factors matter most or what to do |
| Five forces | Designed for established industries; ignores cooperation between firms; needs knowledge of the whole industry |

A good evaluation also notes that scanning costs time and money, and that small businesses often scan informally, by talking to customers and suppliers.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing headings without applying them"}
"Political: government policy" earns almost nothing. Say which policy, how it affects this business and whether it is an opportunity or a threat.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing internal and external factors"}
A strength or weakness is inside the business; an opportunity or threat is outside it. A competitor's new shop is a threat, not a weakness.
:::

:::mistake{title="Stopping at the analysis"}
A SWOT or five forces diagram that is not turned into a recommendation is incomplete. End with what the business should do and why.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (state or outline the parts of SWOT or PEST; apply to a case), Paper 2 (data response and essays on environmental analysis) and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 (analyse and evaluate the use of the tools for a business in a case study).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline*: name the factors. *Describe/explain*: say what each means for this business. *Analyse/examine*: link a factor to its effect on costs, demand or profit. *Discuss/evaluate/assess*: weigh the usefulness of the tool, say which factors matter most and give a justified judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Environmental scanning collects information about internal and external factors so that managers can plan.
- SWOT sorts internal strengths and weaknesses and external opportunities and threats, and is useful only if it leads to a strategy.
- PEST checks political, economic, social and technological factors; PESTEL adds environmental and legal.
- Porter's five forces (rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, buyer power, supplier power) show how attractive an industry is.
- The tools are subjective, may date quickly and do not by themselves say what to do.
:::
