## Introduction

Tatenda owns a fictional garment workshop in Bulawayo that makes school uniforms. Twenty machinists each sew a whole shirt from start to finish. Output is uneven and the best machinists feel that they earn the same as the slowest. Tatenda is considering two very different ideas. One is to break the job into steps, time each step and pay for every shirt produced. The other is to organise the machinists into small teams, hold weekly meetings and ask them for suggestions. These two ideas come from the two oldest schools of thought about managing people: scientific management and human relations.

This lesson is examined in ZIMSEC Business Studies (Form 5), Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge AS Level (Papers 1 and 2). You should already know why a business wants to motivate its workers. Maslow and Herzberg are covered in a separate lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Scientific management"}
An approach developed by Frederick W. Taylor which treats work as something that can be studied scientifically to find the one best way to do each task, and which motivates workers mainly through pay linked to output.
:::

:::definition{term="Time and motion study"}
The measurement of how long each movement in a task takes, in order to remove unnecessary movement, set a standard time for the job and decide how much output to expect from a worker.
:::

:::definition{term="Piece rate"}
A method of payment by which a worker receives a fixed amount for each unit produced.
:::

:::definition{term="Human relations school"}
An approach developed by Elton Mayo which holds that workers are motivated by social needs, attention from managers and belonging to a group, as well as by pay.
:::

## Taylor's scientific management

Taylor believed that managers, not workers, should decide how work is done and that workers were mainly motivated by money. His ideas are summarised below.

| Idea | What it means in practice | Likely effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time and motion study | Observe the fastest workers, time each movement, remove wasted movements | A standard method and a standard time for each task |
| Specialisation | Break a job into small repeated tasks | Faster learning and higher output, but boring work |
| Piece rate | Pay a fixed rate for each unit produced (sometimes higher beyond a target) | Workers aim to produce more; those who work faster earn more |
| Managers plan; workers do | Managers choose methods and select and train workers for each task | Strong control, but little use of workers' ideas |

**Strengths.** It can raise productivity and unit costs fall. It is easy to supervise because standards are clear. It suits repetitive manufacturing, such as a factory line or packing work. Pay is fair in the sense that more output earns more.

**Weaknesses.** Work becomes monotonous, so quality can fall and staff may leave. It assumes that money is the only motivator. Workers may speed up at the cost of safety or quality. It does not suit creative or customer-service work. Piece rates leave workers with no income when there is a machine fault or a shortage of materials.

## Mayo and the human relations school

Elton Mayo led a series of experiments at the Hawthorne plant of an electrical company in the United States in the 1920s and 1930s. He and his colleagues changed lighting, rest breaks and working hours for groups of workers. Output improved even when conditions were made worse. They concluded that the workers responded because managers were paying them attention and because the workers formed a close group with its own norms. This is called the **Hawthorne effect**.

The main ideas are that employees have **social needs**, that informal groups influence how hard people work, that communication and being consulted make people feel valued, and that supervisors who take an interest in workers get better results.

**Practical applications.** Team working, regular meetings, suggestion schemes, consulting staff before change, training supervisors to listen and showing interest in employees' problems.

**Strengths.** It recognises that people are not machines. Staff who feel valued tend to be more committed, which reduces absence and staff turnover. It works well where teamwork and service quality matter.

**Weaknesses.** It may be slow and costly to hold meetings and consult. It assumes that happy workers are productive, which is not always true. Some workers prefer clear instructions and money. Managers may use the attention as a trick without real change. The Hawthorne studies have been criticised for weak methods, so the conclusions cannot be applied everywhere.

## Comparing the two schools

| | Scientific management (Taylor) | Human relations (Mayo) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| View of workers | Economic: work for money | Social: want belonging and attention |
| Main motivator | Pay linked to output | Group membership, communication, recognition |
| Role of managers | Plan and control | Consult and support |
| Organisation of work | Specialised, individual tasks | Teams and groups |
| Best suited to | Repetitive, measurable production | Service, teamwork, creative work |
| Main risk | Boredom and low morale | Slow decisions; may not raise output |

Most real businesses combine them: standard methods and a bonus for output, together with a team approach and regular consultation.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: piece rate in a workshop]
**Context.** In Tatenda's workshop, a machinist is paid a piece rate of US\$0.40 for each collar stitched. Time and motion study shows that the best method takes 3 minutes per collar. A machinist works 7 hours a day for 5 days.

| Item | Working | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard output per hour | 60 minutes / 3 minutes | 20 collars |
| Standard output per day | 20 x 7 | 140 collars |
| Standard output per week | 140 x 5 | 700 collars |
| Standard weekly pay | 700 x US\$0.40 | US\$280 |
| Pay if a worker makes only 550 | 550 x US\$0.40 | US\$220 |

1. The standard time sets what Tatenda expects: 700 collars a week.
2. Piece rate links pay directly to output, so the machinist who produces 550 earns US\$60 less than the standard.
3. The system motivates the machinist to reach the standard, but it also encourages speed over quality, so Tatenda must inspect work and reject faulty collars.
4. Judgement: piece rate suits this repetitive task, provided that quality is checked and the machinist is not penalised for machine breakdowns.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, choosing between the two approaches]
**Context.** A fictional call centre in Harare answers customer queries for a bank. Staff are paid a fixed salary. Managers measure the number of calls per hour and publish a league table. Customer complaints are rising and staff turnover is high.

**Point.** The managers are using a scientific-management approach in a job where service quality matters.

**Application.** Measuring calls per hour (like a standard time) pushes staff to rush callers off the line. The league table puts pressure on individuals instead of teams.

**Analysis.** Taylor's methods suit measurable, repetitive output, but a call centre produces service, and satisfied customers cannot be counted in calls per hour. Staff who feel under pressure and unvalued leave, which increases recruitment and training costs. A human-relations approach would form teams, ask staff how callers could be helped faster and show interest in their problems. This addresses the social needs of the staff.

**Judgement.** The call centre should keep some measurement of speed but add team targets for customer satisfaction and regular consultation. Pure human relations may not control costs, so a mix is better.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing Taylor with Herzberg or Maslow"}
Taylor is associated with money, time and motion and specialisation; Mayo with social needs and the Hawthorne studies. Herzberg and Maslow are different theorists with different ideas.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that piece rate always raises quality"}
Piece rate rewards quantity. Unless quality is checked, workers may rush and produce faulty goods.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the Hawthorne studies as being about pay"}
The studies found that attention, belonging and group norms affected output. Pay was not the main finding.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Taylor (scientific management): find the one best way by time and motion study, specialise tasks and pay piece rates; it assumes money motivates.
- Strengths: higher output, clear standards, easy supervision; weaknesses: boredom, quality problems and little use of workers' ideas.
- Mayo (human relations): the Hawthorne studies showed that attention, group belonging and communication affect output.
- Applications of Mayo: teams, consultation, suggestion schemes and supportive supervision.
- Taylor suits repetitive measurable work; Mayo suits teamwork and service; many firms combine them.
- Always apply the theory to the case, and weigh cost against the effect on quality and morale.
:::
