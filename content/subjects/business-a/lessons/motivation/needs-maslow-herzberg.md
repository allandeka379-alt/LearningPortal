## Introduction

Rudo manages a fictional bakery in Mutare with twelve employees. Her best baker, Farai, has been late three times this month and the cakes he decorates are no longer as neat. When Rudo asks why, Farai says he is tired of doing the same job every day and nobody ever says whether his work is good. Rudo has raised wages twice this year, yet the problem has not gone away. Motivation theories help a manager understand why: they explain what people want from work and why a pay rise is not always the answer.

This lesson is examined in ZIMSEC Business Studies (Form 5), Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge AS Level (Papers 1 and 2). You should already know that a business has objectives and that its workforce helps it to reach them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Motivation"}
The internal and external forces that make an employee want to put effort into work and to continue doing so in order to achieve objectives.
:::

:::definition{term="Maslow's hierarchy of needs"}
A theory by Abraham Maslow that human needs fall into five levels, from physiological needs at the bottom to self-actualisation at the top. A person is motivated by the lowest level that is not yet satisfied, and a satisfied need no longer motivates.
:::

:::definition{term="Hygiene factors"}
In Herzberg's theory, aspects of the job such as pay, working conditions, company policy, supervision and job security. If they are poor they cause dissatisfaction, but improving them does not by itself motivate.
:::

:::definition{term="Motivators"}
In Herzberg's theory, aspects of the job itself such as achievement, recognition, responsibility, interesting work and the chance to advance. They produce satisfaction and drive people to work harder.
:::

## Why motivate employees?

A motivated workforce usually produces more and better output, wastes less, is absent less often and is less likely to leave, which cuts the cost of recruiting and training replacements. An unmotivated workforce has the opposite effect. Needs may or may not be met at work: a worker whose job is secure and well paid has satisfied some needs, but may still find the work dull or feel that nobody values them. A manager has to know which needs are unmet before choosing an action.

## Maslow's hierarchy of needs

Maslow arranged needs in five levels. A person tries to satisfy the lowest unmet level first.

| Level (lowest to highest) | Meaning | Workplace example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Physiological | Food, water, shelter, rest | A wage large enough to feed a family; rest breaks |
| 2. Safety | Security, stability, protection from harm | Permanent contract; safe machinery; pension |
| 3. Social (belonging) | Friendship, acceptance, teamwork | Team events; friendly colleagues |
| 4. Esteem | Respect, status, recognition | Job title; praise; awards |
| 5. Self-actualisation | Fulfilling one's potential; growth | Challenging projects; creative freedom |

**Use.** A manager can ask which level their workers have reached. A business that pays low wages and has an unsafe workplace cannot expect staff to respond to praise or promotion: the lower needs must be met first. For employees on a decent wage, social and esteem rewards become more powerful.

**Criticisms.** People do not always move up in the order set out; some artists accept low pay in exchange for creative freedom. Needs differ between cultures and individuals. The levels overlap, and it is hard to measure which level a person has reached. Maslow did not write the hierarchy for work, so applying it requires judgement.

## Herzberg's two-factor theory

Frederick Herzberg interviewed employees about when they felt good and bad about their jobs. He concluded that two separate sets of factors are at work.

| | Hygiene factors | Motivators |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Examples | Pay, working conditions, company policy, supervision, relations with colleagues, job security | Achievement, recognition, interesting work, responsibility, advancement, growth |
| If present | No dissatisfaction, but no extra effort | Satisfaction and motivation |
| If missing | Dissatisfaction | No satisfaction (but not dissatisfaction) |

The main message is that removing dissatisfaction is not the same as creating motivation. Raising pay to fix a hygiene problem stops complaints but, once workers are used to it, does not lead to lasting extra effort. To motivate, a manager changes the job itself. Herzberg's practical suggestions include **job enrichment** (giving workers more responsibility, planning and decision-making) and **job enlargement** (adding tasks of a similar level, which is less powerful).

**Criticisms.** Herzberg's evidence came from accountants and engineers, who may not represent all workers. People tend to blame outside factors for failure and credit themselves for success, which may produce the two lists. Satisfaction does not always lead to higher productivity. For some workers, especially in low-paid jobs, pay is a strong motivator.

## Comparing the two theories

Maslow's lower levels (physiological, safety and, partly, social) resemble Herzberg's hygiene factors. Esteem and self-actualisation resemble the motivators. Both say that a manager must first meet basic needs. They differ: Maslow says each need motivates until it is met, whereas Herzberg says hygiene factors never motivate, however good they are.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: diagnosing a bakery with both theories]
**Context.** In the opening scenario, Rudo has raised Farai's wages twice, but he finds the job repetitive and gets no feedback.

**Point.** Farai's lower needs appear to be met, so a further pay rise will not solve the problem.

**Application.** His wage covers his basic needs (physiological) and he has a permanent contract (safety). In Herzberg's terms his main hygiene factors are in place. What is missing is esteem and self-actualisation (Maslow) and the motivators of recognition, responsibility and interesting work (Herzberg).

**Analysis.** Pay raised hygiene factors, which removed dissatisfaction with money but did not create motivation. Farai's lateness and lower quality are signs of a missing motivator. Giving him responsibility for designing cakes and training others (job enrichment), and telling him when his work is good, would speak to esteem and growth.

**Judgement.** Rudo should stop relying on pay and enrich Farai's job. The limit is that Farai may want money too; Rudo should ask him, because these theories do not apply to everyone in the same way.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: sorting factors into Maslow levels and Herzberg categories]
A fictional hardware shop in Gweru offers its staff the following. Classify each.

| Benefit | Maslow level | Herzberg category |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A monthly wage above the local average | Physiological | Hygiene (pay) |
| A written permanent contract | Safety | Hygiene (job security) |
| A Friday staff lunch | Social | Hygiene (relations) |
| Employee of the month award | Esteem | Motivator (recognition) |
| Chance to run the paint section and order stock | Self-actualisation | Motivator (responsibility, growth) |

1. Pay, security and the lunch address lower needs and hygiene factors; they prevent dissatisfaction.
2. The award and the paint section address higher needs and are motivators.
3. A mixed package is sensible: the hygiene factors must be adequate, but only the last two items are likely to produce lasting extra effort.
4. The shop owner should also notice that the last item costs little in cash, whereas a wage rise costs more every month.
:::

## Relevance to a modern workplace

Both theories remain useful because they warn managers against assuming that money alone motivates. They are also simple enough for a small business to apply. However, today's workers differ in what they want: a young graduate may value development, a parent may value flexible hours, and a worker in a country with high unemployment may value security first. A manager must find out what each group wants, and the theories are guides, not laws. Neither theory explains how a worker decides how hard to try; that question is answered by process theories such as Vroom's (Cambridge AS).

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying pay is a motivator in Herzberg's theory"}
Pay is a hygiene factor. It can cause dissatisfaction if it is too low, but Herzberg argued that it does not produce lasting motivation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing the needs without applying them"}
Naming five levels earns little. Link each level to an example from the business in the question and say which level is unmet.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the hierarchy as a fixed ladder"}
Maslow's order is a general pattern. Say "tends to" and give a limitation, such as individual and cultural differences.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Motivation is the desire to put effort into work; motivated workers improve output, quality and retention.
- Maslow: physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation; the lowest unmet need motivates.
- Herzberg: hygiene factors (pay, conditions, policy, supervision, security) prevent dissatisfaction; motivators (achievement, recognition, work itself, responsibility, growth) create satisfaction.
- Job enrichment is the main practical application of Herzberg; pay alone seldom gives lasting motivation.
- Criticisms: individual and cultural differences, limited evidence, and satisfaction does not always raise productivity.
- Always apply the theory to the case and judge how far it explains the behaviour.
:::
