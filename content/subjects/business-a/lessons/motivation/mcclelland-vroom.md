## Introduction

Sipho runs a fictional courier company in Gaborone with a sales team, a customer-service desk and a dispatch office. He notices that his sales staff react very differently to the same offer of a bonus. One, Thandeka, works late to hit every target and asks for feedback on her figures. Another, Kagiso, only cares about leading the team. A third, Neo, would rather work in a friendly group than win any prize. Sipho also finds that some staff give up trying because they do not believe extra effort will earn the bonus. Two Cambridge theories explain what Sipho sees: McClelland's three needs and Vroom's expectancy theory.

This lesson is for **Cambridge AS Level only** (Papers 1 and 2). It is not required for ZIMSEC Business Studies or Business Enterprise Skills, although the ideas help in any motivation essay. You should already know Maslow and Herzberg.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Content theory of motivation"}
A theory which explains motivation by identifying what people need or want from work, for example Maslow, Herzberg and McClelland.
:::

:::definition{term="Process theory of motivation"}
A theory which explains how a person decides to put in effort, by looking at the thinking that links effort, performance and reward, for example Vroom.
:::

:::definition{term="Need for achievement, power and affiliation"}
In McClelland's theory, three needs that people develop to different degrees: achievement (to meet challenging goals and to get feedback), power (to influence and lead others) and affiliation (to be liked and to belong to a group).
:::

:::definition{term="Expectancy theory"}
Vroom's theory that a person's motivation to make an effort depends on three beliefs: that effort will lead to good performance (expectancy), that good performance will lead to a reward (instrumentality) and that the reward is worth having (valence).
:::

## McClelland's three needs

David McClelland argued that, regardless of background, people acquire needs through experience and that three of them matter at work. One need tends to dominate in each person.

| Need | Typical person | Suitable work | Reward that motivates |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Achievement | Sets challenging but reachable goals; wants feedback; takes calculated risks | Sales targets, running a project, entrepreneurial roles | Targets, feedback, personal responsibility |
| Power | Wants to influence others and take charge | Management, leadership, negotiation | Authority, status, responsibility for others |
| Affiliation | Wants friendly relationships; avoids conflict | Teamwork, customer service, personnel roles | Working in a close team, being included |

**Use for managers.** Different jobs need different mixes. A sales manager benefits from high achievement and power; a counsellor or customer-service agent from high affiliation. A manager who knows an employee's dominant need can design the job and the reward to suit: feedback and stretching targets for Thandeka, leadership of a project for Kagiso, team projects for Neo.

**Limitations.** The need is hard to measure accurately and may change over time. People have a mix of needs. The theory does not say how to raise a need. Putting the wrong person in a job (a high-affiliation person as a hard-driving sales manager) can cause conflict.

## Vroom's expectancy theory

Victor Vroom suggested that people ask three questions before they make an effort.

| Term | Question the employee asks | What a manager can do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Expectancy** | Can I reach the target if I try hard? | Give training and resources; set achievable targets |
| **Instrumentality** | If I reach it, will I really receive the reward? | Make the link between performance and reward clear and keep promises |
| **Valence** | Do I value the reward? | Find out what employees want and offer rewards of value to them |

Motivation is high only when **all three** are high; if any one is close to zero, motivation is low. A bonus that employees do not believe they can earn (low expectancy), that is often not paid (low instrumentality) or that is not what they want (low valence) will not work.

**Limitations.** It assumes that people make rational calculations, which they do not always do. It is difficult to measure each belief for each person. Managers may not be able to offer the rewards that people want. It describes decisions but does not name the rewards that work best.

## Content and process theories compared

Content theories (Maslow, Herzberg, McClelland) say **what** motivates. Process theories (Vroom) say **how** people decide to try. Managers need both: the content theories point to rewards that people want, and the process theory checks that people believe that they can earn the rewards.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: matching people to jobs with McClelland]
**Context.** Sipho has three vacancies at his courier company: head of sales (a target-driven job leading a team), a customer-service desk agent and a project analyst who sets targets for the dispatch office. His three applicants are Thandeka (high need for achievement), Kagiso (high need for power) and Neo (high need for affiliation).

| Vacancy | Best fit | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Head of sales | Kagiso | A leading role with authority over a team suits a high need for power |
| Customer-service desk | Neo | Work with customers in a friendly setting suits a high need for affiliation |
| Project analyst | Thandeka | Setting and meeting targets, with feedback on results, suits a high need for achievement |

**Judgement.** Matching needs to jobs should improve motivation and performance. However, Sipho should check that Kagiso uses power to help the team and not to dominate it, and that Neo can handle customer complaints without avoiding conflict. Tests of needs are not exact, so Sipho should also look at experience and references.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: applying Vroom to a failed bonus scheme]
**Context.** A fictional bakery chain in Lusaka offers a bonus to the branch with the highest sales. Staff at one branch show no interest. Interviews reveal that (1) they think their small branch cannot beat the large city branches, (2) the last bonus announced was never paid and (3) the bonus is a hamper of goods, while staff would prefer extra leave.

| Vroom's term | Evidence in the case | Score |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expectancy | They believe they cannot beat the larger branches | Low |
| Instrumentality | The previous bonus was never paid | Low |
| Valence | Staff prefer leave to a hamper | Low |

1. All three are low, so motivation will be very low.
2. Expectancy can be raised by setting targets relative to each branch's own previous sales, so small branches also have a chance.
3. Instrumentality can be raised by paying promptly and publishing the rules.
4. Valence can be raised by asking staff what they would like, for example leave or cash.
5. Judgement: the scheme should be redesigned before any money is spent. If only one belief were low, the manager could fix that one first.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the three needs with Maslow's levels"}
McClelland's needs are achievement, power and affiliation. They are not a hierarchy; one is dominant in each person. Do not put them in Maslow's order.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up expectancy and instrumentality"}
Expectancy is the belief that **effort leads to performance**. Instrumentality is the belief that **performance leads to reward**. Valence is how much the reward is **valued**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Applying the theories without the case"}
Naming the terms earns little. Show which belief is weak in the business described and what the manager can do about it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Content theories explain what motivates; process theories explain how people decide to make an effort.
- McClelland: needs for achievement, power and affiliation; one tends to dominate and suits different jobs.
- Vroom: motivation depends on expectancy (effort leads to performance), instrumentality (performance leads to reward) and valence (the reward is valued).
- If any one of Vroom's three beliefs is low, motivation is low.
- Limitations: needs are hard to measure; Vroom assumes rational calculation and is hard to apply to individuals.
- Use the theory to diagnose a problem in the case and to recommend a specific action.
:::
