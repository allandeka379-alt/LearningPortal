## Introduction

Farai owns a fictional garment factory in Bulawayo. His sewing machinists are paid a fixed weekly wage, and output is slow on Fridays. His sales staff are paid a salary, and some rarely visit new shops. He wonders whether paying by results would help, or whether it would simply lead to rushed work and complaints. Choosing how to pay people is one of the most powerful motivational decisions a business makes, and one of the easiest to get wrong.

Financial methods of motivation are tested in ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and in Cambridge AS Level Papers 1 and 2. Questions combine short calculations of pay with an explanation of which method suits a stated business. You should know what motivation means and the idea that money is one of several motivators (the theories are met in later lessons).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Time rate (wages)"}
Pay calculated as an amount for each hour worked, normally paid weekly. Hours beyond the normal week are often paid at a higher overtime rate.
:::

:::definition{term="Salary"}
A fixed annual amount, paid in equal monthly instalments, that does not depend on the hours worked.
:::

:::definition{term="Piece rate"}
Pay for each unit of output produced, so that earnings rise and fall with the number of units.
:::

:::definition{term="Commission"}
Pay calculated as a percentage of the value of sales made by the employee, often added to a basic pay.
:::

:::definition{term="Performance-related pay (PRP)"}
Extra pay linked to how well the employee, team or business meets agreed targets, usually judged in an appraisal.
:::

## The methods

| Method | How it works | Suits | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Time rate | Hourly rate × hours; overtime at a premium | Work where output is hard to measure or quality matters | Simple; predictable for workers; encourages care | No reward for extra effort; needs supervision |
| Salary | Fixed monthly amount | Managers, professionals, office staff | Secure and easy to budget | Unrelated to results; may not motivate extra effort |
| Piece rate | Rate × units produced | Repetitive manufacturing with measurable output | Rewards effort; little supervision needed | Quality may fall; unfair when machines break; unstable income |
| Commission | Percentage of sales | Sales staff | Strong incentive to sell; cost rises only when sales rise | Pressure on customers; unstable income; ignores after-sales service |
| Bonus | One-off payment for reaching a target | Teams and managers | Rewards achievement; can be targeted | Short-term focus; may be seen as part of pay |
| Profit sharing | Share of profit divided among employees | Any firm that wants loyalty | Links staff to the success of the business; encourages teamwork | Depends on factors the individual cannot control; payment delayed |
| Performance-related pay | Pay rises linked to appraisal | Skilled and managerial work | Rewards individual achievement | Appraisals may seem unfair; may harm teamwork |
| Fringe benefits | Non-cash benefits, such as a company vehicle, medical aid, school fees assistance, housing allowance | Staff hard to recruit or retain | Valued by staff; may be tax-efficient | Cost; value differs between individuals |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: calculating pay under different methods]
Farai's factory uses four methods in a week. All amounts are in US dollars.

**Time rate with overtime.** A machinist is paid US\$4.50 an hour for a 40-hour week, with overtime at one and a half times the rate. She works 46 hours.

| Item | Working | Pay (US\$) |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Normal hours | 40 × 4.50 | 180.00 |
| Overtime rate | 4.50 × 1.5 | 6.75 an hour |
| Overtime hours | 6 × 6.75 | 40.50 |
| **Total pay** | 180.00 + 40.50 | **220.50** |

**Piece rate.** A cutter is paid US\$0.80 a piece and cuts 260 pieces: 260 × 0.80 = US\$208.00.

**Basic pay plus commission.** A sales representative receives a basic US\$120 a week plus 4% commission on sales of US\$3,500: 4% of 3,500 = 140, so pay = 120 + 140 = US\$260.00.

**Profit sharing.** The firm shares 10% of its profit of US\$18,000 equally among 24 workers: 10% of 18,000 = 1,800; 1,800 ÷ 24 = US\$75 each.

Check that overtime was calculated only on the six hours beyond 40, not on all 46.
:::

## Why money does not always motivate

Money matters: people must be able to live, and unfair pay creates anger. But Herzberg saw pay as a hygiene factor: it prevents dissatisfaction but does not by itself create lasting motivation. McClelland, Maslow and others show that people also want achievement, recognition, belonging and interesting work. A pay system can also cause harm: piece rate can lead to poor quality; commission can lead to pushing customers; individual bonuses can damage teamwork. Financial methods work best when pay is seen as fair, targets are achievable and within the worker's control, and non-financial motivators are also present.

## Choosing a method

Ask four questions:

1. **Can output be measured?** If yes (units, sales), piece rate or commission is possible; if not (care work, teaching), use time rate, salary or PRP.
2. **Does quality matter more than quantity?** If so, avoid piece rate unless there is quality control.
3. **Is teamwork important?** If so, use team bonuses or profit sharing, not individual bonuses.
4. **How much can the business afford?** Variable pay (piece rate, commission, bonus) costs more only when output or sales rise, which protects the business in a poor year.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis of a pay system]
**Context.** Farai's sales representatives are on a fixed salary and some rarely visit new shops. He considers moving them to commission only, with no basic pay.

**Point.** Commission would raise effort but could cause problems if there is no basic pay.

**Application.** Representatives would earn more by selling more, so they would visit new shops. But if sales fall in a poor month, they would earn nothing and the best may leave for a rival with a basic wage.

**Analysis.** The cost to the factory would rise only with sales, which protects profit. However, representatives might push customers to buy more than they need, damaging the firm's reputation, and they would have no reason to spend time on after-sales service.

**Judgement.** A mixed system, with a modest basic pay plus a commission on sales to new customers, keeps the incentive and gives security. He should review the system after six months to see whether new customers increase.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Paying overtime on all hours"}
Overtime applies only to the hours above the normal week. Calculate normal pay and overtime pay separately and add them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that more pay always means more motivation"}
Evidence from Herzberg and others shows that pay alone does not guarantee motivation. Say when and why a method works for this business.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the effect on quality or teamwork"}
Piece rate may lower quality; individual bonuses may damage teamwork. Mention the possible side effects.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (calculate pay under piece rate, overtime or commission) and Paper 2 (essays on rewards); Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2; Cambridge Paper 1 (define, explain a method) and Paper 2 (calculate and assess a pay system in a data response).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline*: name the method and a feature. *Calculate*: show each stage, separating normal pay and overtime or basic and commission. *Explain/analyse*: link the method to effort, cost and quality in the case. *Discuss/evaluate/assess/advise*: weigh advantages against disadvantages for this business and recommend a method with a reason.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Time rate and salary pay for time; piece rate and commission pay for results; bonuses, profit sharing and PRP reward achievement; fringe benefits add non-cash value.
- Overtime is paid at a premium only for hours beyond the normal week.
- Piece rate suits repetitive measurable work; commission suits sales; salary suits managers and professionals.
- Variable pay links labour cost to output but can harm quality, teamwork and security.
- Money is a hygiene factor for Herzberg; lasting motivation also needs non-financial rewards.
- Choose a method by measurability of output, importance of quality and teamwork, and affordability.
:::
