## Introduction

Tatenda has saved US\$5,000 and wants to start a broiler (meat chicken) project in Gweru. A bank manager asks one question before discussing a loan: "Where is your business plan?" Tatenda's plan must show what he will sell, to whom, how he will run the project and how much money he needs, and it must convince a stranger that the project can repay a loan.

A business plan is examined as a written explanation (*state*, *describe* its purpose and content), as a calculation from its financial section (funding needed, break-even) and as an evaluation (*is a plan worth the effort for a small firm?*). It appears in ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 and Paper 2, Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 (where it is studied across Forms 5 and 6) and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2. You should know what a start-up is; break-even and cash flow forecasts are developed in later lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Business plan"}
A written document setting out a business's objectives, the products and market it will serve, how it will operate, who will manage it, and its expected finances, used to guide the owner and to persuade others to provide support.
:::

:::definition{term="Start-up capital"}
The money needed to buy the assets and meet the running costs of a business before it earns its first revenue.
:::

## Purpose of a business plan

- To **guide the owner** and force careful thinking before money is spent.
- To **raise finance**: lenders and investors expect a plan.
- To **set targets** against which actual performance can later be measured.
- To **reduce risk** by testing ideas on paper and spotting weaknesses early.

## Key elements

| Element | What it contains |
| --- | --- |
| Executive summary | A one-page summary of the whole plan, written last |
| Business description and objectives | What the business is, its legal form, aims and targets |
| Products | What is sold and why customers will buy it |
| Market analysis and marketing plan | Customers, competitors, size of market, prices, promotion, distribution |
| Operations and management plan | Premises, equipment, suppliers, production, who manages |
| Human resources | Staff needed, skills, pay and training |
| Financial plan | Start-up costs, funding required, cash flow forecast, expected profit, break-even |

## Who reads a plan and what they look for

| Reader | What they look for |
| --- | --- |
| Bank (lender) | Ability to repay, security, a realistic cash flow forecast |
| Investor | Return on investment, growth potential, the owner's own stake |
| Owner | A clear direction and targets |
| Suppliers | Evidence the business will pay on time before giving credit |
| Government or support agencies | Job creation and whether the business is legal and viable |

## Benefits and limitations

**Benefits:** helps to obtain finance; shows weaknesses before money is spent; gives targets for control; improves communication among partners and staff.

**Limitations:** takes time and may cost money to prepare; forecasts rely on guesses, especially for a new product; plans become out of date when conditions change; a good plan does not guarantee success; and it may be written to impress lenders rather than to be accurate.

## ZIMSEC detail: preparing and implementing a plan

A BES or Business Studies student should be able to outline the steps: (1) identify the opportunity and set the objectives; (2) research the market; (3) decide the product, price and promotion; (4) decide the operations and the people needed; (5) work out the costs, funding and expected profit; (6) write the plan and have it checked; (7) put it into action; (8) compare results with the plan and revise it. Each component is evaluated by asking whether the figures are realistic, whether the market evidence is sound and whether the plan can be carried out with the resources available.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the financial section of a plan]
Tatenda's plan for the broiler project lists the start-up costs below.

| Start-up item | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Poultry house | 4,500 |
| Equipment (feeders, drinkers, heaters) | 2,800 |
| First batch of 500 chicks and feed | 3,200 |
| Licences and registration | 150 |
| Working capital for the first two months | 1,350 |
| Total funding required | 12,000 |

1. Total funding required = 4,500 + 2,800 + 3,200 + 150 + 1,350 = US\$12,000.
2. Tatenda contributes US\$5,000, so the loan needed = 12,000 − 5,000 = US\$7,000.
3. Selling price per bird is US\$7.50 and variable cost per bird is US\$4.50, so contribution per bird = 7.50 − 4.50 = US\$3.00. Fixed costs are US\$900 a month.
4. Break-even output = 900 ÷ 3.00 = 300 birds a month.

| Output (birds) | Revenue (US\$) | Total cost (US\$) | Profit / (loss) (US\$) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 0 | 900 | (900) |
| 150 | 1,125 | 1,575 | (450) |
| 300 | 2,250 | 2,250 | 0 |
| 450 | 3,375 | 2,925 | 450 |
| 600 | 4,500 | 3,600 | 900 |

5. If planned sales are 450 birds a month, the margin of safety is 450 − 300 = 150 birds, which is 150 ÷ 450 × 100 = 33.3% of planned sales. A lender will see that sales can fall by a third before the project makes a loss.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, is a plan worth it?]
**Context.** Nyasha, a fictional owner in Chinhoyi, plans to open a small stall selling airtime and snacks. She has US\$800 of her own and wants to borrow US\$600 from a relative. A friend says she does not need a plan.

**Point.** A short plan would still be useful, but a long formal plan is probably not worth the cost.

**Application.** The relative will want to know how Nyasha will repay the US\$600; a one-page plan showing expected weekly sales, stock costs and repayments gives that evidence.

**Analysis.** For a small stall a simple cash forecast helps Nyasha to see whether she can repay and to spot cash shortages early. A formal 30-page document would take time and cost money, and its forecasts would still be guesses.

**Judgement.** She should prepare a short plan with a cash forecast, because the risk of the loan being unpaid justifies it. If she were not borrowing, a plan would still be valuable but less necessary.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing the headings without saying why"}
"Marketing plan, financial plan" earns little. Say what each section shows and who needs it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the reader"}
A bank wants to see security and repayment; an investor wants return. Match the content to the reader named in the question.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a plan as a guarantee"}
A plan is a forecast. Always note that it can be wrong and should be reviewed.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 (state and describe the content, prepare or evaluate a plan, calculate funding or break-even from the plan); Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (identify elements, explain purpose, assess whether a plan is useful).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/outline*: list the element. *Describe/explain*: say what the element contains and why it matters. *Discuss/assess/evaluate*: weigh the benefits against the limitations for the business in the case and reach a judgement, for example that the value of a plan is greater when borrowing.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A business plan guides the owner, helps raise finance, sets targets and reduces risk.
- Key elements are the executive summary, description and objectives, products, market and marketing plan, operations, human resources and the financial plan.
- Banks look for repayment ability, investors for return, suppliers for reliability.
- Limitations include cost, guesswork in forecasts and becoming out of date.
- ZIMSEC students also outline the steps for preparing, implementing and evaluating a plan.
:::
