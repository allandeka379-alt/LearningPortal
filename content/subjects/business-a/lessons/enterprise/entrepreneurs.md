## Introduction

Farai Ncube was a mechanic's assistant in Bulawayo when he noticed that most of the farmers who came to the workshop asked the same question: where could they get a tractor repaired without driving to Harare? Within two years he had borrowed from relatives, rented a yard and opened a mobile repair service. His old workshop, meanwhile, had a young supervisor, Sipho, who persuaded the owner to start a parts-delivery service inside the existing business.

Farai is an **entrepreneur**; Sipho is an **intrapreneur**. Both created something new, but in different settings. The topic is tested in Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS Level) and in ZIMSEC Business Enterprise Skills Form 5, where it is called *enterprising*. You need no previous knowledge beyond the idea of a business, but you should be ready to apply the qualities and barriers to a stated case.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Entrepreneur"}
A person who identifies an opportunity, organises resources and takes the risk of starting a new business, in the hope of reward.
:::

:::definition{term="Intrapreneur"}
An employee who acts like an entrepreneur inside an existing organisation, developing new ideas, products or processes with the employer's resources and backing.
:::

:::definition{term="Risk and uncertainty"}
**Risk** is a situation in which the possible outcomes can be foreseen and probabilities can be estimated (for example, the chance that a customer does not pay). **Uncertainty** is a situation in which outcomes and their likelihood cannot be known (for example, how customers will react to a completely new product).
:::

:::definition{term="Enterprising (ZIMSEC Business Enterprise Skills)"}
Showing initiative in identifying opportunities and turning them into income-earning activities. An *enterpriser* is a person who does this; an *enterprise* is the activity or business that results.
:::

## Entrepreneurs and intrapreneurs compared

| Feature | Entrepreneur | Intrapreneur |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where the activity happens | In a new business the person owns | Inside an existing firm that employs the person |
| Who provides finance | The entrepreneur and lenders | The employer |
| Who bears the risk | The entrepreneur (can lose savings) | Mainly the employer (the employee risks reputation and job) |
| Reward | Profit | Salary, bonus, promotion |
| Benefit to the economy | New businesses, jobs | Innovation in established firms |

## Qualities needed

- **Risk-taking**: willingness to commit money and time to an uncertain outcome.
- **Creativity and innovation**: seeing a need others miss and finding a new way to meet it.
- **Determination and resilience**: continuing after setbacks and failures.
- **Self-confidence**: belief in the idea, to persuade lenders, suppliers and customers.
- **Leadership**: recruiting, motivating and directing others.
- **Hard work and organisation**: starting a business often means long hours and many roles.

These qualities help, but they do not guarantee success: many failures lack finance, market knowledge or good timing.

## Barriers to entrepreneurship

- **Finance**: banks want security, and young entrepreneurs often have none.
- **Skills**: weak knowledge of costing, marketing or record keeping.
- **Regulation**: licences, registration and taxes can be slow or costly.
- **Market access**: established firms already hold customers, and transport to markets may be poor.
- **Fear of failure and weak family support** may deter people from trying.

## Role of enterprise in a country's development

Enterprise creates **jobs** and incomes, raises **output**, brings **innovation**, widens **competition** and increases **tax revenue**. Self-employed people also reduce dependence on formal employment. Against these gains, new businesses fail often, so the net gain depends on survival rates and on the quality of the jobs created.

## BES detail: the enterprising person

A BES student should describe an **enterprising person** (curious, spots opportunities, plans and acts) and a **culture of enterprising** (a community or school that rewards initiative and accepts mistakes). A successful enterpriser is organised, honest, willing to learn and able to manage money. Benefits include self-employment and income; drawbacks include uncertain income, long hours and the risk of losing savings.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: case analysis, entrepreneur or intrapreneur?]
**Context.** Sipho (a fictional supervisor) works in Farai's old workshop in Bulawayo. He designs a parts-delivery service, and the owner funds a van and a part-time driver.

**Point.** Sipho is acting as an intrapreneur, not an entrepreneur.

**Application.** He has developed a new idea inside an existing business, using the owner's money. The owner carries the financial risk; Sipho's reward is his salary and perhaps a bonus.

**Analysis.** This allows Sipho to try the idea without risking his own savings, and it gives the firm new income. But if the idea fails, Sipho gains no profit and might lose credibility; he also cannot keep the profits if it succeeds.

**Judgement.** Intrapreneurship suits people with ideas but little capital. If he wants to keep the full reward, he would have to become an entrepreneur and bear the risk himself.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, barriers and risk]
**Context.** Rudo, a fictional 24-year-old graduate in Mutare, wants to start mushroom farming. She has no savings, no experience of selling and finds that the bank asks for security.

**Point.** Rudo faces several barriers: finance, skills and market access.

**Application.** She lacks both capital and collateral, so a loan is unlikely. With no selling experience she may not find regular buyers.

**Analysis.** Mushroom prices and demand are only partly predictable, so some outcomes are risk (an estimated chance of a crop failure) and some are uncertainty (how a new market will respond). Without finance she can only start small. Small scale limits income but limits loss too.

**Judgement.** She should start with a small growing unit, supply a few known buyers and seek training and support, expanding only when income proves reliable.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying entrepreneurs are born, not made"}
Qualities can be learnt and developed. Do not argue that only some people can become entrepreneurs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up intrapreneurs and employees in general"}
An intrapreneur takes the initiative to create something new inside the firm. An ordinary employee who simply follows instructions is not an intrapreneur.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating risk and uncertainty as the same"}
Risk can be estimated and managed (for example with insurance); uncertainty cannot be measured. Say which applies in the case.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS Level): definitions, qualities in a case, barriers, and the role of entrepreneurs. ZIMSEC Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2: enterprising and the qualities of an enterpriser, usually as a structured question or as part of an essay.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Identify/state*: name the quality. *Explain*: link the quality to the case ("her resilience matters because the first harvests failed"). *Analyse*: build a chain of cause and effect. *Assess/evaluate/discuss*: give both sides and reach a judgement about how far enterprise helps development.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An entrepreneur starts a business and bears the risk; an intrapreneur innovates inside an existing firm using the employer's resources.
- Key qualities are risk-taking, creativity, determination, self-confidence and leadership.
- Barriers include finance, skills, regulation and market access.
- Risk can be estimated; uncertainty cannot.
- Enterprise creates jobs, output, innovation and tax revenue, but many new businesses fail.
:::
