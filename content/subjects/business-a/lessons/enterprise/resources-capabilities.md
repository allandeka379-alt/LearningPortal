## Introduction

Two bakeries in Gweru have the same oven. One of them regularly wins school and hotel contracts; the other struggles to hold on to customers. The difference is not the oven: the first has a baker whose recipes people remember, a reputation for delivering on time and good relationships with its flour suppliers. These things are harder to see and to copy than an oven, but they are what turns resources into success.

This lesson is for ZIMSEC Business Enterprise Skills (Forms 5 and 6) only. It is examined in Paper 1 (short structured parts and calculations comparing ways of acquiring an asset) and Paper 2 (essay questions on using resources efficiently and sustainably). Factors of production are covered in the lesson on business activity, and lease and hire purchase as sources of finance are in the finance lessons; you should know both before reading this one.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Resource"}
Anything an enterprise owns or controls that it uses to produce goods and services. Resources are **tangible** (physical, such as land, buildings, machines and stock) or **intangible** (not physical, such as a brand name, skills, a licence or goodwill).
:::

:::definition{term="Capability"}
What an enterprise is able to do by combining and using its resources well, for example producing high quality goods, delivering quickly or developing new products.
:::

:::definition{term="Core competence"}
A particular skill or strength, built up over time, that makes an enterprise better than rivals at something customers value and that is hard to copy.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Board note"}

:::

## Resources and capabilities compared

| Feature | Resource | Capability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | An item the enterprise has | An ability to do something |
| Examples | Delivery van, shed, brand, licence | Fast delivery, reliable quality, creative design |
| Can be bought? | Often yes | Not directly; it must be built through use and experience |
| Value | Little by itself | Arises when resources are used well together |

An enterprise needs **both**: resources without capabilities are idle, and capabilities without resources cannot be put to work.

## Acquiring resources

| Method | How it works | Suits | Drawbacks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hire | Pay a fee to use an asset for a period; the owner remains the hirer | Short-term or occasional use; little capital | Costly if used for a long time; nothing owned at the end |
| Purchase | Pay the price and own the asset | Long-term, frequent use | Large initial cost; ties up capital; owner must maintain it |
| Build-own-transfer (BOT) | A developer builds a facility, owns it and earns income from it for an agreed period, then transfers ownership to the client or government | Large projects, such as a road or power plant, where the client lacks funds now | Long agreements; the client pays over time through fees |
| Build-own-operate-transfer (BOOT) | As above, but the developer also operates the facility throughout the period and is paid from its income | Projects where operating skill matters | Profit sharing and control disputes if terms are unclear |

Usage of the labels varies; follow the definition given in the question.

## Efficient and sustainable use of resources

- **Avoid waste**: plan quantities, reuse offcuts, store stock safely.
- **Maintain** machines and vehicles so they last and do not break down.
- **Share** equipment with other enterprises when each uses it only part of the time.
- **Reuse and recycle** materials such as containers, water and packaging.
- **Sustainable** use means not using resources faster than they renew (for example, cutting only as much timber as is replanted), so the enterprise can operate for the long term.

## Core competences in outline

A competence is *core* when it (1) gives real value to customers, (2) is hard for rivals to copy and (3) can be used in more than one product or market. A mushroom grower whose quality control is far better than that of competitors has a core competence in quality that she could use to sell dried mushrooms and sauces as well.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: hire or purchase?]
A fictional builder in Gweru needs a concrete mixer. Purchase costs US\$6,000 plus US\$300 a year for upkeep. Hiring costs US\$1,400 a year. The mixer would last at least eight years.

| Years of use | Purchase: price + upkeep (US\$) | Hire (US\$) | Cheaper |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 3 years | 6,900 | 4,200 | Hire |
| 5 years | 7,500 | 7,000 | Hire |
| 8 years | 8,400 | 11,200 | Purchase |

1. Purchase cost over n years = 6,000 + 300n; hire cost = 1,400n.
2. The two are equal when 6,000 = (1,400 − 300)n, so n = 6,000 ÷ 1,100 = 5.45 years.
3. For fewer than about five and a half years of use, hiring is cheaper; for longer use, buying is cheaper.
4. Recommendation: if the builder expects to use the mixer for most of every year for eight years, she should buy. If the work is occasional or uncertain, she should hire, since this also avoids tying up capital she may need for materials.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, resources and capabilities]
**Context.** Rudo, a fictional enterpriser in Masvingo, makes and sells packaged peanut butter. She owns a grinder and rents a room. Her jars sell well because customers trust the taste and she always delivers on Friday as promised. A new rival with a larger grinder cannot match her reputation.

**Point.** Rudo's success depends on her capabilities more than on her resources.

**Application.** Her grinder and room are tangible resources which a rival can copy or buy. Her reliable delivery and her reputation for taste are capabilities and intangible resources built over time.

**Analysis.** Because a rival can buy a bigger grinder but cannot instantly create customer trust, her capability gives her a lasting advantage. However, she cannot meet a large order without more resources, so capabilities alone limit growth.

**Judgement.** She should keep her quality and delivery strengths, and acquire more equipment by hiring first, buying only when demand proves steady.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling a capability a resource"}
A grinder is a resource. The ability to produce consistent, high-quality peanut butter with it is a capability. Say which one the question describes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying hire is always cheaper"}
Hire is cheaper for short use but more expensive for long use. Compare the costs over the expected period.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing sustainable with cheap"}
Sustainable use means resources last for the future. It may cost more now, for example replanting trees.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Resources are tangible (land, machines, stock) or intangible (brand, skills, licences); capabilities are what the enterprise can do with them.
- Hire suits short-term use; purchase suits long-term use; BOT and BOOT arrangements suit large projects.
- Compare hire and purchase over the expected years of use before deciding.
- Efficient use avoids waste, maintains equipment, shares and reuses; sustainable use protects future supply.
- A core competence is valuable, hard to copy and usable in several markets.
:::
