## Introduction

Every morning, long before most customers wake, a bakery in Mutare mixes dough, lights its ovens and loads loaves onto a van. By eight o'clock the bread is on shop shelves across town. The owner has taken flour, yeast, packaging, electricity, machines and the skills of a dozen workers and turned them into something customers want to buy for more than the inputs cost. That is business activity in one picture.

This lesson is the starting point for the whole course. In the exams it appears as short definitions (*state*, *define*), as calculations of value added, and as application questions that ask you to explain why a business makes a particular choice. ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 and Paper 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 all draw on it. You need no earlier business knowledge; the lessons on sectors and on ownership build on what follows.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Business activity"}
The organised use of resources to produce goods or services that satisfy customers' needs and wants, normally in the hope of earning a profit or meeting another objective.
:::

:::definition{term="Added value"}
The difference between the selling price of a product and the cost of the bought-in materials and services used to make it. It is the amount the business itself contributes.
:::

:::definition{term="Opportunity cost"}
The value of the next best alternative that is given up when a choice is made.
:::

:::definition{term="Scarcity"}
The situation in which resources are limited while human wants are unlimited, so that choices must be made.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Added value"}
Added value per unit = selling price per unit − cost of bought-in inputs per unit.
Total added value = added value per unit × units sold. Added value is not profit: wages, rent and other running costs are still to be paid out of it.
:::

## Why businesses exist

Businesses exist to meet **needs** (things required for survival, such as food and shelter) and **wants** (things people would like, such as a smartphone). Doing so creates employment, incomes and tax revenue and may lead to growth. Owners may also aim for profit, survival, social goals or independence; objectives are studied in a later lesson.

## Factors of production and their rewards

A business combines four resources, the **factors of production**.

| Factor | What it is | Example in a poultry project | Reward |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Land | Natural resources and the site | The farm plot, water, soil | Rent |
| Labour | Physical and mental effort of workers | Feeders, a vet, the manager | Wages and salaries |
| Capital | Man-made assets used to produce | Cages, incubators, a delivery truck | Interest |
| Enterprise | Organising the other factors and bearing risk | The owner who starts and runs the project | Profit |

Do not confuse **capital** as a factor (equipment) with capital as money invested in the business; examiners expect the factor meaning here.

## Adding value

A business adds value when it makes a product more valuable than the inputs it bought. Ways to raise added value:

- **Lower the cost of bought-in inputs** (negotiate better prices or buy in bulk) while keeping quality.
- **Raise the selling price** by improving quality, design, branding, packaging or service.
- **Process the product further**, for example turning raw groundnuts into packaged peanut butter.
- **Improve convenience**, such as home delivery or longer opening hours.

Higher added value does not guarantee profit: if the extra price needs heavy advertising or costly packaging, running costs may rise faster than added value.

## Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost

Because resources are scarce, every business must choose: which product, which market, how to spend limited funds. Each choice has an opportunity cost, measured in the best alternative lost, which may be money, time or another project. Opportunity cost is not the sum of all the alternatives given up, only the *next best* one.

## The dynamic business environment

The conditions in which a business operates change constantly: tastes shift, rivals appear, technology improves, laws and prices change. Change is a source of **opportunity** (a new product, a new market) and of **threat** (a falling market or a new competitor). Successful businesses scan the environment and adapt; those that do not often fail. The detailed study of the environment comes in later lessons.

## Local, national, international and multinational business

A **local** business serves one area (a tuck shop in Chitungwiza). A **national** business sells across a country. An **international** business trades across borders (exports or imports). A **multinational** business owns operations in more than one country. As a business serves a wider area it gains larger markets and scale but meets stronger competition, different laws and greater management complexity.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: added value and opportunity cost]
Rudo runs a small bakery in Mutare. A loaf sells for US\$1.20. Bought-in inputs (flour, yeast, packaging, fuel for ovens) cost US\$0.70 per loaf. She bakes and sells 500 loaves a day.

1. Added value per loaf = 1.20 − 0.70 = US\$0.50.
2. Total added value per day = 0.50 × 500 = US\$250.
3. By changing to a branded wrapper, Rudo can sell at US\$1.40 while inputs rise to US\$0.75. Added value per loaf = 1.40 − 0.75 = US\$0.65, so total = 0.65 × 500 = US\$325 a day, an increase of US\$75.

Rudo also has US\$4,000 to spend. A second oven would raise annual profit by US\$900. A delivery motorbike would raise it by US\$700. A fixed deposit would earn US\$300.

4. She chooses the oven (the best option). The opportunity cost is the next best alternative, the motorbike's US\$700 a year, not US\$700 + US\$300.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, a business facing change]
**Context.** Tatenda's Tyres, a shop in Gweru, sells second-hand tyres. A new bypass road is opening, and customers are turning to cheaper imported tyres sold online.

**Point.** The business environment is changing, creating both a threat and an opportunity.

**Application.** The bypass moves traffic away from the shop (threat); cheaper imports reduce demand for local second-hand tyres (threat). But the bypass will create a busy truck route where a fitting centre could be placed (opportunity).

**Analysis.** If Tatenda continues to rely on passing trade, revenue will fall, and fixed costs such as rent will stay the same, squeezing profit. A new fitting and balancing service adds value to the tyre, since customers pay for fitting, and is harder for online sellers to copy.

**Judgement.** He should move towards a service-led business near the bypass, provided that rent and start-up cost allow it; if finance is short, he should first add fitting services at the present shop, since that is cheaper and less risky.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing added value with profit"}
Added value ignores wages, rent and other overheads. A business can have positive added value and still make a loss.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing every alternative as the opportunity cost"}
Only the single next best alternative counts. Name it, with its value if the data allows.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating capital as cash only"}
As a factor of production, capital means man-made assets such as machinery and vehicles. Its reward is interest.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers test this"}
ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 test definitions and short structured parts; Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 test added value and opportunity cost in context. Business Enterprise Skills reaches the idea through enterprise lessons rather than this topic.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/define*: one precise sentence. *Calculate*: formula, substitution, answer. *Explain/describe*: point plus reason, with the case named. *Discuss/evaluate*: give both sides of a business's choice and end with a justified judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Businesses combine land, labour, capital and enterprise to meet needs and wants.
- Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in inputs; it is not profit.
- Scarcity forces choice; the opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up.
- Change in the business environment creates both opportunities and threats.
- Business may be local, national, international or multinational, with wider scale bringing both gains and complexity.
:::
