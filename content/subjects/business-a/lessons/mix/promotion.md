## Introduction

Sipho owns a fictional paint business in Bulawayo. His paint is good, his price is fair and his shops are well placed, but few homeowners know the brand. A customer cannot buy a product she has never heard of. **Promotion** is the part of the marketing mix that informs, persuades and reminds customers. It is also one of the largest items of spending in many businesses, so a manager must be able to explain *which* methods to use, *how much* to spend, *through which media*, and *whether it worked*.

This lesson serves ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 6) and Cambridge **AS** (Papers 1 and 2). You should already know the product, price and place elements of the mix and how a market is segmented. Digital promotion is in a separate lesson, and only linked here.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Promotion"}
All the methods by which a business communicates with customers to inform them, persuade them to buy and remind them of its products.
:::

:::definition{term="Promotion mix"}
The combination of promotion methods that a business uses for a product at a given time.
:::

:::definition{term="Branding"}
Giving a product a name, symbol or design that makes it recognisable and different from rivals' products, so that customers can identify it and become loyal to it.
:::

:::definition{term="Promotional budget"}
The amount a business sets aside to spend on promotion over a given period.
:::

## Objectives of promotion

To create **awareness**, to give information, to persuade customers to switch to the product, to increase sales and market share, to build a brand image, to remind existing customers and to support a new launch or an extension strategy.

## Promotion methods

| Method | What it is | Useful when | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Advertising | Paid messages in media (radio, newspapers, posters, television, online) | Reaching many customers; building awareness | Costly; difficult to measure; short-lived |
| Sales promotion | Short-term offers: discounts, coupons, buy-one-get-one-free, free samples, competitions | Launches, clearing stock, quick sales boosts | Can reduce profit and train buyers to wait for offers |
| Direct promotion (direct marketing) | Messages sent straight to chosen customers (letters, calls, messages, email) | A defined group, a customer database | Annoying if unwanted; needs good data |
| Personal selling | A salesperson talks to the customer | Expensive or technical products, business customers | High cost per customer |
| Public relations and publicity | Press releases, events, good relations with the community; publicity is coverage the firm did not pay for | Building trust; low cost | The firm does not control what is said |
| Sponsorship | Paying to link the brand with an event, team or person | Brand image with a target audience | The cost may be high; benefit is hard to measure |

**Packaging and branding.** Packaging protects the product, but it is also promotion: it attracts attention on the shelf, carries information and gives an image of quality. A strong brand supports a higher price and repeat purchases, but the cost of building it is high and a bad event can damage it.

## Choosing the promotion mix

A suitable mix depends on:

- the **product** (a technical machine needs personal selling; low-cost goods need advertising and sales promotion);
- the **market** (size, location and media habits of customers);
- the **budget**;
- the **stage in the life cycle** (launch needs awareness; decline needs less spending);
- the **objective** (awareness, sales, image);
- **rivals' promotion**.

## Promotional budget

Common methods:

- **Percentage of sales** (a fixed percentage of past or expected sales): simple, but spending falls when sales fall, just when promotion may be needed.
- **Objective and task**: decide what must be achieved, cost the actions needed and add them up. Linked to a goal, but needs good estimates.
- **Competitive parity**: spend as much as rivals. Ensures a presence, but ignores the firm's own needs.
- **Affordable**: spend what is left after other needs. Easy, but treats promotion as an expense, not an investment.

Factors include the objective, the size and profit of the firm, rivals' spending, the product's stage in its life cycle and the cost of media.

## Media and agencies

Media are chosen by **reach** (how many people), **cost per person reached**, **suitability** for the target market (radio and posters suit customers who may not use the internet; online suits young urban customers), the type of message (a picture, a demonstration) and the time available. A **promotional agency** designs and places campaigns using expert staff; it costs fees but brings skill and contacts. A small firm may do its own promotion to save cost.

## Measuring the effect of a campaign (ZIMSEC detail; useful for all)

Compare sales **before, during and after** the campaign, allowing for other causes (season, price changes, rivals). Useful measures: extra sales, extra contribution compared with the cost, cost per extra unit sold, number of coupons used or enquiries received, and changes in awareness found by a survey.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: budget and the effect of a campaign]
**Context.** Sipho expects yearly sales of US\$240,000 and sets a promotional budget of 3% of sales. He also runs a month-long discount campaign on a paint tin that sells at US\$8 and has a variable cost of US\$5. The campaign costs US\$2,400.

| Item | Figure |
| --- | ---: |
| Average monthly sales before the campaign (units) | 4,000 |
| Sales in the campaign month (units) | 5,200 |
| Extra units sold | 1,200 |
| Contribution per unit (US\$) | 3 |
| Extra contribution (US\$) | 3,600 |
| Cost of campaign (US\$) | 2,400 |
| **Net gain (US\$)** | 1,200 |
| Cost per extra unit sold (US\$) | 2 |

1. Budget = 3% × 240,000 = US\$7,200 for the year.
2. Extra units = 5,200 − 4,000 = 1,200, an increase of 30%.
3. Contribution per unit = 8 − 5 = US\$3, so extra contribution = 1,200 × 3 = US\$3,600.
4. Net gain = 3,600 − 2,400 = US\$1,200; the cost per extra unit sold is 2,400 ÷ 1,200 = US\$2.
5. **Limits.** The month may have been busier for other reasons, and some buyers may only have bought sooner. Sipho should compare with the months after the campaign before deciding that it worked.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, a mix for a lodge]
**Context.** Matetsi View is a fictional lodge near Victoria Falls. It wants to attract overseas visitors and Zimbabwean families, with a budget that is small compared with the large hotels.

**Point.** A small budget means the lodge must pick methods that reach its target customers cheaply and use word of mouth.

**Application.** A website with good photographs, contact with tour operators (personal selling to business customers), a press release when a rare bird is seen on the grounds (publicity), and discounted midweek rates for Zimbabwean residents (sales promotion) suit the two groups. Television advertising would be too costly.

**Analysis.** Tour operators bring groups, so a few sales visits reach many visitors for a low cost per guest. Publicity costs little and has high credibility, but the lodge cannot control what is written. A midweek discount fills empty rooms, but if it is offered too often visitors may refuse to pay full price.

**Judgement.** Because the target markets differ, the lodge should use at least two methods, one for each group. It should measure the results (bookings by source) after one season and move spending towards the method with the lowest cost per booking.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing methods without choosing"}
A question about a stated business wants a **reasoned choice**. Name the method, say why it suits the product, market and budget, and state a limit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every sales promotion 'advertising'"}
Advertising is paid communication through media. Discounts, coupons and free samples are **sales promotion**; a news story the firm did not pay for is **publicity**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Taking the extra sales as profit"}
Extra revenue is not extra profit. Subtract the variable cost of the extra units and then the cost of the campaign.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Business Studies Papers 1 and 2 and Business Enterprise Skills papers ask you to *state* or *outline* promotion methods, *describe* media and *discuss* a campaign. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 use *explain*, *analyse* and *assess*. Always tie the method to the **product, market and budget** in the case. For an evaluation, give a limit (cost, measurement, rivals' reaction) and a judgement that depends on a condition such as "if the campaign brings extra contribution greater than its cost".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Promotion informs, persuades and reminds; the main methods are advertising, sales promotion, direct marketing, personal selling, public relations and sponsorship.
- Packaging and branding support promotion by giving the product identity and image.
- The mix depends on the product, market, budget, stage of the life cycle, objective and rivals.
- Budgets can be set as a percentage of sales, by objective and task, by competitive parity or by affordability.
- A campaign is judged by extra sales and extra contribution compared with its cost, after allowing for other causes.
:::
