## Introduction

Mhofu Foods is a fictional food manufacturer in Gweru. It sells maize meal that families have bought for years, a chilli sauce that is selling faster each month, a peanut butter that struggles against stronger rivals and a dried-fruit pack that few people buy. The owner cannot give every product the same money and attention. Two tools help her decide: the **product life cycle**, which asks *where in its life is each product?*, and the **Boston Matrix**, which asks *which products deserve investment, and which should be milked or dropped?*

This lesson serves ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 6) and Cambridge **AS** (Papers 1 and 2). Neither board expects you to draw diagrams in the exam: here the life cycle and the matrix are shown as **tables**, which is also how data appear in questions. You should already know what a market, market share and a product are. The Ansoff matrix is a separate strategy lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Product life cycle"}
The pattern of sales that a product follows from launch to withdrawal, usually divided into development, introduction, growth, maturity and decline.
:::

:::definition{term="Extension strategy"}
An action taken to lengthen the life of a product, usually when sales growth is slowing or sales have begun to fall.
:::

:::definition{term="Product portfolio"}
All the products that a business sells. **Portfolio analysis** examines the mix of products to decide where to invest and what to drop.
:::

:::definition{term="Relative market share"}
A product's sales divided by the sales of the largest competing product. A value above 1 means the product leads its market.
:::

## The product life cycle

| Stage | Sales | Profit and cash flow | Typical marketing response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Development | None | Negative: research and design cost money | Market research, testing, prototypes |
| Introduction | Low, growing slowly | Losses likely: high promotion and set-up costs | Awareness campaigns, introductory prices or skimming |
| Growth | Rising fast | Profit appears; cash may still be tight because stock and capacity must expand | Build the brand, widen distribution, add variants |
| Maturity | Highest, levelling out | Highest profit and strongest cash flow | Defend share, keep promotion steady, use extension strategies |
| Decline | Falling | Profit shrinks and may turn to loss | Cut costs, reduce promotion, or withdraw the product |

Remember: the cycle describes *typical* patterns. Fashion goods may rise and fall in months, a staple such as maize meal may stay mature for decades, and many products fail during introduction and never reach growth.

### Extension strategies

When sales level off or fall a business can try to lengthen the life of the product by:

- finding **new markets or uses** (selling the sauce in a neighbouring country; marketing a drink as a cooking ingredient);
- **modifying the product** (new flavour, new pack size, better quality);
- **repositioning** the brand and changing the promotion;
- **cutting the price** or offering sales promotions;
- **widening distribution** (new outlets, online sales).

An extension strategy costs money, so the question is whether the extra sales will repay the cost.

## Product portfolio analysis: the Boston Matrix

The Boston Consulting Group matrix classifies each product by two measures: **market growth** (how fast the whole market is growing) and **relative market share** (how strong the product is compared with the leading rival). In examples we assume high growth means 10% a year or more and a high relative share means above 1.0; a question will state its own thresholds.

| | High market share | Low market share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High market growth** | **Star**: invest to keep the lead | **Question mark** (problem child): invest to build share, or drop it |
| **Low market growth** | **Cash cow**: milk the cash, spend little | **Dog**: sell, close or keep only if it supports other products |

- **Stars** earn well but need heavy spending to keep up with growth. Later, when growth slows, many become cash cows.
- **Cash cows** earn more cash than they need. The surplus pays for stars and question marks.
- **Question marks** use cash and earn little. The business must decide whether they can become stars.
- **Dogs** earn little and have no growth. They are candidates for withdrawal, but a dog may still be kept if it brings customers to other products.

**Impact on decisions.** The matrix helps a business to balance cash: a portfolio of only cash cows has no future, and one of only question marks and stars has no cash. It helps to set priorities for research, promotion and investment.

**Limitations.** Market share is not the only source of profit; the matrix ignores links between products; the cut-off values are arbitrary; data may be out of date or hard to obtain; and a "dog" label may hide a profitable niche. It does not tell a business *what* new product to make. The life cycle likewise cannot predict the length of each stage, and managers' own actions (promotion, price) change the curve.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: classifying a portfolio]
**Context.** Mhofu Foods sells four products. The largest rival in each market is shown. A high relative share is above 1.0 and high growth is 10% a year or more.

| Product | Sales (US\$) | Largest rival (US\$) | Relative share | Market growth | Category |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Maize meal | 480,000 | 300,000 | 1.60 | 2% | Cash cow |
| Peanut butter | 210,000 | 350,000 | 0.60 | 14% | Question mark |
| Chilli sauce | 300,000 | 250,000 | 1.20 | 12% | Star |
| Dried fruit | 60,000 | 240,000 | 0.25 | 3% | Dog |

1. Relative share = firm's sales ÷ largest rival's sales: for maize meal 480,000 ÷ 300,000 = 1.60.
2. Maize meal: high share, low growth, so a cash cow. Peanut butter: low share (0.60) in a fast-growing market, so a question mark. Chilli sauce: high share and high growth, a star. Dried fruit: low on both, a dog.
3. **Recommendation.** Use the cash from maize meal to fund the chilli sauce's growth and a decision on peanut butter. Peanut butter needs a clear test: if extra promotion cannot raise its share towards the leader's, the money may be better spent on the star. Dried fruit is a candidate for withdrawal unless it draws customers to the other products.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, a solar lamp]
**Context.** Tsitsi Solar, a fictional business in Mutare, launched a rechargeable lamp. Yearly sales were as follows.

| Year | Sales (units) | Change on previous year |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Year 1 | 2,000 | - |
| Year 2 | 6,500 | +225.0% |
| Year 3 | 14,000 | +115.4% |
| Year 4 | 22,000 | +57.1% |
| Year 5 | 24,000 | +9.1% |
| Year 6 | 23,500 | -2.1% |
| Year 7 | 19,000 | -19.1% |
| Year 8 | 12,000 | -36.8% |

**Point.** Years 1 to 4 show introduction and growth; year 5 is maturity; years 6 to 8 are decline.

**Application.** Growth slowed from 57.1% to 9.1% between years 4 and 5, and sales fell in year 6. Sales in year 8 are half those of year 5.

**Analysis.** Slowing growth is the warning sign. Competitors have probably entered and the market is filling up. If Tsitsi waits until year 7, a larger fall has already occurred. An extension strategy at the start of year 6 (a new, brighter model or a lower price on a phone-charging version) could slow the fall.

**Judgement.** Because the cost of a new model is high, Tsitsi should first test it on a small batch. If sales of the new model do not recover, the product should be withdrawn and the funds moved to a new product.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using market share instead of relative market share"}
A product with 30% of the market could still be a dog if the leader has 60%. In the Boston Matrix the share is **relative to the largest rival**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that every product follows the textbook curve"}
The stages are typical, not certain. Some products fail at launch, others stay mature for a very long time, and promotion can change the curve. Say so in any evaluation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Recommending that all dogs be dropped"}
A dog may be kept if it brings customers who also buy the stars or if it still earns a small profit. Judge each case using the information given.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Business Studies Paper 1 (structured) and Paper 2 (data response, essays) and Business Enterprise Skills papers ask you to *state* the stages, *describe* extension strategies and *discuss* how the matrix helps a business. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 use *identify*, *explain*, *analyse* and *assess*. Classify with the figures given, then say what the business should **do** with each product. For an evaluation, give a limitation (arbitrary cut-offs, ignores links between products) and a condition ("this depends on whether extra promotion can raise the question mark's share").
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The product life cycle has development, introduction, growth, maturity and decline; profit and cash flow change at each stage.
- Extension strategies (new markets, modified product, repositioning, price, wider distribution) lengthen the life of a product, at a cost.
- The Boston Matrix classifies products by market growth and relative market share as stars, cash cows, question marks (problem children) and dogs.
- Cash cows fund stars and question marks; dogs are candidates for withdrawal.
- Both models help to balance cash and set priorities, but they simplify: cut-offs are arbitrary and links between products are ignored.
:::
