## Introduction

Gweru Nut Company is a fictional business that makes peanut butter. The product is good and the price is fair, but a customer in Mutare or Victoria Falls cannot buy it unless it reaches a shop near her. Choosing **how the product travels from the factory to the customer** is the **place** decision of the marketing mix. Each stage that handles the product takes a share of the final price, but it also saves the producer time, money and effort.

This lesson serves ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 6) and Cambridge **AS** (Papers 1 and 2). You should know the other three Ps and the idea of a mark-up. Digital distribution is covered in more detail in the lesson on digital marketing.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Channel of distribution"}
The route by which a product passes from the producer to the final customer, including any intermediaries.
:::

:::definition{term="Intermediary"}
A business or person between the producer and the final customer, such as a wholesaler, retailer or agent.
:::

:::definition{term="Wholesaler"}
A business that buys goods in large quantities from producers, stores them (breaks bulk) and sells smaller quantities to retailers.
:::

:::definition{term="Agent"}
A person or business that sells on behalf of a producer for a commission, without taking ownership of the goods.
:::

## The main channels

| Channel | Chain | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct to consumer | Producer → consumer | Own shop, stall, delivery or website; the producer keeps the whole price and controls the message, but carries all the cost and effort |
| Retailer | Producer → retailer → consumer | Retailers display products near customers; the producer sells in bulk to fewer customers |
| Wholesaler | Producer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer | Wholesalers store goods and serve many small shops; the producer's selling costs are low, but the chain adds mark-ups |
| Agent | Producer → agent → retailer or consumer | Agents have local knowledge and contacts and are paid by commission; the producer has less control |
| E-commerce | Producer → customer (through a website or platform) | Wide reach, low shop cost; delivery and trust are the main problems |

Each intermediary adds a **mark-up**, so the customer's price rises with the length of the chain. A shorter chain may give a lower price for the customer, or a higher profit for the producer, but only if the producer can do the work that the intermediary did.

## Factors in the choice of channel

- **The product.** Perishable goods (fresh vegetables, milk) need short channels; durable, mass-produced goods can use long channels. Technical or high-value goods may need direct sales or agents who can explain them.
- **The market.** Many customers spread over a wide area suggest wholesalers and retailers; a few large customers suggest direct sales.
- **Cost.** The cost of delivery, storage, staff and sales effort compared with the margin given to intermediaries.
- **Control.** Direct channels give control over price, display, service and brand image.
- **Competitors.** If rivals use certain retailers, a firm may need to be in the same shops.
- **Location and infrastructure.** Roads, transport, electricity for cold storage and internet access affect which channels work.

## Physical distribution (logistics) in outline

Physical distribution covers transport, storage and handling: choosing road, rail, air or courier; holding stock in warehouses; packing; and delivering on time at a reasonable cost. A good system reduces damage and delay and keeps shelves stocked. Digital products (software, online courses) can be distributed electronically and need no warehouse.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: price build-up and a direct-sales decision]
**Context.** Gweru Nut Company sells a jar of peanut butter to a wholesaler at US\$2.00. The wholesaler adds a mark-up of 20% and the retailer adds a mark-up of 30%.

| Stage | Buys at (US\$) | Mark-up | Sells at (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Manufacturer | - | - | 2.00 |
| Wholesaler | 2.00 | 20% | 2.40 |
| Retailer | 2.40 | 30% | 3.12 |

1. Wholesaler's price = 2.00 × 1.20 = US\$2.40. Retailer's price = 2.40 × 1.30 = US\$3.12.
2. The consumer pays US\$3.12 for a jar that left the factory at US\$2.00.
3. **Direct sales.** The company considers selling to shops itself at US\$2.60 a jar, using its own van and a salesperson. Delivery costs US\$0.35 a jar, and the salesperson and van cost an extra US\$1,400 a month. Sales are 8,000 jars a month.
4. Net price per jar = 2.60 − 0.35 = US\$2.25, which is US\$0.25 more than the US\$2.00 from the wholesaler. Extra income = 0.25 × 8,000 = US\$2,000 a month.
5. Net gain = 2,000 − 1,400 = US\$600 a month.
6. **Interpretation.** The gain is small. If the shops buy less than 8,000 jars the gain disappears, because the fixed cost of US\$1,400 would be spread over fewer jars (the break-even is 1,400 ÷ 0.25 = 5,600 jars). The company should consider the risk, the unpaid accounts that shops may leave, and the loss of the wholesaler's wide reach.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, a mushroom grower]
**Context.** Rudo grows oyster mushrooms in Chinhoyi. They must be sold within three days. She supplies a supermarket in Harare, a few restaurants and local market traders. A wholesaler offers to buy her entire crop at a fixed price.

**Point.** Mushrooms are perishable and are produced in small quantities, so the channel must be short and quick.

**Application.** Selling the whole crop to the wholesaler would save time and give a guaranteed buyer, but the wholesaler would take a margin and a longer chain adds delay for a product that spoils in three days. Direct sales to restaurants give a higher price and fresh delivery, but need time for orders and deliveries.

**Analysis.** A mix of channels spreads the risk: restaurants pay more and want freshness; the market traders take surplus; the wholesaler can be a back-up when there is a bumper crop. Control over freshness protects the quality of the brand.

**Judgement.** Rudo should sell to restaurants and the supermarket directly where she can deliver within a day, and use the wholesaler only for surplus. The decision depends on how much her time and delivery cost, and on whether the wholesaler can guarantee a fast chain.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that a shorter chain is always better"}
A shorter chain can give a higher profit or a lower price, but the producer then takes on the work, cost and risk of the missing intermediary. Compare the extra income with the extra cost.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up wholesalers, retailers and agents"}
A wholesaler buys in bulk and sells to retailers, a retailer sells to final customers, and an agent sells for a commission **without owning** the goods.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the product"}
The choice of channel must fit the product: perishable and technical goods need different channels from durable, mass-produced goods.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *state* and *describe* channels and to *discuss* the most suitable one for a stated product. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 use *identify*, *explain*, *analyse* and *assess*. Name the channel, then link it to the product, market and cost in the case. For an evaluation, weigh a benefit (lower cost, control) against a limitation (effort, risk, loss of reach) and make your judgement depend on a condition, such as "if the firm can deliver within the time that the product stays fresh".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A channel of distribution is the route from producer to final customer; the main channels are direct, retailer, wholesaler, agent and e-commerce.
- Each intermediary adds a mark-up, so longer chains raise the customer's price but save the producer work and cost.
- The choice depends on the product, market, cost, control, competitors and location and infrastructure.
- Physical distribution covers transport, storage and handling; digital goods can be distributed electronically.
- A direct channel is better only if the extra income exceeds the extra cost and risk.
:::
