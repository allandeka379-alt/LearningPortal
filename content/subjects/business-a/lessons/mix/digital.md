## Introduction

Thandeka runs a fictional business in Harare that sells hand-woven baskets. For years she sold at roadside stalls and craft markets. Then a visitor photographed a basket, posted it online, and within a week orders arrived from three countries. Thandeka now has a website, a social media page and a mobile payment option. She also faces new problems: parcels arrive late, a customer says the colour in the photograph was wrong, and her page was copied by a stranger. Digital methods change how a business communicates, sells, delivers and collects information, and they bring both opportunities and risks.

This lesson applies to ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 6) and Cambridge **AS** (Papers 1 and 2). You should know the main promotion methods and channels of distribution. The wider role of ICT in a firm is a separate lesson; here the focus is **marketing and selling**.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |
| ICTs in marketing; e-business; e-commerce | Developments in digital communications; digital and physical distribution; e-commerce |

The content is the same for both boards. Cambridge expects you to judge the effect on a stated business.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Digital marketing"}
Promoting products using electronic devices and the internet, such as websites, social media, search, email and mobile messages.
:::

:::definition{term="E-commerce"}
The buying and selling of goods and services over electronic networks, especially the internet.
:::

:::definition{term="E-business"}
All business activities carried out using electronic networks, including buying from suppliers, managing orders and customer service, not only selling.
:::

:::definition{term="Conversion rate"}
The percentage of visitors to a website (or recipients of a message) who go on to make a purchase.
:::

## Digital promotion

| Method | How it is used | Benefit | Risk or limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Website | Shows products, prices and contact details; may take orders | Open all day; low cost per customer reached | Needs design and updating; useless if no one finds it |
| Social media | Posts, photographs, adverts and conversations | Cheap, can target groups, customers can share | Needs constant attention; complaints are public |
| Search marketing | Paid adverts or work to appear high in search results | Reaches people who are looking to buy | Cost per click can rise; depends on search companies |
| Email and mobile messages | Direct messages to a customer list | Low cost, personal, easy to measure | Unwanted messages annoy; needs consent and data |
| Influencers | A well-known person recommends the product | Trusted by their followers | Cost; loss of image if the person behaves badly |

## E-commerce business models

| Model | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B2C (business to consumer) | A business sells to the final customer | A fictional bakery sells cakes online |
| B2B (business to business) | A business sells to another business | A seed supplier takes online orders from farm shops |
| B2G (business to government) | A business sells to a government body | A stationery firm supplies a ministry through an online tender |
| C2C (consumer to consumer) | Individuals sell to each other through a platform | A student sells a used textbook through an online notice board |

## Benefits and risks of doing business online

| Area | Benefit | Risk or problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reach and market | Customers anywhere; open 24 hours | More rivals can reach your customers too |
| Cost | Lower rent and staff cost; cheaper promotion | Cost of website, security and delivery |
| Data | Information on what customers view and buy | Data must be protected; customers need to trust the firm |
| Security | Secure payment systems exist | Fraud, hacking and fake sites damage trust |
| Delivery | Digital goods are delivered instantly | Physical goods need reliable and affordable delivery |
| Customer trust | Reviews and ratings help new buyers | Customers cannot touch the product; returns are harder |
| Infrastructure | Mobile money and phones widen access | Poor internet, power cuts and data costs limit some customers |

**Digital and physical distribution.** Some products can be distributed digitally (music, software, training courses). Physical goods still need warehouses, transport and delivery, so the business must plan couriers, cost and speed. Many firms combine both: customers order online and collect from a shop.

**The effect of ICT on the marketing mix.** *Product*: customers can ask for customised items. *Price*: prices are easy to compare and can change fast (dynamic pricing). *Promotion*: targeted, measurable messages. *Place*: online ordering and new channels, but delivery cost.

## Measuring digital marketing

Because online behaviour is recorded, a business can calculate:

- **Conversion rate** = orders ÷ visitors × 100;
- **Cost per order** = promotion cost ÷ orders;
- **Promotion cost as a percentage of revenue**.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: measuring an online campaign]
**Context.** Thandeka's website had 12,000 visitors in a month and received 360 orders, with an average order of US\$22. She spent US\$1,584 on online adverts that month.

| Measure | Working | Result |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Conversion rate | 360 ÷ 12,000 × 100 | 3.0% |
| Revenue (US\$) | 360 × 22 | 7,920 |
| Advertising cost per order (US\$) | 1,584 ÷ 360 | 4.40 |
| Advertising as % of revenue | 1,584 ÷ 7,920 × 100 | 20.0% |

1. Conversion rate = 360 ÷ 12,000 × 100 = 3.0%.
2. Revenue = 360 × 22 = US\$7,920.
3. Advertising cost per order = 1,584 ÷ 360 = US\$4.40, which is 20% of the revenue.
4. **Interpretation.** Revenue is not profit: after the cost of making the baskets, delivery and advertising Thandeka may keep little. She should compare the US\$4.40 with the profit she makes on each order before deciding whether the adverts pay.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, which channel?]
**Context.** The US\$1,584 was split between social media and search adverts.

| Channel | Spend (US\$) | Visitors | Orders | Conversion rate | Cost per order (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Social media | 800 | 5,000 | 200 | 4.0% | 4.00 |
| Search | 784 | 7,000 | 160 | 2.3% | 4.90 |

**Point.** Social media is the more efficient channel on these figures.

**Application.** Social media converted 4.0% of visitors and cost US\$4.00 an order. Search brought more visitors (7,000) but converted only 2.3% and cost US\$4.90 an order.

**Analysis.** Visitors from social media may already know the baskets through photographs and friends' posts, so they are more ready to buy. Search visitors include people who were only looking. However, search reaches new customers who did not know of Thandeka, and one month of data may not show repeat orders.

**Judgement.** Thandeka should move some of the money to social media but keep a smaller search budget to find new customers. She should review the figures for three months and also count repeat buyers before cutting search completely.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating e-commerce and e-business as the same"}
E-commerce is the buying and selling online. E-business is wider: it includes ordering from suppliers, managing orders and serving customers using electronic networks.
:::

:::mistake{title="Only listing benefits"}
Online trading has risks for the firm and for customers: security, delivery, trust, cost of the system and unequal access to the internet. Good answers weigh both sides for the **stated** business.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading conversion rate as sales"}
Conversion rate is the **percentage of visitors who buy**. A high number of visitors with a low conversion rate may still give fewer orders than a smaller, better-targeted audience.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *state* uses of ICT in marketing, *describe* e-business and *discuss* its benefits and risks. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 use *identify*, *explain*, *analyse* and *assess*. Refer to the type of business in the case (customers, product, delivery), and weigh a benefit against a risk. A judgement should depend on a condition, for example "if the firm's customers have reliable internet access and the product can be delivered at reasonable cost".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Digital promotion includes websites, social media, search, email and mobile messages, and influencers; most can be targeted and measured.
- E-commerce models are B2C, B2B, B2G and C2C; e-business covers all business activity done electronically.
- Benefits include wider reach, lower cost and customer data; risks include security, delivery, trust and unequal access.
- Digital goods are delivered electronically, physical goods still need transport and logistics.
- Conversion rate and cost per order show how efficiently an online campaign works.
:::
