## Introduction

Nyasha runs a fictional bakery in Mutare. Her flour costs more this month, a supermarket has started selling cheaper bread nearby, and a school wants to place a large weekly order. What price should she charge? A price that is too high loses customers, and a price that is too low loses profit. Price is the only element of the marketing mix that brings in revenue directly, so it is examined in every syllabus: ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 6) and Cambridge **AS** (Papers 1 and 2), where it appears in short answers, data response questions and essays.

You should already know the difference between fixed and variable costs and how a market is described by demand and supply. The link between price and the response of customers (elasticity) is a separate lesson; here we use it only as an idea.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |
| Pricing strategies; mark-up; cost-plus; price skimming; price penetration; psychological pricing | Pricing methods: cost-based (cost-plus, mark-up), competitive, penetration, skimming, price discrimination, dynamic, psychological |

The methods are the same. Cambridge answers usually need an evaluation of which method suits a stated business.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cost-plus pricing"}
Setting the price by adding a profit margin or percentage mark-up to the cost of making or buying one unit.
:::

:::definition{term="Price skimming"}
Setting a high price for a new product to earn a large profit per unit from buyers who will pay more, usually lowering the price later.
:::

:::definition{term="Penetration pricing"}
Setting a low price for a new product to win market share quickly, usually raising the price later.
:::

:::definition{term="Price discrimination"}
Charging different prices to different groups of customers, or at different times, for the same product, where the cost of supplying is not the reason for the difference.
:::

:::keyfact
Price = cost per unit + mark-up. **Mark-up (%) = profit ÷ cost × 100.** **Profit margin (%) = profit ÷ selling price × 100.** The margin is always smaller than the mark-up.
:::

## Pricing objectives

A business sets its price to meet an objective: **profit maximisation**, a target return, **survival** (covering costs in a bad time), **market share** growth, **sales maximisation**, quick return of the investment in a new product, or building a quality image. The same business may change its objective: a new firm may aim for share first and profit later.

## Factors that determine price

- **Costs.** In the long run the price must cover the cost of each unit; otherwise the business makes a loss.
- **Competition.** A product with many close substitutes cannot be priced far above its rivals.
- **Demand.** How many customers will buy at each price, and how sharply sales respond to a change in price (elasticity).
- **Market conditions.** Growth, season, income levels, inflation or a shortage of supply.
- **Objectives and the rest of the mix.** A premium brand needs a high price; a price must be consistent with the product, promotion and place.
- **Legal rules.** Some prices are controlled, and agreements between firms to fix prices are restricted in most countries (refer to the general rule, not a named law).

## Pricing methods

| Method | How it works | Suitable when | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cost-plus (mark-up) | Add a percentage to the cost per unit | Many different products; simple and quick | Ignores demand and rivals; cost per unit depends on output |
| Competitive | Match or stay close to rivals' prices | Many close substitutes; an established market | Little freedom; may start a price war |
| Penetration | Low price at launch | New product, price-sensitive buyers, scale economies likely | Low early profit; difficult to raise price later |
| Skimming | High price at launch | Unique product, loyal early adopters, short life | Attracts rivals; sells fewer units |
| Price discrimination | Different prices for different groups or times | Markets can be separated (students, peak and off-peak) | Needs control to stop resale; customers may resent it |
| Dynamic | Price changes quickly with demand, stock or competitors' prices | Online sales, transport, accommodation | Customers may distrust constantly changing prices |
| Psychological | Prices such as US\$4.95 rather than US\$5.00 | Retail goods bought by habit | Effect is uncertain and may be small |

**Cost-based pricing.** *Cost-plus* uses the full cost per unit (direct cost plus the share of overheads) and a **mark-up**. A business may also use only the **variable cost** and ensure that total contribution covers fixed cost. In both cases the price chosen should still be tested against what customers will accept.

**Elasticity and price.** When demand is price elastic, a price cut raises total revenue; when it is inelastic, a price rise raises revenue. A business that knows its customers are not sensitive to price can use skimming or a higher mark-up.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: cost-plus pricing]
**Context.** Nyasha's bakery makes 6,000 loaves a month. Direct costs (flour, yeast and baking labour) are US\$0.42 a loaf and monthly overheads are US\$1,800. She adds a mark-up of 50% on full cost.

| Item | US\$ per loaf |
| --- | ---: |
| Direct costs (flour, yeast, labour) | 0.42 |
| Overheads: 1,800 ÷ 6,000 | 0.30 |
| **Full cost per loaf** | 0.72 |
| Mark-up 50% of cost | 0.36 |
| **Selling price** | 1.08 |

1. Overheads per loaf = 1,800 ÷ 6,000 = US\$0.30, so full cost = 0.42 + 0.30 = US\$0.72.
2. Mark-up = 50% × 0.72 = US\$0.36, so the price is 0.72 + 0.36 = US\$1.08.
3. Profit margin = 0.36 ÷ 1.08 × 100 = 33.3%. A 50% mark-up gives a margin of only one third: the mark-up is based on cost, the margin on price.
4. **Check against the market.** If the supermarket's bread sells at US\$0.95, Nyasha must decide whether customers value fresh baking enough to pay 13 cents more, or whether she should reduce costs, lower her mark-up or make a different product.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, skimming or penetration]
**Context.** Zuva Gadgets, a fictional business in Harare, launches a solar phone charger. Variable cost is US\$9 per unit. The owner expects the following sales in the first month.

| Strategy | Price (US\$) | Units sold in month 1 | Revenue (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Skimming | 25 | 2,000 | 50,000 |
| Penetration | 15 | 5,200 | 78,000 |

**Point.** Penetration brings more revenue and more customers; skimming brings more profit per unit.

**Application.** Contribution at US\$25 is (25 − 9) × 2,000 = US\$32,000. At US\$15 it is (15 − 9) × 5,200 = US\$31,200. The first month's contribution is almost equal, but penetration sells 3,200 more chargers.

**Analysis.** Penetration builds a customer base, brand awareness and larger production, which may lower unit cost later. But a low price makes a later price rise hard, and rivals may copy the product. Skimming earns cash early and suits a product that rivals cannot quickly copy.

**Judgement.** If copying is easy, penetration is the better choice because it gains market share before rivals arrive. If the charger is protected by a unique feature, skimming is better because early buyers will pay more. Zuva should also compare the early cash needs of the business with the extra volume.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing mark-up and margin"}
A 50% mark-up on cost is a margin of 33.3% on price. Check which base a question uses: mark-up divides by **cost**, margin by **selling price**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting overheads in cost-plus"}
Full-cost pricing adds a share of overheads to the direct cost. Adding the mark-up to direct cost only underprices the product.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling any low price penetration"}
Penetration is a **low launch price to win share**. A permanently low price for a cheap product is not necessarily penetration pricing, and a sale price is a promotion.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *state* objectives, *describe* methods and *discuss* the choice of a method for a firm. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 use *define*, *calculate* (price from a mark-up), *explain*, *analyse* and *assess*. Show every step of a cost-plus calculation. For an evaluation, say which method suits the case and why, then add a limit (rivals' reaction, customers' response, the effect on cash flow). Always link the method to the product, the market and the firm's objective.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A price should fit the firm's objective and be set with costs, competition, demand and market conditions in mind.
- Cost-plus adds a mark-up to the full cost per unit; mark-up is based on cost and profit margin on price.
- Penetration pricing seeks market share with a low launch price; skimming seeks high unit profit from a high one.
- Competitive, dynamic, psychological and discriminatory pricing suit particular markets.
- A method is judged by the product, the customers' sensitivity to price, rivals' reaction and the effect on cash flow.
:::
