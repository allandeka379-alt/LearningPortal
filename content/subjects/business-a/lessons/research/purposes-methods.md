## Introduction

Rudo wants to open a fictional frozen-yoghurt kiosk near the bus terminus in Gweru. Her savings are limited, so she cannot afford to get it wrong. Before she buys a freezer she needs to know how many people pass the terminus each hour, what they would pay, whether they would prefer fruit or chocolate flavours, and whether another vendor is planning to open next door. She could guess, or she could do **market research**: collect and analyse information about customers, competitors and the market before she decides. Research costs time and money, but a wrong decision costs more.

This lesson applies to ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **AS** (Papers 1 and 2). Analysing and presenting the data is the next lesson. All sample-size calculations are produced by code.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Market research"}
The process of collecting, recording and analysing information about the market, customers and competitors to help a business make marketing decisions.
:::

:::definition{term="Primary research"}
Original information collected for a specific purpose, directly from the market (field research).
:::

:::definition{term="Secondary research"}
Information that already exists, collected by others for another purpose (desk research).
:::

:::definition{term="Sample"}
A group chosen from the whole population of interest, from which findings are drawn and applied to the whole population.
:::

## Purposes of market research

- **Market size and growth**: how many customers, and is the market rising or falling?
- **Customer profile**: who buys, their age, income and location.
- **Wants and needs**: tastes, preferences and problems; what would make them buy.
- **Competitors**: their products, prices and strengths.
- **Reaction to price, product or promotion**: before a launch, or after a change.
- **Reducing risk** in decisions about new products, prices and markets.

## Primary and secondary research

| | Primary (field) | Secondary (desk) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source | Collected directly: surveys, interviews, observation | Existing data: government statistics, trade bodies, company records, newspapers, internet |
| Advantages | Specific to the problem; up to date; under the firm's control; competitors do not have it | Quick and cheap; wide coverage; shows trends over time |
| Disadvantages | Costly and slow; needs skills; sample may be biased | May be out of date, not exactly what is needed, or collected for a different purpose; open to competitors |

Most firms start with **secondary** research, then use **primary** research to fill the gaps.

## Methods and instruments

| Method | Description | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Questionnaire (paper, telephone or online survey) | Standard questions to many people | Cheap per respondent; easy to compare results; large samples | Low response; shallow answers; misunderstanding; dishonest replies |
| Interview | A trained interviewer asks questions face to face or by phone | Can explain and probe; higher response | Costly; interviewer bias; slow |
| Focus group | A small group discusses a product led by a moderator | Rich opinions and reasons; ideas | Small, not representative; strong voices dominate; cost |
| Observation | Watching customers' behaviour (counting, noting) | Shows what people really do | Does not show why; observer bias |
| Document review | Studying existing records (sales, complaints) | Cheap; real data | May be incomplete or out of date |

**Quantitative** research produces numbers (how many, how much) which can be counted and compared; **qualitative** research produces opinions, reasons and attitudes (why). Many studies use both: qualitative first to find out *what to ask*, then quantitative to measure *how many*.

## Sampling

It is usually impossible or too costly to ask the whole population, so a **sample** is chosen. A sample reduces cost and time but may not represent the population, so results have a margin of error. **Bigger** samples are more reliable but cost more.

| Method | How the sample is chosen | Advantage | Disadvantage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Random (probability) | Every member has an equal chance (draw names, random numbers) | Unbiased; no choice by the researcher | Needs a full list; a small random sample may still miss groups |
| Systematic (probability) | Choose every *n*th member from a list, after a random start | Simple and quick | A pattern in the list may bias the sample |
| Stratified (probability) | Divide the population into groups (strata) and sample from each in proportion | Each group represented | Needs knowledge of the groups; more work |
| Quota (non-probability) | Interviewers choose people to fill set numbers in each category | Cheap and fast; groups represented | Interviewer choice may bias; not random |

Sample interval for systematic sampling = population ÷ sample size. In stratified sampling the sample for each stratum = stratum size ÷ population × total sample.

## Designing a questionnaire

Good questions are **clear**, **short**, one idea per question, **neutral** (not leading: "Don't you agree our service is excellent?"), and in a logical order, with easy questions first. Use closed questions (tick a box or scale) for counting and a few open questions for opinions. State the purpose and keep the questionnaire short. **Pilot** the questionnaire on a few people before the main survey.

## The research process

1. Define the problem and the objectives.
2. Decide the budget, method and sample.
3. Collect secondary data, then primary data.
4. Analyse and interpret the results.
5. Report and use the findings in decisions; review the results later.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: choosing a systematic and a stratified sample]
**Context.** A fictional supermarket in Gweru has a list of 1,200 loyalty-card customers and wants to ask 60 of them about a new range.

**Systematic.** Interval = 1,200 ÷ 60 = every 20th customer on the list after a random start. If the random start is customer 7, the sample is customers 7, 27, 47, and so on.

**Stratified by age.** The customers are: under 25 (600), 25 to 44 (360), 45 and over (240).

| Age group | Customers | Calculation | Sample |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Under 25 | 600 | 600 ÷ 1,200 × 60 | 30 |
| 25 to 44 | 360 | 360 ÷ 1,200 × 60 | 18 |
| 45 and over | 240 | 240 ÷ 1,200 × 60 | 12 |

1. The stratified sample (30 + 18 + 12 = 60) has the same age mix as the customer list.
2. A systematic sample would probably be similar, but a pattern in the list (for example, customers listed in order of joining) might bias it.
3. **Judgement.** If age affects what customers want, the stratified sample is better because it guarantees that every age group is heard; a systematic sample is quicker if the list is in random order.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: case analysis, choosing a research method]
**Context.** Rudo's fictional kiosk needs to know whether people would pay US\$2 for a cup of frozen yoghurt. She compares three methods.

| Method | Cost (US\$) | Number of people | Cost per person (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Online survey shared on social media | 60 | 120 | 0.50 |
| Face-to-face interviews at the terminus | 200 | 40 | 5.00 |
| Focus group with snacks | 120 | 8 | 15.00 |

**Point.** The online survey is much the cheapest per person.

**Application.** Her customers are commuters. Many have phones, but a social media survey would reach those who follow her page and not necessarily commuters.

**Analysis.** Interviews at the terminus cost more per person but reach the actual target group and allow questions about reasons. The focus group is the most expensive per person and only 8 people attend, but it could suggest flavours she had not thought of.

**Judgement.** Rudo should use secondary research on commuter numbers (free), then face-to-face interviews for a quantitative picture. She could add a small focus group later if the result is unclear and her budget allows.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing primary and secondary research"}
Primary research is collected **by the firm for its own purpose**; secondary data already exists. A survey the firm runs is primary even if it is carried out online.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a larger sample always gives the right answer"}
A larger sample is more reliable if it is selected properly, but a large biased sample is still biased and costs more.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing leading questions"}
A question that suggests the answer ("Do you agree that our prices are low?") gives biased results. Use neutral wording and balanced options.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *state*, *outline* and *describe* research methods and *draft* a short questionnaire; Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *identify*, *explain*, *analyse* and *assess* the usefulness of a method or sampling choice. Always link the choice to the case: the size of the market, the budget, the type of customer and the decision to be made. For an *assess* answer, weigh cost, speed, reliability and bias, and make a judgement with a condition.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Market research reduces risk by finding out about market size, customers, wants and competitors.
- Primary research is collected first-hand; secondary research already exists; most firms start with secondary.
- Methods include questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, observation and document review; they can be quantitative or qualitative.
- Samples save cost but may not be representative; random, systematic, stratified and quota sampling differ in bias and cost.
- Questionnaires should be clear, short, neutral and piloted; research follows a process from objectives to action.
:::
