## Introduction

Rudo's fictional frozen-yoghurt kiosk in Gweru has run a two-week survey. Of 240 people asked, 96 prefer chocolate, 72 strawberry, 48 vanilla and 24 something else. She also counted customers each day for ten days. The numbers are in her notebook but numbers alone do not tell her what to order, what to charge or when to hire help. She must **analyse** them, **present** them clearly and decide how far she can **trust** them. Data that is badly collected or misread leads to confident but wrong decisions.

This lesson applies to ZIMSEC Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills (Form 5) and Cambridge **AS** (Papers 1 and 2). Measures of central tendency and dispersion are ZIMSEC detail; Cambridge expects interpretation of tables, charts and graphs and judgement of reliability. All calculations are produced by code.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC | Cambridge |
| --- | --- |
| Mean, median, mode, range and standard deviation; tables, graphs and pie charts; research report | Analysis of quantitative and qualitative data; interpretation of tables, charts and graphs; reliability of data |

Standard deviation is ZIMSEC detail. Cambridge does not ask for a calculation of it but expects an understanding of spread and reliability.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Reliability of data"}
The extent to which research findings can be trusted to be accurate and to represent the market, judged by the source, sample, method and date.
:::

:::definition{term="Mean"}
The sum of all the values divided by the number of values.
:::

:::definition{term="Median"}
The middle value when the data is arranged in order of size.
:::

:::definition{term="Mode"}
The value that occurs most often.
:::

:::definition{term="Standard deviation (ZIMSEC detail)"}
A measure of how widely the values are spread around the mean.
:::

## How reliable is the data?

Before using data, ask:

- **Source**: who collected it, and why? Could they be biased?
- **Sample**: how many people were asked, how were they chosen, and do they represent the market?
- **Method**: were the questions clear and neutral? Did people answer honestly?
- **Date**: is it up to date?
- **Response rate**: did most people reply?
- **Consistency**: do different sources agree?

Poor reliability should lead to caution, a larger or better sample, or more research.

## Analysing data

**Quantitative data** is analysed with totals, percentages, averages and trends. **Qualitative data** (comments, interview notes) is analysed by grouping common themes and counting how often each appears, then summarising with typical quotations. Both kinds should be compared with the research objectives.

### Presenting data

| Form | Best for | Points to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Table | Exact figures and comparisons | Clear headings, units and totals |
| Bar chart | Comparing categories | Axis starts at zero; same scale |
| Line graph | Change over time | Time on the horizontal axis; trend and seasonal pattern |
| Pie chart | Shares of a whole | Angles add up to 360°; angle = share × 360 |

A misleading chart (truncated axis, unequal scales) can exaggerate differences. Always read the labels and the scale.

### Measures of central tendency and spread (ZIMSEC detail)

| Measure | How to find it | Use | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mean | Total ÷ number of values | Typical value using all the data | Affected by very high or low values |
| Median | Middle value when ordered (mean of the two middle values if an even number) | Typical value not distorted by extremes | Ignores the size of other values |
| Mode | Most frequent value | Most popular size or choice | May not exist or be several |
| Range | Highest − lowest | Quick idea of spread | Uses only two values |
| Standard deviation | √(sum of (x − mean)² ÷ n) | Spread around the mean using all values | More complex; needs a calculator |

A small standard deviation means that the values are close to the mean (consistent demand); a large one means that they vary widely (unpredictable demand).

## Using the results

The analysis is written up in a **research report**: purpose, method and sample, findings (with tables and charts), conclusions and recommendations, and the limitations. The findings are then used for decisions on the product, price, promotion and place, but should be combined with managers' judgement, costs and other evidence, since research shows what people say, not always what they do.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: mean, median, mode, range and standard deviation]
**Context.** The kiosk counted customers each day for ten days: 42, 38, 45, 38, 50, 41, 38, 47, 51, 40.

| Day | Customers (x) | x − mean | (x − mean)² |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 42 | (1) | 1 |
| 2 | 38 | (5) | 25 |
| 3 | 45 | 2 | 4 |
| 4 | 38 | (5) | 25 |
| 5 | 50 | 7 | 49 |
| 6 | 41 | (2) | 4 |
| 7 | 38 | (5) | 25 |
| 8 | 47 | 4 | 16 |
| 9 | 51 | 8 | 64 |
| 10 | 40 | (3) | 9 |

1. **Mean** = 430 ÷ 10 = 43 customers a day.
2. **Median**: in order, 38, 38, 38, 40, 41, 42, 45, 47, 50, 51. The middle two values are 41 and 42, so the median is 41.5.
3. **Mode** = 38 (it occurs three times).
4. **Range** = 51 − 38 = 13.
5. **Standard deviation** = √(222 ÷ 10) = √22.2 = 4.71 (to two decimal places), using the population formula (divide by n).
6. **Judgement.** The mean (43) is above the median (41.5) because a few busy days pull it up. The standard deviation is small compared with the mean (about 11%), so daily demand is fairly steady, and the kiosk can plan staff and stock on about 43 customers a day, though it should be ready for days of 50 or more.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a pie chart and the use of survey results]
**Context.** The survey of 240 people asked for their favourite flavour.

| Flavour | Number | Percentage | Pie chart angle |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Chocolate | 96 | 40% | 144° |
| Strawberry | 72 | 30% | 108° |
| Vanilla | 48 | 20% | 72° |
| Other | 24 | 10% | 36° |
| **Total** | **240** | **100%** | **360°** |

1. Percentage = number ÷ 240 × 100; angle = number ÷ 240 × 360. For chocolate, 96 ÷ 240 × 360 = 144°.
2. The angles add up to 360° and the percentages to 100%, which is a check on the arithmetic.

**Point.** Chocolate and strawberry together are 70% of preferences.

**Application.** Rudo should stock chocolate and strawberry as the main flavours and keep vanilla as a smaller line.

**Analysis.** The finding is from a survey of 240 people, but if they were all asked at the university gate, they may be younger than the average customer. The preferences of commuters at the bus terminus could differ.

**Judgement.** She should order flavours in the ratio 4 : 3 : 2 : 1 as a starting point, then adjust when real sales data is available, and check whether the sample represented her actual customers.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using the mean when there are extreme values"}
One very large value pulls the mean up. In such cases the median is a better "typical" value. State which measure you use and why.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading a chart without checking the scale"}
A bar chart whose axis starts above zero exaggerates differences. Check the axes, units and title before drawing a conclusion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating survey answers as certain facts"}
People may say they will buy and then do not. State the limits of the sample and the method when you draw conclusions.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Business Studies and Business Enterprise Skills Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *calculate* the mean, median, mode, range or standard deviation, *draw or read* a pie chart and *discuss* the use of results. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *interpret* tables and graphs and to *assess* the reliability of data. Show your working and the units. For an assessment, comment on the sample, the method and the date, and make a judgement on how far the findings should be relied on, with a condition (for example, confirm with a pilot sale).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Reliability depends on the source, sample, method, date and response rate.
- Quantitative data is analysed with totals, percentages, averages and trends; qualitative data by grouping themes.
- Tables show exact figures, bar charts compare, line graphs show change and pie charts show shares (angle = share × 360°).
- Mean, median, mode, range and standard deviation describe the typical value and the spread (ZIMSEC detail).
- Results are written in a report and combined with judgement and costs; they show what people say, not always what they do.
:::
