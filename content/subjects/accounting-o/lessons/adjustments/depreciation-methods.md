## Introduction

Farai runs a small engineering workshop in Bulawayo. Three years ago he bought a metal-cutting machine. It still works, but it is slower than it was, its blades are worn and newer machines cut more accurately. The machine has helped Farai earn income in every one of those years, so part of its cost should be charged against the income of each year. That part is **depreciation**.

Depreciation appears in almost every set of financial statements you will prepare. Both ZIMSEC and Cambridge set questions that ask you to state the causes of depreciation, to calculate it by different methods, to choose a suitable method for an asset and, in Paper 2, to use your figures in ledger accounts and statements. This lesson covers the meaning and the calculations; the next two lessons record depreciation and disposals in the ledger.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards use **provision for depreciation**; Cambridge also calls the total so far **accumulated depreciation**. The income statement is the **statement of profit or loss** in Cambridge papers. Both boards examine the straight-line, reducing-balance and revaluation methods.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Depreciation"}
The part of the cost of a non-current asset that is used up (consumed) in an accounting period, charged as an expense in that period.
:::

:::definition{term="Net book value (carrying amount)"}
The cost of a non-current asset less the total depreciation charged on it so far.
:::

:::definition{term="Residual value"}
The amount the business expects to receive for an asset at the end of its useful life (also called scrap value or trade-in value).
:::

:::definition{term="Useful life"}
The number of years the business expects to use the asset to earn income.
:::

## Causes of depreciation

Non-current assets lose value for four main reasons:

- **Physical deterioration (wear and tear):** machines, vehicles and furniture wear out as they are used and as they are exposed to weather. A minibus running daily between Harare and Chitungwiza wears out faster than one used once a week.
- **Obsolescence:** an asset becomes out of date because a newer, more efficient model appears, even though the old one still works. Computers are the usual example.
- **Passage of time:** some assets have a fixed legal life. A ten-year lease on a shop loses one tenth of its value every year, whatever the business does.
- **Depletion:** natural resources such as a quarry or a mine are used up as material is removed.

Some books add **inadequacy**: an asset is no longer big enough for a growing business.

## Why a business must provide for depreciation

Depreciation applies the **matching (accruals) concept**. The cost of the machine is matched against the income it helps to earn over its life, instead of being charged in full in the year it was bought. If Farai charged nothing, his profit would be overstated every year and the machine would appear in the statement of financial position at a value it no longer has. Providing for depreciation also applies **prudence**: assets are not overstated.

Depreciation is a **non-cash expense**. No money leaves the business when depreciation is charged; the cash was paid when the asset was bought.

## The straight-line method

The same amount is charged every year:

**Annual depreciation = (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life in years**

Questions sometimes give a percentage of cost instead ("20% per annum on cost"), which means the same thing with no residual value: 20% of cost every year.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: straight-line depreciation on Farai's machine]
Farai bought the cutting machine on 1 January 2022 for US\$18,000. He expects to use it for four years and then sell it for US\$2,000. His year end is 31 December.

1. Amount to be depreciated: 18,000 − 2,000 = 16,000.
2. Annual depreciation: 16,000 ÷ 4 = 4,000.
3. Each year the net book value falls by 4,000 until it reaches the residual value.

| Year | Depreciation US\$ | Accumulated depreciation US\$ | Net book value US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2022 | 4,000 | 4,000 | 14,000 |
| 2023 | 4,000 | 8,000 | 10,000 |
| 2024 | 4,000 | 12,000 | 6,000 |
| 2025 | 4,000 | 16,000 | 2,000 |

At the end of 2025 the net book value equals the residual value of US\$2,000.
:::

## The reducing-balance method

A fixed percentage is applied each year to the **net book value at the start of the year** (cost less depreciation so far), not to the cost. The charge is highest in the first year and falls every year after that, and the net book value never quite reaches zero.

**Depreciation for the year = rate % × (cost − accumulated depreciation brought forward)**

:::worked-solution[Example 2: reducing balance on a delivery van]
Rudo bought a delivery van for her wholesale business in Mutare on 1 January 2023 for US\$24,000. She depreciates vans at 25% per annum using the reducing-balance method.

1. 2023: 25% × 24,000 = 6,000; net book value 24,000 − 6,000 = 18,000.
2. 2024: 25% × 18,000 = 4,500; net book value 18,000 − 4,500 = 13,500.
3. 2025: 25% × 13,500 = 3,375; net book value 13,500 − 3,375 = 10,125.

| Year | Depreciation US\$ | Accumulated depreciation US\$ | Net book value US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2023 | 6,000 | 6,000 | 18,000 |
| 2024 | 4,500 | 10,500 | 13,500 |
| 2025 | 3,375 | 13,875 | 10,125 |

Each year's charge is 25% of the previous year's closing net book value, never 25% of the original cost.
:::

## The revaluation method

Some assets are small, numerous and hard to track one by one: loose tools in a workshop, crockery in a restaurant, livestock on a farm. For these the business values the whole group at the end of each year. Depreciation is the fall in value:

**Depreciation = value at the start of the year + items bought during the year − value at the end of the year**

If any items were sold, the money received is also deducted before comparing with the closing value.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: revaluing loose tools]
Farai's loose tools were valued at US\$2,400 on 1 January 2025. During the year he bought more tools for US\$900. On 31 December 2025 the tools were valued at US\$2,650.

| Loose tools | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Valuation at 1 January 2025 | 2,400 |
| Add: Tools bought during the year | 900 |
|  | 3,300 |
| Less: Valuation at 31 December 2025 | (2,650) |
| **Depreciation for the year** | **650** |

The tools available during the year were worth 3,300; only 2,650 remain, so 650 was used up.
:::

## Assets bought or sold during the year

A question always states the policy. Common ones are:

- **A full year's depreciation in the year of purchase and none in the year of sale.**
- **Depreciation on a monthly basis (time apportioned).** A machine costing US\$9,600 bought on 1 April and depreciated at 10% per annum on cost is charged 10% × 9,600 × 9/12 = US\$720 in the first year (April to December).

Read the policy carefully: it decides how many months, or whether any depreciation at all, is charged.

## Choosing a method

| Method | Suits | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Straight line | Buildings, furniture, leases, equipment used evenly | Benefit is about the same each year; simple to calculate |
| Reducing balance | Motor vehicles, machinery, computers | They lose most value early; repairs rise as charges fall, so the total cost each year is more even |
| Revaluation | Loose tools, crockery, livestock, small equipment | Many low-cost items that are impractical to depreciate one by one |

Once a method is chosen it should be used every year (the **consistency** concept), so that profits can be compared.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Applying the reducing-balance rate to cost every year"}
That turns reducing balance into straight line. Apply the rate to the net book value brought forward.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the residual value"}
Straight-line depreciation spreads cost **less** residual value over the useful life.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking depreciation is money set aside"}
Depreciation does not put cash into a fund. It is an expense that allocates cost; the business is no richer in cash because of it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the stated policy for part years"}
If the question says "monthly basis", charge only the months the asset was owned.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Show the formula and one line of working for every figure, for example "(18,000 − 2,000) ÷ 4 = 4,000". Method marks are given even if a later figure is wrong.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to choose a method, name the method **and** give a reason linked to how the asset loses value.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Depreciation is the part of a non-current asset's cost used up in a period; it is a non-cash expense.
- Causes: wear and tear, obsolescence, passage of time and depletion.
- It applies the matching concept and prevents profit and assets being overstated.
- Straight line: (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life, the same every year.
- Reducing balance: a fixed percentage of the net book value brought forward; falls every year.
- Revaluation: opening value + purchases − closing value; used for loose tools and similar assets.
- Follow the stated policy for assets bought or sold during the year, and apply the chosen method consistently.
:::
