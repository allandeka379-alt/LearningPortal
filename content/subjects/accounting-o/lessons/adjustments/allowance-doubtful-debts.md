## Introduction

Chipo supplies school uniforms on credit to shops across Mashonaland West. At the end of each year about forty shops still owe her money. She cannot name any customer who will definitely fail to pay, so nothing can be written off. Experience tells her, though, that roughly one dollar in every twenty owed at the year end is never collected.

To show a realistic value for her trade receivables, Chipo keeps an **allowance for irrecoverable debts**, which ZIMSEC calls a **provision for doubtful debts**. It is an estimate, it is adjusted every year, and only the **change** in it affects profit. Questions on the allowance appear in nearly every Paper 2, either on their own (the allowance account over two or three years) or as an adjustment in a full set of financial statements.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC: **provision for doubtful debts** and **bad debts**. Cambridge 0452: **allowance for irrecoverable debts** and **irrecoverable debts**. The income statement is the **statement of profit or loss** in Cambridge papers. The method and the entries are the same for both boards.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Doubtful debt"}
A debt that may not be paid, although the business has not yet given up on it.
:::

:::definition{term="Allowance for irrecoverable debts (provision for doubtful debts)"}
An estimate of the part of trade receivables at the year end that will probably not be collected. It is kept in its own account with a credit balance and deducted from trade receivables in the statement of financial position.
:::

:::definition{term="Net trade receivables"}
Trade receivables less the allowance for irrecoverable debts: the amount the business realistically expects to collect.
:::

## Why keep an allowance?

- **Prudence:** trade receivables should not be shown at more than the business expects to collect.
- **Matching:** the expected loss on this year's credit sales is charged against this year's profit, not next year's when the customers actually default.

The allowance is usually a **percentage of trade receivables**, based on past experience. Sometimes it is a specific amount for named customers, or a mixture of both.

## Calculating the allowance

1. Start with trade receivables at the year end.
2. **Deduct any further irrecoverable debts first.** A debt being written off cannot also be doubtful.
3. Apply the percentage to the remaining trade receivables: this is the allowance **needed** (the closing balance).
4. Compare with the allowance brought forward:
   - **Increase** = needed − existing: an expense in the income statement.
   - **Decrease** = existing − needed: added to gross profit as other income (or deducted from expenses).

## The double entry

| Event | Debit | Credit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Create or increase the allowance | Income statement | Allowance for irrecoverable debts |
| Reduce the allowance | Allowance for irrecoverable debts | Income statement |

The allowance account is never debited with individual customers' debts; those go to the irrecoverable debts account.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the allowance over three years]
Chipo keeps an allowance of 5% of trade receivables. She created it on 31 December 2023. Trade receivables (after writing off irrecoverable debts) were:

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a write-off before the allowance]
Sipho's trial balance at 31 December 2025 shows trade receivables US\$18,400 and an allowance for irrecoverable debts of US\$650. A further debt of US\$400 is to be written off, and the allowance is to be 4% of trade receivables.

1. Trade receivables after the write-off: 18,400 − 400 = 18,000.
2. Allowance needed: 4% × 18,000 = 720.
3. Increase: 720 − 650 = 70.
4. Income statement expenses: irrecoverable debts 400; increase in allowance 70.

## Irrecoverable debts and the allowance together

The two adjustments deal with different things:

- **Irrecoverable debts** are actual losses on named customers, written off in full.
- **The allowance** is an estimate for debts that **might** not be paid, and only its change hits profit.

In an income statement both may appear among the expenses: "Irrecoverable debts 400" and "Increase in allowance for irrecoverable debts 70". A decrease in the allowance is shown with other income.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Charging the whole allowance every year"}
Only the first year charges the whole allowance. After that, only the increase or decrease is entered in the income statement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calculating the allowance before the write-off"}
Deduct further irrecoverable debts from trade receivables first, then apply the percentage.
:::

:::mistake{title="Showing the allowance as a liability"}
The allowance is deducted from trade receivables under current assets.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the trial balance allowance as the closing figure"}
The trial balance figure is the opening allowance; calculate the new one from this year's trade receivables.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Lay out the calculation in three lines: trade receivables after write-offs; allowance needed; change (increase or decrease). Examiners give method marks for each line.
:::

:::exam-tip
In the statement of financial position, show trade receivables, the allowance deducted in brackets, and the net figure in the next column. Use the board's term: provision for doubtful debts (ZIMSEC) or allowance for irrecoverable debts (Cambridge).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The allowance (provision) for irrecoverable (doubtful) debts estimates debts that may not be collected; it applies prudence and matching.
- Write off irrecoverable debts first, then apply the percentage to the remaining trade receivables.
- Only the change in the allowance affects profit: an increase is an expense, a decrease is income.
- Increase: debit income statement, credit allowance. Decrease: debit allowance, credit income statement.
- The closing allowance is deducted from trade receivables in the statement of financial position.
:::
