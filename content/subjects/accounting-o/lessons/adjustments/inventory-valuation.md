## Introduction

Thandeka runs a clothing shop in Bulawayo. On 31 December 2025 she counts the goods on her shelves: suits, school blazers, jackets and shoes. The count tells her how many items she has, but not what they are worth. Some blazers were damaged by a leaking roof and will need repairs before they can be sold. A rack of jackets has gone out of fashion and will only sell at a reduced price.

The value Thandeka puts on her closing inventory decides her cost of sales, so it decides her gross profit, her profit for the year and the current assets in her statement of financial position. The Cambridge 0452 syllabus requires you to value inventory at the **lower of cost and net realisable value** and to explain what happens when inventory is valued incorrectly. Expect multiple-choice questions on the rule, short calculations on a list of items, and Paper 2 parts on the effect of an error.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
This topic is in the **Cambridge** syllabus only; the ZIMSEC syllabus does not examine valuation at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Cambridge papers use **statement of profit or loss** and **profit for the year**.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cost of inventory"}
The purchase price of the goods plus the costs of bringing them to their present location and condition, such as carriage inwards. It does not include selling costs.
:::

:::definition{term="Net realisable value (NRV)"}
The estimated selling price of the goods less any costs still needed to complete them and to sell them (for example repairs, commission or delivery to the customer).
:::

:::definition{term="Lower of cost and net realisable value"}
The rule that each item of inventory is valued at whichever is lower: what it cost, or what it will bring in after the costs of selling it.
:::

## Why the lower figure?

The rule applies the **prudence** concept. Profit should not be anticipated: if goods are expected to sell for more than they cost, the profit is not recognised until they are sold, so they stay at cost. Losses should be recognised as soon as they are expected: if goods will sell for less than they cost, the loss is recognised now by valuing them at net realisable value.

It also supports the **realisation** concept (profit is recognised when goods are sold) and gives a value in the statement of financial position that is not overstated.

## Valuing item by item

The rule is applied to **each item or group of similar items separately**, not to the total. Comparing total cost with total NRV lets the profit on some items hide the losses on others.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: Thandeka's closing inventory]
Thandeka's inventory count at 31 December 2025:

- 40 men's suits: cost US\$85 each, normal selling price US\$140.
- 25 damaged school blazers: cost US\$32 each; after repairs costing US\$6 each they will sell for US\$30.
- 18 out-of-fashion jackets: cost US\$55 each; they will sell for US\$60 each through an agent who takes commission of US\$6 each.
- 60 pairs of leather shoes: cost US\$28 a pair, selling price US\$45.

1. NRV per unit = selling price − costs to complete and sell: blazers 30 − 6 = 24; jackets 60 − 6 = 54.
2. Take the lower figure for each item.

| Item | Units | Cost per unit US\$ | NRV per unit US\$ | Lower per unit US\$ | Value US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Men's suits | 40 | 85 | 140 | 85 | 3,400 |
| Damaged school blazers | 25 | 32 | 24 | 24 | 600 |
| Out-of-fashion jackets | 18 | 55 | 54 | 54 | 972 |
| Leather shoes (pairs) | 60 | 28 | 45 | 28 | 1,680 |

3. Closing inventory: 3,400 + 600 + 972 + 1,680 = 6,652.

At cost the inventory totals 6,870 and at NRV it totals 9,872. Taking the lower of those two totals (6,870) would be wrong: it ignores the expected losses of 200 on the blazers and 18 on the jackets.
:::

## Effect of an incorrect inventory valuation

Closing inventory is deducted in the cost of sales calculation:

**Cost of sales = opening inventory + purchases (+ carriage inwards − returns) − closing inventory**

So if closing inventory is **overstated**:

| Item | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Cost of sales | Understated |
| Gross profit and profit for the year | Overstated |
| Current assets (inventory) | Overstated |
| Equity (capital, through profit) | Overstated |

If closing inventory is **understated**, every effect is the other way round.

There is a second effect. This year's closing inventory becomes **next year's opening inventory**. An overstated closing inventory this year becomes an overstated opening inventory next year, which overstates next year's cost of sales and **understates** next year's profit by the same amount. Over the two years the error cancels out, but each year's profit is wrong.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: closing inventory overstated by US\$1,200]
Farai's sports shop recorded closing inventory at 31 December 2025 at US\$8,900. Some footballs were included at cost although they had been damaged and could only be sold at a loss; the correct value is US\$7,700. Revenue was US\$58,000, opening inventory US\$7,400 and purchases US\$41,300.

|  | As recorded US\$ | Corrected US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue | 58,000 | 58,000 |
| Opening inventory | 7,400 | 7,400 |
| Purchases | 41,300 | 41,300 |
| Less: Closing inventory | (8,900) | (7,700) |
| Cost of sales | 39,800 | 41,000 |
| Gross profit | 18,200 | 17,000 |

1. Inventory overstated by 8,900 − 7,700 = 1,200.
2. Cost of sales understated by 1,200, so gross profit (and profit for the year) is overstated by 1,200.
3. Current assets and equity at 31 December 2025 are overstated by 1,200.
4. In 2026 the opening inventory would be 1,200 too high, so 2026 profit would be 1,200 too low unless the error is corrected.
:::

## What is included in cost

- **Included:** purchase price (after trade discount), carriage inwards, import duty, and the cost of getting goods ready for sale.
- **Excluded:** carriage outwards, selling commission, advertising and storage after the goods are ready. These are selling or running costs; they are deducted when calculating NRV, not added to cost.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Comparing totals instead of items"}
Apply the rule to each item or group separately, then add up.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using selling price as NRV"}
NRV is selling price **less** the costs to complete and sell, such as repairs and commission.
:::

:::mistake{title="Valuing at NRV when it is higher than cost"}
Goods expected to sell at a profit stay at cost: profit is recognised only when they are sold.
:::

:::mistake{title="Getting the direction of the error wrong"}
Overstated closing inventory means understated cost of sales and **overstated** profit this year.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Lay out an item-by-item table with cost, NRV and the lower figure. Even if one NRV is wrong, the method earns marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
When explaining an error, follow the chain: closing inventory → cost of sales → gross profit → profit for the year → equity, and state the next year's effect too.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value (Cambridge).
- NRV = estimated selling price − costs to complete and sell.
- Apply the rule item by item; never compare totals.
- The rule applies prudence: profits are not anticipated, expected losses are recognised.
- Overstated closing inventory: cost of sales understated; gross profit, profit for the year, current assets and equity overstated; next year's profit understated.
:::
