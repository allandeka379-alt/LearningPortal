## Introduction

Tendai runs a delivery business in Harare. After three and a half years his first van is costly to repair, so he sells it to a mechanic and uses the money towards a newer one. The van must now leave his books: its cost must come out of the motor van account, the depreciation charged on it must come out of the provision account, and the business must work out whether it sold the van for more or less than its net book value.

Disposals are a favourite Paper 2 topic for both boards. A typical question gives you the asset and provision balances, a sale or a part-exchange during the year and a depreciation policy, and asks for the asset account, the provision for depreciation account and the disposal account, plus the profit or loss on disposal. This lesson builds on the two previous depreciation lessons.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards use a **disposal account** and the **provision for depreciation** (Cambridge: **accumulated depreciation**). The profit or loss on disposal goes to the **income statement** (ZIMSEC) or the **statement of profit or loss** (Cambridge). Net book value is also called **carrying amount**.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Disposal"}
Removing a non-current asset from the business by selling it, trading it in (part-exchange) or scrapping it.
:::

:::definition{term="Disposal account"}
A temporary ledger account that collects the cost, the accumulated depreciation and the proceeds of an asset disposed of; its balance is the profit or loss on disposal.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit or loss on disposal"}
The difference between the proceeds of disposal and the net book value of the asset at the date of disposal. Proceeds above net book value give a profit; below give a loss.
:::

:::definition{term="Part-exchange (trade-in) allowance"}
The amount a supplier allows for an old asset handed over as part of the price of a new one. It is treated as the proceeds of the old asset.
:::

## Calculating the profit or loss

**Net book value at disposal = cost − accumulated depreciation up to the date of disposal**

**Profit (or loss) on disposal = proceeds − net book value**

A profit on disposal does not mean the asset was a good buy. It usually means too much depreciation was charged in earlier years; a loss means too little was charged. It is an adjustment to earlier depreciation, shown in the income statement for the year of disposal.

## The double entry

Four entries remove the asset and find the result:

| Step | Debit | Credit | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Transfer the cost | Disposal | Asset account | Cost of the asset sold |
| 2. Transfer the depreciation | Provision for depreciation | Disposal | Depreciation on that asset to date |
| 3. Record the proceeds | Bank / Cash (or the new asset, for a part-exchange) | Disposal | Amount received or allowed |
| 4. Close the disposal account | Disposal (profit) **or** Income statement (loss) | Income statement (profit) **or** Disposal (loss) | Balancing figure |

Before step 2, make sure the depreciation includes any charge for the year of disposal required by the policy.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: Tendai sells a van for cash]
Tendai bought a van on 1 January 2022 for US\$25,000. Vans are depreciated at 20% per annum on cost, with a full year's charge in the year of purchase and none in the year of sale. On 30 June 2025 he sold the van for US\$9,500, received by bank transfer.

1. Depreciation charged: 2022, 2023 and 2024 at 20% × 25,000 = 5,000 a year, so 15,000 in total. None is charged in 2025, the year of sale.
2. Net book value at disposal: 25,000 − 15,000 = 10,000.
3. Loss on disposal: 9,500 − 10,000 = a loss of 500.

## Part-exchange

When an old asset is traded in, the supplier's allowance replaces cash as the proceeds. The new asset is recorded at its **full cost**: the allowance plus the cash paid.

- Debit the asset account (new asset) with the allowance; credit the disposal account.
- Debit the asset account with the balance paid; credit bank.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: part-exchange with monthly depreciation]
Nyasha's printing business bought a machine on 1 April 2023 for US\$16,000. Machinery is depreciated at 10% per annum on cost, on a monthly basis. On 30 September 2025 she traded the machine in for a new one costing US\$20,000. The supplier allowed US\$13,100 for the old machine and Nyasha paid the balance by bank transfer. She owns no other machinery.

1. Depreciation on the old machine: 2023 (9 months) 1,200; 2024 1,600; so the provision at 1 January 2025 is 2,800. 2025 to 30 September (9 months): 10% × 16,000 × 9/12 = 1,200. Total 4,000.
2. Net book value at disposal: 16,000 − 4,000 = 12,000.
3. Profit on disposal: 13,100 − 12,000 = 1,100.
4. Cash paid: 20,000 − 13,100 = 6,900. Depreciation on the new machine for October to December: 10% × 20,000 × 3/12 = 500.

## Where the results appear

- **Profit on disposal:** added to gross profit as other income in the income statement.
- **Loss on disposal:** listed among the expenses in the income statement.
- **Statement of financial position:** the asset and its depreciation are no longer included; only assets still owned are shown at cost less accumulated depreciation.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Transferring the net book value instead of the cost"}
Step 1 always transfers the full cost; the depreciation is transferred separately in step 2.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting depreciation in the year of disposal"}
If the policy is monthly, charge depreciation up to the disposal date before working out the net book value.
:::

:::mistake{title="Recording the new asset at the cash paid only"}
In a part-exchange the new asset's cost is the allowance plus the cash paid.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the loss on the wrong side"}
On the disposal account a loss is the credit balancing figure (debited to the income statement); a profit is the debit balancing figure.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Work out the profit or loss with a quick calculation first (proceeds − net book value), then check that the balancing figure in your disposal account agrees with it.
:::

:::exam-tip
Label each entry with the account it comes from or goes to ("Machinery", "Provision for depreciation", "Bank", "Income statement"). Examiners give one mark per correctly labelled entry.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Disposal removes an asset's cost and its accumulated depreciation from the books.
- Profit or loss on disposal = proceeds − net book value at the date of disposal.
- Disposal account: debit cost; credit provision for depreciation and proceeds; the balancing figure is the profit or loss.
- In a part-exchange the allowance is the proceeds, and the new asset is recorded at allowance plus cash paid.
- Charge depreciation up to the date of disposal if the policy requires it.
- A profit is other income and a loss is an expense in the income statement.
:::
