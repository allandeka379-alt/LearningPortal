## Introduction

Tendai rents a hardware shop in Gweru for US\$450 a month. In December 2025 he paid the rent for January and February 2026 early, because he had spare cash after the festive season. At the same time, his December electricity bill had not yet arrived. If he simply added up what he paid during 2025, his rent expense would be too high and his electricity expense too low.

The financial statements must show the expenses that **belong** to the year, whether or not they have been paid. Adjusting for accrued and prepaid expenses is tested every year by both boards: in multiple-choice questions on the expense figure, in ledger accounts with opening and closing balances, and inside full sets of financial statements.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards use **accrued expenses** and **prepaid expenses**. In the statement of financial position, accrued expenses are **other payables** and prepaid expenses are **other receivables** (Cambridge wording; ZIMSEC also accepts "accruals" and "prepayments"). The income statement is the **statement of profit or loss** in Cambridge papers.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Matching (accruals) concept"}
Income and expenses are recognised in the period in which they are earned or incurred, not when cash is received or paid.
:::

:::definition{term="Accrued expense (accrual)"}
An expense of the current period that has not yet been paid at the year end. It is a current liability (other payables).
:::

:::definition{term="Prepaid expense (prepayment)"}
An amount paid during the current period for a service that belongs to a later period. It is a current asset (other receivables).
:::

## The basic rule

**Expense for the year = amount paid + closing accrual − closing prepayment**

When there are balances from the previous year as well:

**Expense for the year = amount paid − opening accrual + opening prepayment + closing accrual − closing prepayment**

An opening accrual was an expense of **last** year, paid this year, so it is subtracted. An opening prepayment was paid **last** year for this year, so it is added.

| Adjustment | Effect on this year's expense | Statement of financial position |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Closing accrual | Increase | Current liability: other payables |
| Closing prepayment | Decrease | Current asset: other receivables |

## Year-end entries

The adjustment is made in the expense account itself. The amount owing or prepaid is carried down as a balance:

- **Accrual:** a balance c/d on the **debit** side of the expense account, brought down on the **credit** side (a liability).
- **Prepayment:** a balance c/d on the **credit** side of the expense account, brought down on the **debit** side (an asset).
- The figure transferred to the income statement is the balancing figure: the expense for the year.

As a journal entry, a prepayment is debit other receivables, credit the expense; an accrual is debit the expense, credit other payables.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: a prepayment of rent]
Tendai's shop rent is US\$450 a month. During 2025 he paid US\$6,300, which includes January and February 2026.

1. Rent that belongs to 2025: 450 × 12 = 5,400.
2. Prepaid at 31 December 2025: 6,300 − 5,400 = 900 (two months).

**Rent account**

| Date | Details | US\$ | Date | Details | US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- | --- | ---: |
| Various | Bank | 6,300 | 31 Dec 2025 | Income statement | 5,400 |
|  |  |  | 31 Dec 2025 | Prepaid c/d | 900 |
|  |  | **6,300** |  |  | **6,300** |
| 1 Jan 2026 | Prepaid b/d | 900 |  |  |  |

**Journal**

| Date | Details | Debit US\$ | Credit US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| 31 Dec 2025 | Other receivables (rent prepaid) | 900 |  |
|  | Rent |  | 900 |
|  | *Rent paid in advance at the year end* |  |  |

The income statement shows rent of 5,400; the statement of financial position shows other receivables of 900 under current assets.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: electricity with opening and closing accruals]
On 1 January 2025 Tendai owed US\$380 for electricity used in December 2024. During 2025 he paid electricity bills of US\$3,140, including the 380. At 31 December 2025 he owed US\$415 for December 2025.

1. Expense: 3,140 − 380 + 415 = 3,175.
2. The opening accrual is on the credit side (a liability brought down); the closing accrual is carried down on the debit side.

**Electricity account**

| Date | Details | US\$ | Date | Details | US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- | --- | ---: |
| Various | Bank | 3,140 | 1 Jan 2025 | Accrued b/d | 380 |
| 31 Dec 2025 | Accrued c/d | 415 | 31 Dec 2025 | Income statement | 3,175 |
|  |  | **3,555** |  |  | **3,555** |
|  |  |  | 1 Jan 2026 | Accrued b/d | 415 |

The 380 paid in 2025 was last year's expense, so it is cancelled by the opening balance; only the electricity used in 2025 reaches the income statement.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: insurance with an opening prepayment]
On 1 January 2025 insurance of US\$240 had been paid in advance. On 1 April 2025 Tendai paid US\$1,560 for the year to 31 March 2026.

1. Prepaid at 31 December 2025: January to March 2026 is 3 months, 1,560 × 3/12 = 390.
2. Expense: 240 + 1,560 − 390 = 1,410.

|  |  | **1,800** |  |  | **1,800** |

## Adjustments in a trial balance question

In a full-statement question the trial balance shows the amount **paid**, and the notes list the accruals and prepayments. For each note:

1. Adjust the expense line in the income statement (add an accrual, deduct a prepayment).
2. Show the same figure in the statement of financial position (accrual under current liabilities, prepayment under current assets).

Each adjustment is used **twice**. Ticking each note when you have used it in both statements avoids the most common slip.

## Effect on profit and working capital

- A closing **accrual** increases expenses, so it **reduces** profit; it also increases current liabilities.
- A closing **prepayment** reduces expenses, so it **increases** profit; it also increases current assets.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reversing the adjustment"}
An accrual is added to the amount paid; a prepayment is subtracted. Ask: "Does this belong to this year?"
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the opening balances"}
An opening accrual reduces this year's expense; an opening prepayment increases it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Showing an accrual as a current asset"}
Money still owed is a liability (other payables); money paid in advance is an asset (other receivables).
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting months wrongly"}
A payment on 1 April for twelve months covers April to March: nine months this year, three months next year.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Write the adjustment beside the expense in the income statement, for example "Rent 6,300 − 900 = 5,400". If the final figure is wrong, the working can still earn method marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
In ledger accounts, label the balances "Accrued c/d" and "Prepaid c/d" and bring them down to the next year: the b/d entries carry marks.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Expenses are matched to the period in which they are incurred, not when paid.
- Expense = paid − opening accrual + opening prepayment + closing accrual − closing prepayment.
- A closing accrual is a current liability (other payables); a closing prepayment is a current asset (other receivables).
- In the ledger, balances are carried down in the expense account; the income statement transfer is the balancing figure.
- Accruals reduce profit; prepayments increase profit.
- Use each adjustment twice: once in the income statement, once in the statement of financial position.
:::
