## Introduction

Sipho runs a commuter minibus between Gweru and Kwekwe. This year he bought a second minibus, paid to have it delivered and fitted with extra seats, and then paid for fuel, insurance and a driver every week. All of these are payments, but they are not the same kind of payment. The minibus will earn fares for years; the fuel is used up in a day.

Accountants separate spending that buys or improves long-lasting assets (**capital expenditure**) from spending on the day-to-day running of the business (**revenue expenditure**). Getting this right decides whether an amount goes in the income statement, reducing this year's profit, or in the statement of financial position as an asset. Examiners test the distinction in multiple-choice questions and in structured questions that ask you to classify a list of payments.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards examine capital and revenue **expenditure**. Cambridge 0452 also examines capital and revenue **receipts**. The income statement is the **statement of profit or loss** in Cambridge papers, and net profit is **profit for the year**.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Capital expenditure"}
Spending on buying non-current assets, on bringing them into use, or on improving them so that they last longer or earn more. It benefits the business for more than one year.
:::

:::definition{term="Revenue expenditure"}
Spending on running the business day to day, including keeping non-current assets in their existing condition. It benefits only the current period.
:::

:::definition{term="Capital receipt"}
Money received that is not part of the normal trading income of the business, such as capital introduced by the owner, a loan received, or the proceeds from selling a non-current asset.
:::

:::definition{term="Revenue receipt"}
Money earned from the normal activities of the business, such as sales, rent received, commission received and interest received.
:::

## Capital or revenue expenditure?

Ask two questions. Does the spending create or improve a long-term asset? Is the benefit used up within the year?

| Capital expenditure | Revenue expenditure |
| --- | --- |
| Buying premises, vehicles, machinery, furniture | Buying goods for resale (purchases) |
| Delivery (carriage inwards on a non-current asset) | Fuel, electricity, rent, wages |
| Installation and testing of machinery | Repairs and maintenance |
| Legal fees on buying premises | Insurance and annual licences |
| Building an extension; fitting extra seats | Repainting, servicing, replacing worn parts |

The key test for repairs is **improvement or maintenance**. Replacing a broken window keeps the building as it was: revenue. Adding a new storeroom makes the building bigger: capital.

Costs that are needed to get an asset **ready for use** are part of its cost. Wages paid to the business's own staff for installing a machine are capital expenditure; wages paid to staff for running it afterwards are revenue.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: classifying Sipho's payments]
Sipho made these payments in connection with his new minibus:

| Payment | US\$ | Type |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Purchase price of the minibus | 28,500 | Capital |
| Delivery from Harare | 650 | Capital |
| Registration and number plates | 320 | Capital |
| Fitting extra seats | 1,400 | Capital |
| Insurance for the first year | 980 | Revenue |
| Fuel for the first week | 240 | Revenue |
| Driver's wages for the first month | 1,100 | Revenue |

1. Price, delivery, first registration and fitting the seats are needed to buy the minibus, get it on the road and improve its earning power: capital.
2. Insurance, fuel and wages are running costs that are used up: revenue.
3. Cost of the minibus (non-current asset): $28,500 + 650 + 320 + 1,400 = 30,870$.
4. Revenue expenditure (expenses this year): $980 + 240 + 1,100 = 2,320$.
:::

## Capital or revenue receipts? (Cambridge)

| Capital receipts | Revenue receipts |
| --- | --- |
| Capital introduced by the owner | Sales (revenue) |
| A loan received | Rent received, commission received |
| Proceeds from selling a non-current asset | Interest received |

Capital receipts change the statement of financial position (capital, liabilities or non-current assets). Revenue receipts are income in the income statement.

## How each is accounted for

- **Capital expenditure** is debited to a non-current asset account and shown in the statement of financial position. Its cost is spread over the asset's life by depreciation (studied in a later lesson).
- **Revenue expenditure** is debited to an expense account (or purchases) and charged in full in this year's income statement.
- **Capital receipts** are credited to capital, a loan account or the asset (or disposal) account, and appear in the statement of financial position.
- **Revenue receipts** are credited to income accounts and appear in the income statement.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: Rudo's bakery in the financial statements]
In 2025 Rudo bought a new oven for US\$7,200 and paid US\$450 to have it installed. She paid US\$380 to repair the oven later in the year, electricity of US\$1,240 and wages of US\$9,600. She received rent of US\$1,800 for letting a back room and took a loan of US\$5,000 from Mutapa Finance. Her gross profit was US\$24,600.

1. Oven cost (capital): $7,200 + 450 = 7,650$, shown as a non-current asset.
2. Repairs, electricity and wages (revenue expenditure): $380 + 1,240 + 9,600 = 11,220$, shown as expenses.
3. Rent received is a revenue receipt, added to gross profit: $24,600 + 1,800 = 26,400$.
4. The loan is a capital receipt, shown as a non-current liability.
5. Profit for the year: $26,400 - 11,220 = 15,180$.

## Why the distinction matters

If capital expenditure were charged in full in the year of purchase, Sipho's profit would collapse in the year he bought the minibus and look unusually high in the following years, even though the minibus earned fares in all of them. Treating it as an asset and charging depreciation spreads its cost over the years that benefit, which applies the matching (accruals) concept. A loan treated as income would make profit look far better than it is. The next lesson shows how to calculate the effect on profit and on asset values when the distinction is made wrongly.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating all costs of a new asset as expenses"}
Delivery, installation and legal fees needed to bring an asset into use are part of its cost.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating all repairs as capital"}
Repairs and maintenance keep an asset in its existing condition: revenue. Only improvements are capital.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a loan income"}
A loan received is a capital receipt and a liability. It must be repaid, so it is never income.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When classifying, give a reason: "capital, because it improves the building" or "revenue, because it maintains the van". Many marks are for the reason.
:::

:::exam-tip
Watch for wording such as "extension", "improvement", "installation" (usually capital) and "repair", "repaint", "service", "replace a part" (usually revenue).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Capital expenditure buys, brings into use or improves non-current assets; it is shown in the statement of financial position.
- Revenue expenditure runs the business and maintains assets; it is an expense in this year's income statement.
- Capital receipts (loans, capital introduced, sale of non-current assets) affect the statement of financial position.
- Revenue receipts (sales, rent, commission, interest) are income in the income statement.
- Decide by asking whether the benefit lasts beyond the year and whether the asset is improved or merely maintained.
:::
