## Introduction

Chipo runs a tailoring business in Bulawayo. Her bookkeeper recorded a new industrial sewing machine as "repairs" because it arrived on the same invoice as some spare needles. Nothing went wrong in the trial balance, which still balanced. Yet Chipo's profit for the year was far too low, and her statement of financial position did not show a machine she uses every day.

An error of this kind is an **error of principle**: an amount is posted to the right side of the wrong **class** of account. It does not show up in the trial balance, so it has to be found by checking, and its effects calculated. This is a favourite examination task: "State the effect on profit" or "Calculate the corrected profit".

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC calls the result **net profit** and the statement the **income statement**; Cambridge uses **profit for the year** and the **statement of profit or loss**. Both call the long-term assets **non-current assets**.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Overstated"}
Shown higher than the correct figure.
:::

:::definition{term="Understated"}
Shown lower than the correct figure.
:::

:::definition{term="Error of principle"}
An entry made in the wrong class of account, such as a non-current asset recorded as an expense. The trial balance still agrees.
:::

:::definition{term="Working capital"}
Current assets minus current liabilities (also called net current assets).
:::

## The two errors and their effects

| Error | Effect on profit | Effect on non-current assets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capital expenditure treated as revenue (asset debited to an expense) | Understated | Understated |
| Revenue expenditure treated as capital (expense debited to an asset) | Overstated | Overstated |

The logic is simple. An expense reduces profit. If something that should be an asset is wrongly charged as an expense, profit falls too far and the asset is missing. If an expense is wrongly added to an asset, profit is too high and the asset is too high.

### Allowing for depreciation

Capital expenditure is not charged to profit in full; part of it is charged each year as depreciation. So when capital expenditure has been wrongly charged as an expense, the correct profit is:

draft profit **+** the amount wrongly expensed **−** the depreciation that should have been charged.

The asset's net book value is understated by the same net amount. In the opposite case (revenue expenditure wrongly capitalised) any depreciation charged on it must be added back as well.

### Working capital

In most of these errors the payment itself (the credit to bank or to a supplier) was recorded correctly, so current assets and current liabilities are not affected and **working capital does not change**. Only the debit is in the wrong place. Working capital is affected only when the misclassification involves a current item, for example if an item of inventory is wrongly added to equipment instead of being included in closing inventory.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: Chipo's sewing machine]
Chipo bought an industrial sewing machine for US\$2,400 at the start of 2025. It was debited to the repairs account. Machinery is depreciated at 10% of cost per year. Her draft profit was US\$9,600.

1. The machine was charged as an expense, so profit is understated by US\$2,400.
2. Depreciation that should have been charged: $2,400 \times 10\% = 240$.
3. Net effect on profit: understated by $2,400 - 240 = 2,160$.
4. Corrected profit: $9,600 + 2,160 = 11,760$.
5. Non-current assets are understated by the net book value of the machine, $2,400 - 240 = 2,160$.
6. Working capital is unchanged: the bank payment was recorded correctly.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: several errors at Sipho's Engineering]
Sipho's draft profit for the year ended 31 December 2025 was US\$18,750, calculated before any depreciation on the items below. Checking the books revealed:

- (i) Repairs to the delivery van, US\$650, had been debited to the motor vehicles account.
- (ii) A new computer, US\$1,800, had been debited to office expenses. Computers are depreciated at 20% of cost.
- (iii) Delivery of a new machine, US\$320, had been debited to carriage inwards. Machinery is depreciated at 10% of cost.
- (iv) Wages of US\$2,100 paid to Sipho's own staff for building a storeroom extension had been debited to wages. Buildings are not depreciated in the first year.

1. (i) Revenue treated as capital: profit overstated, so deduct 650.
2. (ii) Capital treated as revenue: add 1,800, then deduct depreciation $1,800 \times 20\% = 360$.
3. (iii) Delivery is part of the machine's cost: add 320, deduct depreciation $320 \times 10\% = 32$.
4. (iv) Own staff building an extension is capital: add 2,100.
5. Corrected profit: $18,750 - 650 + 1,800 - 360 + 320 - 32 + 2,100 = 21,928$.

## Why it matters

Misclassification misleads every user of the accounts. If profit is understated, the owner may think the business is failing and the tax computed on profit may be wrong. If profit is overstated, the owner may take drawings the business cannot afford, and a bank may lend against assets that do not really exist. Correct classification is a direct application of the **matching** (accruals) concept: costs are charged against the income of the periods they help to earn.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Getting the direction the wrong way round"}
Too much expense means profit is too **low** (understated). Too little expense means profit is too **high** (overstated).
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting depreciation"}
When an asset is put back into the accounts, the depreciation that should have been charged on it must be deducted from profit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the trial balance will not agree"}
Errors of principle do not affect the agreement of the trial balance: a debit is still matched by a credit.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Use the words "overstated" and "understated" and always give the amount. "Profit is overstated by US\$650" earns the mark; "profit is wrong" does not.
:::

:::exam-tip
Set out a corrected profit as a statement starting from the draft figure, with "Add" and "Less" labels and a final total. Method marks are given for each line even if one figure is wrong.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Capital expenditure treated as revenue: profit and non-current assets understated.
- Revenue expenditure treated as capital: profit and non-current assets overstated.
- When correcting, also adjust for the depreciation that should (or should not) have been charged.
- Working capital is usually unaffected, because only the debit was misposted.
- These are errors of principle: the trial balance still agrees.
:::
