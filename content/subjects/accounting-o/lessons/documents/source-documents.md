## Introduction

Nyasha runs a tuck shop near a secondary school in Masvingo. Every week she buys cooking oil, sugar, bread and drinks from a wholesaler, sells to pupils for cash, pays her assistant and banks her takings. None of this can be recorded from memory. Each transaction leaves a piece of paper or a digital record behind: an invoice, a receipt, a cheque counterfoil, a bank statement. These are **source documents**, and they are the evidence on which every entry in the books is based.

In the examination you are expected to say what a document shows, who prepares it, who receives it, and in which **book of prime entry** it is first recorded. Questions often give you a document and ask for the double entry, so this topic links directly to the ledger.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC calls the first books of record **subsidiary books** or **books of original entry**. Cambridge calls them **books of prime entry**. They are the same books: the sales, purchases, returns and general journals, the cash book and the petty cash book.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Source document"}
A business document, on paper or in digital form, that gives evidence of a transaction and supplies the details needed to record it.
:::

:::definition{term="Book of prime entry"}
The book in which a transaction is first recorded from its source document before it is posted to the ledger.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade discount"}
A reduction in the list price given by a supplier to a trade customer, usually for buying in bulk. It is deducted on the invoice and is **never recorded** in the ledger.
:::

## The main documents

### Invoices

An **invoice** is issued by the seller to a customer who buys goods **on credit**. It lists the goods, quantities, unit prices, any trade discount and the amount owed, and it may state the payment terms (for example "2.5% cash discount if paid within 14 days").

- The **seller** keeps a copy and records it in the **sales journal**.
- The **buyer** records the invoice received in the **purchases journal**.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: reading an invoice with a trade discount]
On 3 March 2025 Masvingo Wholesale Supplies sold goods on credit to Nyasha's Tuck Shop.

**Masvingo Wholesale Supplies — Invoice No. 2147 to Nyasha's Tuck Shop, 3 March 2025**

|  | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| 20 cartons of cooking oil at US\$18 | 360 |
| 15 bags of sugar (10 kg) at US\$12 | 180 |
| Gross value of goods | 540 |
| Less trade discount 10% | (54) |
| **Amount due** | 486 |

1. The goods cost $20 \times 18 = 360$ and $15 \times 12 = 180$, a gross value of US\$540.
2. The 10% trade discount is US\$54, so the amount due is US\$486.
3. Nyasha records **US\$486** (not US\$540) in her purchases journal, then posts it to the credit of Masvingo Wholesale Supplies' account and the debit of purchases.
4. The wholesaler records US\$486 in its sales journal. The trade discount appears in neither set of ledger accounts.
:::

### Debit notes and credit notes

When goods are returned (damaged, wrong size, not ordered) or a customer has been overcharged, the invoice must be reduced.

- A **credit note** is issued by the **seller** to the customer. It tells the customer that their account has been credited (they owe less). The seller records it in the **returns inwards (sales returns) journal**; the customer records it in the **returns outwards (purchases returns) journal**.
- A **debit note** is sent by the **buyer** to the seller to request a credit note, or to show that the buyer has debited the supplier's account. It supports the entry in the buyer's returns outwards journal.

### Receipts, cheques and paying-in slips

- A **receipt** is written by the person who **receives** money as proof of payment. The payer keeps it as evidence for a payment entry; the receiver keeps a copy for a receipt entry. Both go to the **cash book**.
- A **cheque** is a written order to a bank to pay a stated sum. The drawer keeps the **cheque counterfoil** (stub), which is the source for the payment in the bank column of the cash book.
- A **paying-in slip** (deposit slip) is completed when cash or cheques are paid into the bank. Its counterfoil supports the debit in the bank column of the cash book (and the matching credit in the cash column if office cash was banked).

### Vouchers

A **petty cash voucher** supports each small payment from the petty cash float (bus fares, cleaning materials, postage). It is signed by the person receiving the money and recorded in the **petty cash book**. Other payment vouchers authorise larger payments and support the cash book.

### Bank statements and statements of account

- A **bank statement** is sent by the bank to its customer. It shows deposits, withdrawals, bank charges, interest, standing orders and direct transfers. It is used to **check and update** the cash book and to prepare a bank reconciliation, rather than as an original record of most transactions.
- A **statement of account** is sent by a seller to a credit customer, usually monthly. It summarises the opening balance, invoices, credit notes and payments and shows how much is owed. The customer checks it against their own records; it is not entered in a book of prime entry.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: checking a statement of account]
Masvingo Wholesale Supplies sent Nyasha this statement at the end of April.

**Masvingo Wholesale Supplies — Statement of account: Nyasha's Tuck Shop, April 2025**

| Date | Details | Debit US\$ | Credit US\$ | Balance US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 Apr | Balance brought forward | 486 |  | 486 Dr |
| 4 Apr | Invoice 2190 | 720 |  | 1,206 Dr |
| 9 Apr | Credit note 77 (damaged goods) |  | 60 | 1,146 Dr |
| 18 Apr | Cheque received — thank you |  | 486 | 660 Dr |
| 28 Apr | Invoice 2236 | 315 |  | 975 Dr |

1. The statement is written from the **seller's** point of view: invoices increase what Nyasha owes (debit), and the credit note and her cheque reduce it (credit).
2. The closing balance, US\$975, is the amount Nyasha owes on 30 April.
3. Nyasha compares each line with her purchases journal, returns outwards journal and cash book. In her own ledger the supplier's account should show a **credit** balance of US\$975, the mirror image of the statement.
:::

### Documents, books and entries at a glance

| Document | Issued by | Book of prime entry (in the books of the business named) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Invoice | Seller | Seller: sales journal. Buyer: purchases journal |
| Credit note | Seller | Seller: returns inwards journal. Buyer: returns outwards journal |
| Debit note | Buyer | Buyer: returns outwards journal (supporting document) |
| Receipt | Receiver of money | Cash book (both parties) |
| Cheque counterfoil | Drawer of the cheque | Cash book, bank column (payment) |
| Paying-in slip counterfoil | Business banking money | Cash book, bank column (receipt) |
| Petty cash voucher | Business (signed by the payee) | Petty cash book |
| Bank statement | Bank | Used to update the cash book and reconcile |
| Statement of account | Seller | Not entered; used for checking |

## Manual and digital documents

Many Zimbabwean businesses now send invoices by email or through accounting software, receive payment confirmations from mobile money and banking apps, and download bank statements. A digital document is a source document in exactly the same way as a paper one: it must show the date, the parties, the details and the amount, and it must be kept safely (backed up) as evidence. Digital documents can be produced quickly and copied without error, but they need passwords and back-ups to protect them from loss or alteration.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Recording the trade discount"}
Trade discount is deducted on the invoice and never entered in the books. Record the **net** invoice value. Only cash discounts (for prompt payment) are recorded, in the cash book.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up debit notes and credit notes"}
The **seller** issues the credit note, because it credits the customer's account in the seller's books. The buyer sends the debit note.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a statement of account as a book of prime entry"}
A statement of account only summarises transactions that have already been recorded from invoices, credit notes and receipts. Entering it again would double-count them.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked "which book of prime entry?", first decide **whose books** you are in. An invoice goes in the sales journal of the seller but the purchases journal of the buyer.
:::

:::exam-tip
In "describe" questions earn the marks with three facts: who issues the document, what it shows, and what it is used for in the records.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Source documents are the evidence for every entry; they may be paper or digital.
- Invoices record credit sales and purchases; credit notes and debit notes record returns and allowances.
- Receipts, cheque counterfoils and paying-in slips support the cash book; petty cash vouchers support the petty cash book.
- Trade discount is deducted on the invoice and never recorded; record the net amount.
- Bank statements and statements of account are used for checking and updating the records, not as books of prime entry.
:::
