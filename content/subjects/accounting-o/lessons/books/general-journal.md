## Introduction

Most transactions in a business fit neatly into one of the specialised books: credit sales into the sales journal, money into the cash book. But some do not. When Tatenda buys new shelving for his Bulawayo shop on credit, when a customer goes bankrupt and will never pay, or when Tatenda takes a bag of rice home for his family, there is no special book for the entry.

These unusual transactions go in the **general journal**, also called the **journal proper**. It is a book of prime entry where every entry shows the account to debit, the account to credit, the amounts and a short explanation called the **narrative**. Journal entries appear in almost every Paper 2: for opening entries, adjustments and, later in the course, the correction of errors.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC calls it the **general journal** or **journal proper** and lists it with the **subsidiary books**; Cambridge calls it the **general journal**, a **book of prime entry**. A debt written off is a **bad debt** in ZIMSEC usage and an **irrecoverable debt** in Cambridge papers. ZIMSEC journals may include a folio column; Cambridge ones do not need it.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="General journal"}
The book of prime entry used to record transactions that do not belong in any other book of prime entry.
:::

:::definition{term="Narrative"}
A short explanation written under each journal entry describing the transaction and, where useful, the source document or authority.
:::

:::definition{term="Opening entries"}
The journal entry that records the assets, liabilities and capital of a business when it opens a new set of books.
:::

:::definition{term="Irrecoverable debt (bad debt)"}
A debt that the business decides will never be paid and therefore writes off as an expense.
:::

## Layout of a journal entry

| Column | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| Date | The date of the transaction |
| Details | The account debited first; the account credited below it, often indented; then the narrative |
| Debit | The amount debited |
| Credit | The amount credited |

Each entry must balance: total debits equal total credits. An entry may have more than one debit or credit (a **compound** entry), as opening entries usually do.

## What goes in the general journal?

| Use | Debit | Credit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening entries | Each asset | Each liability, and capital (the balancing figure) |
| Non-current asset bought on credit | The asset account | The supplier |
| Non-current asset sold on credit | The buyer | The asset account (or disposal account) |
| Irrecoverable debt written off | Irrecoverable debts | The customer |
| Goods taken by the owner | Drawings | Purchases |
| Asset brought in by the owner | The asset account | Capital |
| Correction of errors | Depends on the error | Depends on the error |

Correction of errors uses the same layout; it is studied in a later lesson. When a non-current asset is sold for more or less than its book value, a disposal account is used, which is covered with depreciation.

### Why goods taken by the owner are credited to purchases

The goods were bought for resale and recorded in purchases. Taking them for private use means they will never be sold, so purchases are reduced (credited) at **cost** and drawings increased (debited).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: opening entries]
On 1 January 2025 Tatenda opened a new set of books for his grocery business. He had: premises US\$45,000, a motor van US\$12,000, inventory US\$6,300, a trade receivable (R. Moyo) US\$1,450 and US\$3,250 in the bank. He owed Harare Supplies US\$2,100 and had a loan of US\$10,000 from Mutapa Finance.

1. Total assets: $45,000 + 12,000 + 6,300 + 1,450 + 3,250 = 68,000$.
2. Total liabilities: $2,100 + 10,000 = 12,100$.
3. Capital (the balancing figure): $68,000 - 12,100 = 55,900$.
4. Debit each asset; credit each liability and capital.

**Journal**

| Date | Details | Debit US\$ | Credit US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| 1 Jan | Premises | 45,000 |  |
|  | Motor van | 12,000 |  |
|  | Inventory | 6,300 |  |
|  | Trade receivable: R. Moyo | 1,450 |  |
|  | Bank | 3,250 |  |
|  | Trade payable: Harare Supplies |  | 2,100 |
|  | Loan: Mutapa Finance |  | 10,000 |
|  | Capital |  | 55,900 |
|  | *Assets, liabilities and capital at the start of the year (opening entries)* |  |  |

5. Check: debits 68,000 equal credits $2,100 + 10,000 + 55,900 = 68,000$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: other uses in February]
Tatenda's transactions in February 2025 included:

- 4 Feb: bought shelving on credit from Gweru Shopfitters, US\$2,800.
- 10 Feb: sold an old delivery van on credit to Sipho Ncube for US\$3,500, its book value.
- 18 Feb: Farai Dube, who owed US\$340, was declared bankrupt; the debt is written off.
- 25 Feb: Tatenda took goods costing US\$180 for his family.
- 28 Feb: Tatenda brought his own computer, worth US\$900, into the business.

1. Shelving is fixtures and fittings (a non-current asset), not purchases. The supplier is credited.
2. The van leaves the business: credit motor vehicles; Sipho Ncube now owes the money, so debit him.
3. The bad debt becomes an expense: debit irrecoverable debts, credit Farai Dube.
4. Goods for own use: debit drawings, credit purchases, at cost.
5. The computer increases the owner's investment: debit office equipment, credit capital.

|  | Motor vehicles |  | 3,500 |
|  | *Old delivery van sold on credit at its book value* |  |  |
| 18 Feb | Irrecoverable debts | 340 |  |
|  | Farai Dube |  | 340 |
|  | *Debt written off: customer declared bankrupt* |  |  |
| 25 Feb | Drawings | 180 |  |
|  | Purchases |  | 180 |
|  | *Goods taken by the owner for private use* |  |  |

## Posting and interpreting journal entries

The journal itself is not part of the double entry. Each line is posted to the ledger: the debit line to the debit side of the named account, the credit line to the credit side, and the details column of each ledger entry names the **other** account.

To **interpret** an entry, read it as a sentence: "Debit drawings, credit purchases, US\$180" means the owner took goods costing US\$180 for private use, so drawings rise and the cost of goods available for sale falls.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Leaving out the narrative"}
Every entry needs a narrative. Examiners often give a mark for it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Recording goods for own use at selling price"}
Goods taken by the owner are recorded at cost, because purchases are recorded at cost.
:::

:::mistake{title="Debiting purchases for a non-current asset"}
Shelving, vehicles and computers bought for use in the business are debited to their asset accounts, never to purchases.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that capital is the balancing figure"}
In opening entries capital is found as total assets minus total liabilities.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Write the debit line first and the credit line below it, and check that each entry balances before you move on. Dates and narratives earn marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
If a question says "journal entries (narratives are not required)", you may leave them out; otherwise always include them.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The general journal records transactions that do not fit any other book of prime entry.
- Uses: opening entries, credit purchases and sales of non-current assets, irrecoverable debts, goods taken by the owner, assets brought in, transfers and correction of errors.
- Each entry has a date, the account debited, the account credited, equal amounts and a narrative.
- Goods taken for own use: debit drawings, credit purchases, at cost.
- Entries are posted to the ledger and can be read as a sentence to interpret them.
:::
