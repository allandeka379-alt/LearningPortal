## Introduction

The office of a hardware business in Masvingo needs stamps, a taxi to the bank, cleaning materials and the odd packet of pens. None of these is worth a bank transfer or a cheque, and the owner does not want the main cash book cluttered with dozens of tiny payments. So the business keeps a small amount of cash, the **petty cash**, in a locked box, looked after by a junior clerk called the **petty cashier**.

The petty cash book records these small payments in analysis columns, so that the totals can be posted to the ledger in one go. Both boards examine the imprest system and the analysed petty cash book, usually alongside the cash book. Cambridge also asks you to weigh up the benefits and limitations of keeping cash on the premises.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC treats the petty cash book as a **subsidiary book**; Cambridge calls it a **book of prime entry**. Both use the terms **imprest**, **float** and **petty cash voucher**.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Petty cash"}
A small amount of cash kept on the premises for small, everyday business payments.
:::

:::definition{term="Imprest system"}
A method of controlling petty cash in which the petty cashier starts each period with a fixed amount (the **imprest** or **float**). At the end of the period the amount spent is given back, restoring the float to the same fixed amount.
:::

:::definition{term="Petty cash voucher"}
A numbered form, signed by the person receiving the money and authorised by a manager, supporting each petty cash payment. A receipt is attached when possible.
:::

:::definition{term="Analysed petty cash book"}
A petty cash book with a total column and several analysis columns, one for each type of expense, plus a ledger column for payments to personal accounts.
:::

## How the imprest system works

1. The chief cashier gives the petty cashier the imprest, for example US\$250, by cheque or bank transfer. This is recorded as a payment in the cash book and a receipt in the petty cash book.
2. Each payment is made only against a signed and authorised voucher.
3. At any time, **cash in the box + vouchers = imprest**. A spot check can be made in seconds.
4. At the end of the period the petty cashier hands in the vouchers. The chief cashier checks them and gives back exactly the amount spent, so the float is restored to the imprest.

The amount needed to restore the float is therefore **imprest − cash left = total spent**.

## Layout of the analysed petty cash book

The receipts column is on the left, followed by the date, details, voucher number and **total** payment columns. To the right are the analysis columns, chosen by the business: postage, cleaning, travel, stationery, refreshments and so on. A **ledger** column records payments to suppliers' personal accounts.

Each payment is entered twice: in the total column and in one analysis column. At the end of the period every column is added, and the analysis totals must add up to the total column. This **cross-check** is how the petty cashier knows the analysis is right.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: writing up and balancing the petty cash book]
The petty cashier of Masvingo Hardware received an imprest of US\$250 from the bank on 1 August 2025. Payments in August:

- 2 Aug: postage stamps US\$18 (voucher 1).
- 4 Aug: cleaning materials US\$26.50 (2).
- 7 Aug: bus fares US\$14 (3).
- 11 Aug: envelopes and pens US\$22 (4).
- 15 Aug: paid T. Ndlovu, a supplier, US\$45 (5).
- 19 Aug: cleaner's wages US\$30 (6).
- 23 Aug: courier US\$16.50 (7).
- 27 Aug: taxi fare US\$21 (8).

1. Enter each payment in the total column and its analysis column.
2. Total the columns: total paid $18 + 26.50 + 14 + 22 + 45 + 30 + 16.50 + 21 = 193$.
3. Cross-check: $34.50 + 56.50 + 35 + 22 + 45 = 193$.
4. Balance c/d: $250 - 193 = 57$.
5. On 1 September bring down US\$57 and record the US\$193 received from the bank to restore the imprest: $57 + 193 = 250$.

## Posting the petty cash book

The petty cash book, like the cash book, is usually treated as both a book of prime entry and the ledger account for petty cash. The other halves of the entries are posted from the column totals:

- Each **analysis column total** is debited to the matching expense account.
- Each item in the **ledger column** is debited to the personal account of that supplier.
- The restoration of the float is credited to the bank column of the cash book.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: posting Masvingo Hardware's petty cash]
Using the August totals from Example 1:

1. Debit postage US\$34.50, cleaning US\$56.50, travel US\$35 and stationery US\$22.
2. Debit T. Ndlovu's account with US\$45. This clears the US\$45 he was owed.
3. Credit the bank column of the cash book with US\$193 on 1 September (details: petty cash).
4. Check: the debits $34.50 + 56.50 + 35 + 22 + 45 = 193$ equal the amount spent.

## Benefits and limitations of keeping cash on the premises

| Benefits | Limitations |
| --- | --- |
| Small payments can be made at once without bank transfers or cheques | Cash can be stolen, lost or misused |
| Saves the time of senior staff, who check the vouchers only once a period | It earns no interest and is not available for other uses |
| The main cash book is not cluttered with small payments | Counting, locking away and insuring cash takes time and costs money |
| Analysis columns give totals for each type of expense | Payments without proper vouchers are hard to trace |

The imprest system reduces the risks: the float is small, every payment needs a voucher, and the cash plus vouchers can be checked against the imprest at any time.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Restoring the float with the wrong amount"}
The amount given back is the amount **spent** (imprest − balance), not the full imprest.
:::

:::mistake{title="Analysis totals that do not agree"}
The analysis column totals must add up to the total column. If they do not, an amount is missing from one column.
:::

:::mistake{title="Posting the ledger column to an expense account"}
A payment to a supplier reduces a liability: debit the supplier's personal account, not an expense.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Show the cross-check of the analysis totals and the calculation "imprest − balance = amount to restore". Both earn marks and catch your own errors.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "evaluate" questions on cash on the premises, give a benefit and a limitation and finish with a short judgement, for example that the imprest system keeps the risk small.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Petty cash pays small everyday expenses; each payment needs a signed, authorised voucher.
- Under the imprest system the float is restored to a fixed amount by giving back exactly what was spent.
- The analysed petty cash book has a total column and analysis columns that must cross-check.
- Analysis totals are debited to expense accounts; ledger column items to suppliers' accounts; the restoration is credited to bank.
- Keeping cash is convenient but risky; the imprest system and vouchers control the risk.
:::
