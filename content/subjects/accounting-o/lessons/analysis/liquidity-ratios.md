## Introduction

Farai's electrical shop in Harare made a good profit last year, yet in March he could not pay a supplier on time and lost his credit terms. How can a profitable business run short of money? Profit measures how much the business earned over a year; **liquidity** measures whether it has enough cash, or assets that will soon become cash, to pay the debts that fall due in the next few months.

Suppliers, banks and owners all watch liquidity. A supplier deciding whether to give credit, or a bank deciding on an overdraft, will look at two ratios built from the current assets and current liabilities in the statement of financial position. ZIMSEC (Form 4) and Cambridge 0452 both examine them, and both expect the answer **as a ratio**, for example 1.8 : 1, not as a percentage.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
The **current ratio** is also called the **working capital ratio**. ZIMSEC usually calls the second ratio the **quick ratio**; Cambridge 0452 calls it the **acid test ratio** (also known as the liquid ratio). The formula is the same.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Liquidity"}
The ability of a business to pay its short-term debts as they fall due.
:::

:::definition{term="Working capital (net current assets)"}
Current assets minus current liabilities.
:::

:::definition{term="Current ratio (working capital ratio)"}
Current assets divided by current liabilities, expressed as x : 1.
:::

:::definition{term="Acid test ratio (quick or liquid ratio)"}
Current assets less inventory, divided by current liabilities, expressed as x : 1.
:::

## The formulas

| Ratio | Formula | Expressed as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Current ratio | Current assets ÷ current liabilities | x : 1 |
| Acid test (quick) ratio | (Current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities | x : 1 |

Inventory is removed in the acid test because it is the least liquid current asset: goods must first be sold, often on credit, and then the customer must pay. The acid test therefore asks a harder question: could the business pay its current liabilities **without** selling any more inventory?

Write the answer to two decimal places followed by ": 1", for example 2.48 : 1. The second number is always 1.

## Interpreting the ratios

There is no single correct level; it depends on the type of business. A supermarket that sells for cash and turns its inventory over quickly can safely run with lower ratios than a furniture shop that holds slow-moving goods. As a rough guide often used in textbooks:

- A current ratio **below about 1 : 1** means current liabilities are greater than current assets: the business may struggle to pay its debts.
- An acid test **below about 1 : 1** means the business relies on selling inventory to pay its debts.
- **Very high** ratios are not ideal either. They may mean too much money is tied up in inventory or receivables, or cash is lying idle instead of being used to earn a return.

The **trend** matters more than one year's figure: a current ratio falling year after year is a warning sign even if it is still above 1 : 1.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: Farai's Electricals]
**Extract from the statement of financial position at 31 December 2025**

|  | US\$ | US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| **Current assets** |  |  |
| Inventory | 18,600 |  |
| Trade receivables | 9,400 |  |
| Other receivables (prepaid insurance) | 600 |  |
| Bank | 3,400 |  |
|  |  | 32,000 |
| **Current liabilities** |  |  |
| Trade payables | 12,300 |  |
| Other payables (wages owing) | 500 |  |
|  |  | 12,800 |
| Net current assets (working capital) |  | 19,200 |

1. Current ratio = 32,000 ÷ 12,800 = **2.50 : 1**.
2. Liquid assets = 32,000 − 18,600 = 13,400.
3. Acid test = 13,400 ÷ 12,800 = 1.046… = **1.05 : 1**.

Farai has US\$2.50 of current assets for every US\$1 he owes in the short term, which looks comfortable. But inventory is more than half of his current assets. Without it he has only just over US\$1 for each US\$1 owed, and only US\$3,400 is actually in the bank. This explains why he struggled to pay a supplier: his liquidity depends on selling electrical goods quickly.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: how transactions change the current ratio]
Rudo's current assets are US\$24,000 and her current liabilities US\$12,000: a current ratio of 24,000 ÷ 12,000 = **2.00 : 1**.

1. She pays a supplier US\$4,000 from the bank. Current assets fall to 20,000 and current liabilities fall to 8,000. New ratio = 20,000 ÷ 8,000 = **2.50 : 1**. Working capital is unchanged at US\$12,000, but the ratio rises because both figures fell by the same amount.
2. She then buys inventory of US\$3,000 on credit. Current assets become 23,000 and current liabilities 11,000. Ratio = 23,000 ÷ 11,000 = 2.0909… = **2.09 : 1**.

The lesson: when the ratio is above 1 : 1, a transaction that changes current assets and current liabilities by the same amount moves the ratio, even though working capital does not change. Receiving cash from a customer changes neither (one current asset replaces another).
:::

## Improving liquidity

A business with poor liquidity can:

- ask the owner to introduce more capital, or take a **long-term** loan (a non-current liability) instead of relying on an overdraft;
- sell surplus inventory, even at reduced prices, and buy smaller quantities more often;
- collect debts faster, for example by offering cash discounts for prompt payment;
- negotiate longer credit from suppliers;
- sell unused non-current assets;
- reduce drawings.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving the answer as a percentage"}
Liquidity ratios are written x : 1, for example 1.35 : 1. "135%" or "1.35" alone may lose the mark.
:::

:::mistake{title="Deducting the wrong item in the acid test"}
Only inventory is removed. Trade receivables, prepayments and bank stay in the liquid assets.
:::

:::mistake{title="Including non-current items"}
A long-term loan is not a current liability, and machinery is not a current asset. Use only the current sections of the statement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating an overdraft as a negative asset"}
A bank overdraft is a current liability. Add it to current liabilities; do not subtract it from current assets.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Show the figures you used: "32,000 ÷ 12,800 = 2.50 : 1". If you have taken a wrong total from the statement, the examiner can still give the method mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to comment, compare with the previous year or another business, say whether liquidity has improved or worsened, and give a reason linked to the figures (for example "inventory has doubled while the bank balance has fallen").
:::

:::exam-tip
"Explain why the acid test is a better measure of liquidity than the current ratio" wants one idea: inventory may take time to sell and turn into cash.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Liquidity is the ability to pay short-term debts when they fall due; it is not the same as profit.
- Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities, written x : 1.
- Acid test (quick) ratio = (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities, written x : 1.
- Low ratios warn of difficulty paying debts; very high ratios suggest idle resources.
- Look at the trend and the type of business, not just one figure.
:::
