## Introduction

Rudo's clothing boutique in Mutare and her friend's boutique in Masvingo have similar sales. Rudo always seems to have new stock and money in the bank; her friend's shelves are full of last season's dresses and she is always chasing customers who owe her money. The difference is **efficiency**: how quickly goods are sold, how quickly customers pay, and how long the business takes to pay its own suppliers.

Efficiency ratios link the income statement (sales, cost of sales, purchases) with the working capital in the statement of financial position (inventory, trade receivables, trade payables). They explain many changes in liquidity. ZIMSEC (Form 4) examines the **rate of inventory (stock) turnover**. Cambridge 0452 examines that and three more, each measured in **days**.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC calls it the **rate of stock turnover** and lists it with the liquidity ratios; Cambridge 0452 calls it the **rate of inventory turnover**. **Inventory days, trade receivables turnover (days) and trade payables turnover (days)** are in the Cambridge syllabus only.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Rate of inventory turnover"}
The number of times in a year that a business sells and replaces its average inventory: cost of sales ÷ average inventory.
:::

:::definition{term="Average inventory"}
(Opening inventory + closing inventory) ÷ 2.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade receivables turnover (days)"}
The average number of days credit customers take to pay (the collection period).
:::

:::definition{term="Trade payables turnover (days)"}
The average number of days the business takes to pay its credit suppliers (the payment period).
:::

## The formulas

| Ratio | Formula | Expressed as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rate of inventory turnover | Cost of sales ÷ average inventory | times |
| Inventory turnover (days) (Cambridge) | Average inventory ÷ cost of sales × 365 | days |
| Trade receivables turnover (days) (Cambridge) | Trade receivables ÷ credit sales × 365 | days |
| Trade payables turnover (days) (Cambridge) | Trade payables ÷ credit purchases × 365 | days |

Notice the pattern: the "times" ratio has cost of sales on top, the "days" ratios have the balance on top and multiply by 365. Inventory days = 365 ÷ rate of inventory turnover. Use **cost of sales**, not revenue, with inventory, because inventory is valued at cost. Use **credit** sales and **credit** purchases for the receivables and payables ratios, because cash customers and cash suppliers never appear in those balances. If a question gives only total sales and says all sales are on credit, use that figure.

## What each ratio shows

- **Rate of inventory turnover.** A higher rate means goods sell faster: less money tied up in inventory and less risk of goods becoming damaged or out of fashion. A falling rate can mean slow-moving or obsolete inventory, or buying too much. What is "good" depends on the trade: a vegetable vendor turns inventory over far more often than a furniture shop.
- **Trade receivables days.** Compare with the credit period the business allows. If customers are given 30 days but take 55, credit control is weak; cash comes in late, and irrecoverable debts become more likely.
- **Trade payables days.** A longer period keeps cash in the business for longer, but paying too late damages relationships with suppliers and may lose cash discounts or credit altogether.

Ideally a business collects from its customers faster than it pays its suppliers.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: rate of inventory turnover]
Rudo's Boutique had inventory of US\$6,800 at 1 January 2025 and US\$10,200 at 31 December 2025. Cost of sales for 2025 was US\$61,200.

1. Average inventory = (6,800 + 10,200) ÷ 2 = 8,500.
2. Rate of inventory turnover = 61,200 ÷ 8,500 = **7.2 times**.
3. Inventory days (Cambridge) = 8,500 ÷ 61,200 × 365 = 50.69… = **51 days** (to the nearest day). As a check, 365 ÷ 7.2 = 50.7.

On average Rudo's stock stays on the shelf for about seven weeks before it is sold. If last year's rate was 9 times, she would want to know why sales have slowed or why she bought more: the closing inventory has risen by half.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: collection and payment periods (Cambridge)]
Tatenda sells building materials on credit in Chinhoyi. His credit sales for 2025 were US\$69,000 and his credit purchases US\$52,000. At 31 December 2025 trade receivables were US\$7,560 and trade payables US\$6,240. He allows customers 30 days' credit; his suppliers allow him 45 days.

1. Trade receivables days = 7,560 ÷ 69,000 × 365 = 39.99… = **40 days**.
2. Trade payables days = 6,240 ÷ 52,000 × 365 = 43.8 = **44 days**.

Customers take 10 days longer than the 30 days allowed, so Tatenda should tighten credit control: send statements promptly, chase overdue accounts and perhaps offer a small cash discount for early payment. He pays his suppliers in 44 days, just within their 45-day terms, so he is not damaging his relationship with them. Because he collects in 40 days and pays in 44, cash comes in slightly before it goes out, which helps his liquidity.
:::

## Improving efficiency

- **Inventory:** buy smaller quantities more often, stop stocking slow lines, run sales of old stock, and keep records of what sells.
- **Receivables:** check customers' creditworthiness, set credit limits, send invoices and statements on time, offer cash discounts, chase overdue debts.
- **Payables:** take the full credit period offered, but pay on time to keep discounts and goodwill.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using revenue with inventory"}
Inventory turnover uses cost of sales. Using revenue overstates the rate because revenue includes the profit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using closing inventory instead of average inventory"}
The syllabus formula uses average inventory: (opening + closing) ÷ 2. Using one figure only gives the wrong answer.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using total sales or total purchases"}
Receivables and payables days use credit sales and credit purchases. Cash transactions never create a receivable or payable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting × 365"}
Without multiplying by 365 the answer is a fraction of a year, not a number of days.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
State the unit: "7.2 times" or "40 days". Round days to the nearest whole day unless told otherwise.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to comment on receivables days, always compare with the credit period allowed if the question gives it; this is where the comment marks are.
:::

:::exam-tip
Link efficiency to liquidity in your comments: slow inventory turnover and slow collection both tie up cash and lower the acid test ratio.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Rate of inventory turnover = cost of sales ÷ average inventory (times); average inventory = (opening + closing) ÷ 2.
- Cambridge only: inventory days = average inventory ÷ cost of sales × 365.
- Cambridge only: trade receivables days = trade receivables ÷ credit sales × 365.
- Cambridge only: trade payables days = trade payables ÷ credit purchases × 365.
- Faster inventory turnover and faster collection free cash; paying suppliers late risks goodwill and discounts.
:::
