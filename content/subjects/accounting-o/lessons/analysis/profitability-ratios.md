## Introduction

Tendai's hardware shop in Gweru made a profit of US\$14,400 last year. Is that good? The answer depends on how big the business is. US\$14,400 on sales of US\$50,000 is excellent; on sales of US\$500,000 it is poor. **Ratios** turn raw figures into percentages and proportions so that results can be compared fairly: with last year, with a target, or with another business.

Profitability ratios measure how well a business turns sales, and the money invested in it, into profit. ZIMSEC teaches them in Form 4 under accounting ratios (mark-up, margin and net profit percentage). Cambridge 0452 includes the same three plus **return on capital employed (ROCE)**. Both boards expect you to calculate a ratio correctly, state it in the right form, and say what it shows. Cambridge accepts only the formulas printed in its syllabus, so learn them exactly as they appear below.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC calls the profit margin the **net profit percentage** (net profit ÷ sales × 100). Cambridge calls it the **profit margin** (profit for the year ÷ revenue × 100). They are the same ratio. **ROCE** is in the Cambridge syllabus only.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Gross profit margin"}
Gross profit as a percentage of revenue: how many cents of gross profit each dollar of sales earns.
:::

:::definition{term="Mark-up"}
Gross profit as a percentage of cost of sales: how much is added to the cost of goods to set the selling price.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit margin (net profit percentage)"}
Profit for the year as a percentage of revenue: how many cents of profit remain from each dollar of sales after all expenses.
:::

:::definition{term="Return on capital employed (ROCE)"}
Profit before interest as a percentage of the capital employed in the business: the return earned on the long-term finance invested.
:::

## The formulas

| Ratio | Formula | Expressed as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gross profit margin | Gross profit ÷ revenue × 100 | % |
| Mark-up | Gross profit ÷ cost of sales × 100 | % |
| Profit margin (net profit percentage) | Profit for the year ÷ revenue × 100 | % |
| ROCE (Cambridge) | Profit for the year before interest ÷ capital employed × 100 | % |

For ROCE, Cambridge defines **capital employed = issued shares + reserves + non-current liabilities** for a limited company. For a sole trader the equivalent is the owner's capital plus non-current liabilities such as a long-term loan. Interest on those loans is added back to the profit, because the ratio measures the return on all the long-term finance, whoever provided it.

Revenue means **net** revenue: sales less sales returns. Give percentages to one decimal place unless the question says otherwise.

## What each ratio shows

- **Gross profit margin and mark-up** depend on buying and selling prices. They fall if suppliers raise prices and the business does not pass the increase on, if selling prices are cut, if inventory is stolen or damaged, or if the mix of goods sold shifts towards lower-margin lines.
- **Profit margin** also reflects the running expenses. If the gross profit margin is steady but the profit margin falls, expenses have grown faster than sales.
- **ROCE** compares profit with the whole investment. An owner can compare it with the interest a bank deposit would earn: if ROCE is lower, the risk of running the business may not be worth it.

Margin and mark-up describe the same gross profit in two ways. Because cost of sales is smaller than revenue, the mark-up is always the larger percentage.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: Tendai's Hardware]
Tendai's income statement for 2025:

**Tendai's Hardware**

**Income statement (statement of profit or loss)**

**for the year ended 31 December 2025**

|  | US\$ | US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue |  | 120,000 |
| Less: Cost of sales |  |  |
| Opening inventory | 9,000 |  |
| Purchases | 86,500 |  |
|  | 95,500 |  |
| Less: Closing inventory | (11,500) | (84,000) |
| **Gross profit** |  | 36,000 |
| Add: Other income |  |  |
| Rent received |  | 1,200 |
|  |  | 37,200 |
| Less: Expenses |  |  |
| Wages | 13,800 |  |
| Rent and rates | 4,500 |  |
| Electricity | 1,900 |  |
| Depreciation | 2,600 | (22,800) |
| **Profit for the year (net profit)** |  | 14,400 |

1. Gross profit margin = 36,000 ÷ 120,000 × 100 = **30.0%**. Each US\$1 of sales earns 30 cents of gross profit.
2. Mark-up = 36,000 ÷ 84,000 × 100 = 42.857… = **42.9%**. Tendai adds about 43% to the cost of his goods.
3. Profit margin = 14,400 ÷ 120,000 × 100 = **12.0%**. After all expenses (and the rent received), 12 cents of each US\$1 of sales is profit.

Note that the rent received is included in profit for the year but not in revenue: revenue is sales only.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: ROCE for a company (Cambridge)]
Mazowe Packaging Ltd has issued ordinary shares of US\$80,000, reserves (retained earnings) of US\$25,000 and a 12% loan of US\$15,000 repayable in 2030. Its profit for the year after loan interest of US\$1,800 was US\$16,200.

1. Profit before interest = 16,200 + 1,800 = 18,000.
2. Capital employed = 80,000 + 25,000 + 15,000 = 120,000.
3. ROCE = 18,000 ÷ 120,000 × 100 = **15.0%**.

If the directors could earn 6% by investing the same money safely, a 15% return suggests the business is using its finance well. Using the profit **after** interest (16,200) would give 13.5%, which understates the return because it leaves out the reward paid to the lender.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Dividing by the wrong figure"}
Margin divides by revenue; mark-up divides by cost of sales. Writing the formula before substituting stops this mistake.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using gross revenue when there are sales returns"}
Use revenue less sales returns. The examiner's revenue figure in a ratio question is the net figure.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using profit after interest for ROCE"}
The Cambridge formula uses profit for the year **before** interest. Add back loan or debenture interest.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving a ratio without saying what it means"}
"The margin is 30%" earns the calculation mark only. Questions that ask you to comment want a sentence: what the figure shows, whether it is better or worse, and a likely reason.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Write the formula, substitute the figures, then give the answer with the % sign and to one decimal place. Method marks are often available even if the arithmetic slips.
:::

:::exam-tip
In Cambridge Paper 1, every ratio option will be close to the right one. Check which figure is on the bottom of the fraction before choosing.
:::

:::exam-tip
A ZIMSEC question saying "net profit percentage" wants profit for the year ÷ sales × 100: the same as Cambridge's profit margin.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Gross profit margin = gross profit ÷ revenue × 100.
- Mark-up = gross profit ÷ cost of sales × 100; it is always larger than the margin.
- Profit margin (net profit percentage) = profit for the year ÷ revenue × 100.
- ROCE (Cambridge) = profit before interest ÷ capital employed × 100; capital employed = issued shares + reserves + non-current liabilities.
- A lower gross margin points to buying and selling prices; a lower profit margin with a steady gross margin points to expenses.
:::
