## Introduction

Farai is deciding whether to buy a transport business in Bulawayo. He has its income statement and statement of financial position for the last year, and the ratios look healthy. But the statements do not tell him that the business's best driver is about to retire, that its depot is worth three times what the books say, or that a new competitor is opening on the same route next month.

Financial statements are essential, but they have **limitations**. They record past transactions in money, at the prices paid at the time, using methods and estimates chosen by the business. Cambridge 0452 (topic 6.5) asks you to explain these limitations, usually as a short evaluation question after a set of ratios: "State **two** limitations of using the financial statements to assess the business." Good answers name the limitation and explain how it could mislead a user.

:::keyfact{title="Cambridge only"}
The limitations of accounting statements are in the Cambridge 0452 syllabus only. The underlying ideas (historic cost, money measurement, prudence) also appear in the accounting concepts that both boards examine.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Historic cost"}
Recording assets at the price originally paid for them, not at their current value.
:::

:::definition{term="Accounting policies"}
The methods a business chooses to apply, such as its depreciation method and rates or how it values inventory.
:::

:::definition{term="Accounting estimate"}
A judgement used in preparing the statements, such as the useful life of an asset or the proportion of receivables that may not pay.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-financial factors"}
Things that affect a business's success but cannot be measured reliably in money, such as staff skills, location, reputation and the economic climate.
:::

## Limitation 1: historic cost

Assets are recorded at what they cost. This is objective and can be checked against invoices, but over time it means:

- Land and buildings bought many years ago may be shown at a fraction of their current value, so capital employed is understated and **ROCE is overstated**.
- Two businesses with identical assets bought at different times show different values.
- When prices rise, depreciation based on old costs is too small to replace the assets, so profit may be overstated.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: historic cost and ROCE]
Rudo's bakery in Gweru has capital employed of US\$80,000, which includes premises bought in 2012 for US\$20,000. A valuer says the premises are now worth about US\$65,000. Profit for the year (no loans, so no interest) was US\$12,000.

1. ROCE at historic cost = 12,000 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = **15.0%**.
2. Capital employed if the premises were shown at current value = 80,000 − 20,000 + 65,000 = 125,000.
3. ROCE on current values = 12,000 ÷ 125,000 × 100 = 9.6%.

The statements make Rudo's return look much better than it really is: her money tied up in the bakery could be earning a return elsewhere on its true value.
:::

## Limitation 2: accounting policies and estimates

Many figures depend on choices and judgements:

- the **depreciation method** (straight line or reducing balance), the **rate**, and the estimated **useful life** and **residual value**;
- the size of the **allowance for irrecoverable debts**;
- how inventory is valued (at the lower of cost and net realisable value, which needs an estimate of selling price).

Different reasonable choices give different profits and asset values from the same transactions. Users comparing two businesses, or one business before and after a change of policy, may be misled. The consistency concept limits this within one business, but not between businesses.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: the effect of estimates]
Two new delivery businesses each have revenue of US\$150,000, gross profit of US\$41,000, other expenses of US\$22,500, a van costing US\$30,000 (no residual value) and trade receivables of US\$18,000 at the end of their first year.

- Business A estimates the van will last 5 years and makes an allowance of 2% for irrecoverable debts.
- Business B estimates 6 years and an allowance of 5%.

| | Business A | Business B |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Depreciation (30,000 ÷ life) (US\$) | 6,000 | 5,000 |
| Allowance for irrecoverable debts (US\$) | 360 | 900 |
| Profit for the year (US\$) | 12,140 | 12,600 |
| Profit margin | 8.1% | 8.4% |

1. A: 41,000 − 22,500 − 6,000 − 360 = 12,140; margin 12,140 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = 8.1%.
2. B: 41,000 − 22,500 − 5,000 − 900 = 12,600; margin 12,600 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = 8.4%.

Neither business is better run; they made different judgements. The difference would be larger with more assets or bigger receivables.
:::

## Limitation 3: non-financial factors

Accounting records only items that can be measured in money (the money measurement concept). Many things that matter to the future of a business are left out:

- **Workforce:** the skills, experience, loyalty and motivation of staff; dependence on one key person.
- **Location:** access to customers, transport links, security, rent reviews.
- **Economic climate:** inflation, exchange rates, interest rates, droughts affecting farming customers, power cuts.
- **Competition and market changes:** a new competitor, changes in customer tastes or technology.
- **Reputation and customer loyalty**, quality of products and service, relationships with suppliers.
- **Environmental and social impact**, which customers and lenders increasingly consider.

## Other limitations

- Statements describe the **past**; users want to predict the **future**.
- They are prepared at **one date**, which may not be typical (for example, just before a busy season).
- Published or summarised statements give limited detail to outsiders.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Naming a limitation without explaining it"}
"Historic cost" alone earns little. Add the effect: "assets bought long ago may be undervalued, so ROCE is overstated".
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving financial items as non-financial factors"}
"Low profit" or "high expenses" are in the statements. Non-financial factors are things like staff skills, location and the economy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the statements are wrong"}
Historic cost and estimates are accepted practice, not errors. The point is that they limit what the statements can show.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Use the business in the question: "The premises were bought in 2008, so their value is likely to be understated" scores better than a general statement.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked for non-financial factors, give one from each area (people, place, economy) to make sure your points are distinct.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Historic cost records assets at original cost: values may be out of date and ROCE flattered.
- Accounting policies and estimates (depreciation, allowances, inventory) change reported profit and assets.
- Statements omit non-financial factors: workforce skills, location, economic climate, competition, reputation.
- Statements are historical and prepared at one date.
- Cambridge 0452 only.
:::
