## Introduction

Thandeka is thinking of buying a share in one of two businesses in Mutare: a busy supermarket and a furniture shop. The supermarket's gross profit margin is 18%; the furniture shop's is 45%. Does that mean the furniture shop is the better business? Not necessarily. The two businesses sell different goods to different customers in different ways, so their ratios would be expected to differ even if both were equally well run.

Comparing a business with another one, rather than with its own past, is called **inter-business** (or inter-firm) comparison. It is useful to investors, lenders and owners who want to know whether their results are good for their type of business. Cambridge 0452 (topic 6.3) asks you to explain the **factors** that make two businesses' ratios differ and the **problems** of making the comparison. These questions usually appear as the evaluation part of a Paper 2 ratio question.

:::keyfact{title="Cambridge only"}
Inter-business comparison is in the Cambridge 0452 syllabus only. ZIMSEC questions compare a business with its own earlier years (see the lesson on interpreting financial statements). The ratios themselves are the same for both boards.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Inter-business comparison"}
Comparing the financial results and ratios of one business with those of another business, usually in the same trade, for the same period.
:::

:::definition{term="Accounting policy"}
A method a business chooses for recording and presenting items, such as its depreciation method or inventory valuation method.
:::

:::definition{term="Benchmark"}
A standard to compare against, such as the average ratios for businesses in the same trade.
:::

## Why two businesses' ratios differ

**1. Type of business.** A supermarket sells everyday goods quickly at low margins; a furniture or jewellery shop sells slowly at high margins. Service businesses have no inventory at all. Comparing ratios across different trades says little about which is better managed.

**2. Size.** A large business may buy in bulk at lower prices (higher margin) and spread its fixed expenses over more sales (higher profit margin). A small business may have higher costs per dollar of sales.

**3. Location.** Rent, wages and customer spending vary between a city centre in Harare and a small town, and between a main road and a back street.

**4. How the business is financed.** A business financed by loans pays interest, which reduces profit for the year; one financed by the owner's capital does not. Capital employed and liquidity ratios differ as a result.

**5. Accounting policies.** Different depreciation methods and rates, or different inventory valuation methods, give different profits and asset values from identical activities.

**6. Age of non-current assets.** A business with old, mostly depreciated equipment shows a low capital employed and therefore a high ROCE, even though it may soon need to spend heavily on replacements.

**7. Business strategy.** One business may choose high volume at low prices, another low volume at high prices; both can be successful.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: different types of business]
Two businesses in Mutare report the following for 2025:

| | Supermarket | Furniture shop |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue (US\$) | 960,000 | 240,000 |
| Cost of sales (US\$) | 787,200 | 132,000 |
| Opening inventory (US\$) | 31,000 | 42,000 |
| Closing inventory (US\$) | 34,600 | 46,000 |
| Gross profit margin | 18.0% | 45.0% |
| Rate of inventory turnover | 24.0 times | 3.0 times |

1. Supermarket gross margin = (960,000 − 787,200) ÷ 960,000 × 100 = 18.0%; inventory turnover = 787,200 ÷ 32,800 = 24.0 times.
2. Furniture shop gross margin = 108,000 ÷ 240,000 × 100 = 45.0%; inventory turnover = 132,000 ÷ 44,000 = 3.0 times.

The supermarket sells its average inventory about every two weeks at a thin margin; the furniture shop holds each item for about four months and needs a high margin to cover the cost of holding it. Neither set of ratios is "better". Each should be compared with businesses of its own type.
:::

## Problems of comparing two businesses

- **Different accounting policies:** straight-line versus reducing balance depreciation, or different rates, change profit and asset values.
- **Different year ends:** a shop whose year ends just after the festive season holds less inventory than one whose year ends in November.
- **Different sizes:** percentages help, but economies of scale still affect the ratios.
- **Different types of business or product mix:** two "hardware" shops may sell very different ranges.
- **Only one year's data:** a single year may be unusual (a fire, a new branch, a drought affecting farming customers). Trends over several years are more reliable.
- **Limited information:** outsiders see only the published statements, not the detailed records, and sole traders do not publish accounts at all.
- **Historic cost:** assets bought at different times are recorded at different price levels.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: the effect of depreciation policy]
Two hardware shops in Bulawayo are identical: each has revenue of US\$200,000, gross profit of US\$60,000, other expenses of US\$34,000 and equipment that cost US\$40,000 a year ago. Neither has any loans.

- Shop A depreciates equipment at 10% per year straight line: US\$4,000. Its capital employed is US\$100,000.
- Shop B depreciates at 25% per year reducing balance: US\$10,000 in the first year. Its capital employed is US\$94,000.

1. Profit for the year: A = 60,000 − 34,000 − 4,000 = 22,000; B = 60,000 − 34,000 − 10,000 = 16,000.
2. Profit margin: A = 22,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = **11.0%**; B = 16,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = **8.0%**.
3. ROCE: A = 22,000 ÷ 100,000 × 100 = **22.0%**; B = 16,000 ÷ 94,000 × 100 = **17.0%**.

The shops trade identically, yet A appears more profitable on every measure. An investor comparing them must first check that they use the same accounting policies, or adjust the figures so that they do.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Concluding that a higher margin means a better business"}
A higher margin may simply reflect a different type of business or strategy. Look at the whole picture: margins, turnover, profit and return on capital.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing problems without explaining them"}
"Different accounting policies" earns more when you add how: "for example, a higher depreciation charge lowers profit and ROCE".
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the non-financial side"}
Location, staff skills and reputation affect future results but do not appear in the ratios.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Questions often say "State **two** factors that may cause the ratios of the two businesses to differ" and then "Explain **one** problem of comparing them". Keep factors (reasons for real differences) separate from problems (reasons the comparison may mislead).
:::

:::exam-tip
Tie your answer to the businesses in the question: if one is newer, mention the age of its assets; if their year ends differ, say so.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Inter-business comparison judges a business against others, ideally in the same trade.
- Ratios differ because of type of business, size, location, financing, accounting policies, asset age and strategy.
- Problems: different policies, year ends, sizes, types of business, one year's data, limited information and historic cost.
- Compare like with like, use several years, and check accounting policies before drawing conclusions.
- Cambridge 0452 only; ZIMSEC compares a business with its own past results.
:::
