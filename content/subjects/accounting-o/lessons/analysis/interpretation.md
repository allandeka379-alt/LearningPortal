## Introduction

Calculating a ratio is only half the job. Tendai, who owns a building-supplies business in Gweru, does not want to be told that his gross profit margin is 26.0%; he wants to know whether that is good, why it has changed, and what he should do about it. Interpretation turns numbers into advice.

ZIMSEC asks candidates to "interpret financial statements for the benefit of their users" (Form 3) and to use ratios in Form 4. Cambridge 0452 asks you to compare results for different years, interpret the ratios, and suggest ways of improving profitability, liquidity and working capital. In both Paper 2s the comment and advice parts carry a large share of the marks, and they are where many candidates lose them by writing vague sentences.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC: **net profit percentage**, **quick ratio**, **rate of stock turnover**. Cambridge 0452: **profit margin**, **acid test ratio**, **rate of inventory turnover**. The interpretation is the same.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Interpretation"}
Explaining what financial results and ratios show, why they have changed, and what the users of the statements should do as a result.
:::

:::definition{term="Trend"}
The direction in which a figure or ratio moves over two or more periods.
:::

:::definition{term="Working capital"}
Current assets less current liabilities: the resources available to run the business day to day.
:::

## A method for comments

For each ratio, write three things:

1. **Direction and size:** "The gross profit margin fell from 30.0% to 26.0%."
2. **Possible cause**, linked to the figures: "Cost of sales rose by 26.9% while revenue rose by only 20%, so buying prices may have risen without being passed on."
3. **Effect or advice:** "Tendai should look for cheaper suppliers or review his selling prices."

Group your comments by type of ratio: profitability, then liquidity, then efficiency. Finish with an overall conclusion if the question asks you to evaluate.

## What changes gross profit?

- **Selling prices:** cutting prices to win customers lowers the margin, even if more is sold.
- **Buying prices:** if suppliers charge more and prices are not raised, the margin falls. Bulk discounts raise it.
- **Sales volume:** selling more at the same margin raises gross profit in dollars but not the margin percentage.
- **Sales mix:** selling more low-margin goods lowers the overall margin.
- **Inventory valuation:** an overstated closing inventory makes cost of sales too low and gross profit too high (and next year's too low). Theft or damage of goods raises cost of sales.

## What changes profit for the year?

Profit for the year also depends on what happens below gross profit:

- **Other income** such as rent received, commission received and discount received adds to profit.
- **Expenses** (wages, rent, electricity, depreciation, irrecoverable debts, interest) reduce it. If the gross profit margin is steady but the profit margin falls, expenses have grown faster than revenue.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: comparing two years]
Extracts from Tendai's statements:

|  | 2024 US\$ | 2025 US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue | 150,000 | 180,000 |
| Cost of sales | 105,000 | 133,200 |
| Gross profit | 45,000 | 46,800 |
| Expenses | 27,000 | 30,600 |
| Profit for the year | 18,000 | 16,200 |
| Inventory at 1 January | 13,000 | 14,000 |
| Inventory at 31 December | 14,000 | 22,000 |
| Current assets at 31 December | 30,000 | 36,800 |
| Current liabilities at 31 December | 15,000 | 23,000 |

| Ratio | 2024 | 2025 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gross profit margin | 30.0% | 26.0% |
| Mark-up | 42.9% | 35.1% |
| Profit margin (net profit percentage) | 12.0% | 9.0% |
| Current ratio | 2.00 : 1 | 1.60 : 1 |
| Acid test (quick) ratio | 1.07 : 1 | 0.64 : 1 |
| Rate of inventory turnover | 7.8 times | 7.4 times |

**Profitability.** Revenue grew by 20%, but the gross profit margin fell from 30.0% to 26.0% and the mark-up from 42.9% to 35.1%. Tendai has probably cut prices to win sales, or his suppliers have raised prices and he has not passed the increase on. The profit margin fell from 12.0% to 9.0%, more than the gross margin, because expenses also rose (by 13.3%). Profit for the year fell by US\$1,800 despite higher sales.

**Liquidity.** The current ratio fell from 2.00 : 1 to 1.60 : 1 and the acid test from 1.07 : 1 to 0.64 : 1. Without selling inventory Tendai now has only 64 cents for each dollar he owes in the short term.

**Efficiency.** Closing inventory rose from US\$14,000 to US\$22,000 and the rate of inventory turnover slowed from 7.8 to 7.4 times. Money is tied up in stock, which explains much of the fall in the acid test.

**Advice.** Review selling prices and suppliers to restore the margin; control expenses; buy smaller quantities and clear slow-moving stock to release cash.
:::

## Improving profitability, liquidity and working capital

**Profitability:** raise selling prices where customers will accept it; find cheaper suppliers or bulk discounts; promote high-margin lines; reduce waste and theft; cut unnecessary expenses (for example energy use, unprofitable delivery routes).

**Liquidity and working capital:** introduce more capital or take a long-term loan rather than an overdraft; reduce inventory; collect debts faster; negotiate longer credit from suppliers; sell unused non-current assets; reduce drawings. Note that buying non-current assets out of the bank account reduces working capital.

## Why profit and cash are different

A business can be profitable yet short of cash, or have cash while making a loss, because:

- **Capital expenditure** (a new van) uses cash but only its depreciation reduces profit.
- **Depreciation** reduces profit but uses no cash.
- **Drawings** take cash out but are not an expense.
- **Credit sales and purchases**: revenue is earned when goods are sold, but cash arrives when customers pay. More receivables means profit without cash.
- **Inventory** bought but not yet sold uses cash without affecting profit.
- **Loans and capital introduced** bring in cash but are not income.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: profitable but the bank balance fell]
Nyasha's minimart in Kwekwe made a profit of US\$12,000 in 2025, but her bank balance fell by US\$3,000. Her accountant explains the difference:

| Item | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Profit for the year | 12,000 |
| Add: Depreciation (an expense that uses no cash) | 2,000 |
| Less: Cash spent on a new van (capital expenditure) | (9,000) |
| Less: Drawings | (6,000) |
| Less: Increase in inventory | (2,500) |
| Less: Increase in trade receivables | (1,500) |
| Add: Increase in trade payables | 2,000 |
| **Decrease in bank balance** | (3,000) |

1. Start with profit and add back depreciation, which reduced profit but not cash: 14,000.
2. Deduct cash used for things that are not expenses: the van and drawings (15,000 in total).
3. Adjust for working capital: more inventory and receivables used cash; more payables saved cash.
4. 14,000 − 15,000 − 2,500 − 1,500 + 2,000 = −3,000.

The business is profitable. The cash shortage comes mainly from buying the van out of the bank account and from drawings.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing without explaining"}
"The current ratio went down" earns little. Say by how much, why (using the figures), and what it means.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing profitability with liquidity"}
A falling profit margin is not a liquidity problem, and a low acid test does not mean the business is unprofitable. Keep the two ideas apart.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying revenue rose so the business did better"}
Higher revenue can come with lower margins and lower profit, as in Tendai's case. Look at the percentages.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Quote the ratios you are commenting on with both years' figures. It shows the examiner you are using the data, which is what the comment marks reward.
:::

:::exam-tip
Make suggestions specific to the business in the question: a vegetable vendor and a furniture shop need different advice on inventory.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to "evaluate" or "advise", end with a clear conclusion (for example "overall, profitability and liquidity have both worsened").
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Comment on direction and size, cause, and effect or advice for each ratio.
- Gross profit depends on selling prices, buying prices, sales mix, volume and inventory valuation.
- Profit for the year also depends on other income and expenses.
- Liquidity improves with more long-term finance, less inventory, faster collection and longer supplier credit.
- Profit and cash differ because of capital expenditure, depreciation, drawings, credit, inventory and loans.
:::
