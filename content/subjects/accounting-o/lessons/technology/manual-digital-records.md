## Introduction

Farai's uniform business in Mutare sells blazers and tracksuits on credit to schools, clinics and lodges. For years his assistant wrote every invoice by hand into a hard-covered sales journal, added the columns with a calculator and copied each amount into the customers' ledger accounts. Now his nephew suggests a spreadsheet, and a supplier is selling a monthly subscription to accounting software. Which should he use?

Both syllabuses expect you to name the methods of processing accounting data and to weigh their advantages and disadvantages. ZIMSEC calls them **manual** and **electronic** methods and asks Form 3 students to demonstrate both; Cambridge asks you to **evaluate manual and digital methods for the original entry of transactions**. You will not be asked about any particular software package, only about the general features.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Manual accounting"}
Recording transactions by hand in paper books: journals, cash book, ledger and trial balance, with totals worked out by the book-keeper.
:::

:::definition{term="Electronic (digital or computerised) accounting"}
Recording and processing transactions on a computer, tablet or phone, using a spreadsheet or accounting software that performs calculations and postings automatically.
:::

:::definition{term="Spreadsheet"}
A digital grid of rows and columns in which figures can be entered and formulas used to calculate totals, balances and ratios automatically.
:::

:::definition{term="Accounting software"}
A program in which each transaction is entered once; the software then updates the books of prime entry, the ledger accounts, the trial balance and the financial statements.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC speaks of **electronic** methods of processing data; Cambridge speaks of **digital** methods. ZIMSEC's **subsidiary books** are Cambridge's **books of prime entry**. The ideas are the same.
:::

## How each method works

In a **manual** system every stage of the accounting cycle is done by a person. The book-keeper writes each source document into the correct book of prime entry, adds the columns, posts every item to the ledger, balances each account and lists the balances in the trial balance. Each figure is written several times, and each copying is a chance for error.

In a **digital** system the data are keyed in once, normally at the original entry stage. With a **spreadsheet**, formulas add columns and carry balances forward; the user still decides where each figure goes. With **accounting software**, entering a sales invoice automatically debits the customer, credits sales, updates the trial balance and changes the draft income statement. Many systems also produce invoices, statements of account and reminders, and some can import bank transactions directly.

:::worked-solution{title="Example 1: Farai's sales journal as a spreadsheet"}
Farai's nephew sets up the September 2025 sales journal in a spreadsheet. Row 1 holds the headings; column D holds the invoice amounts.

| Row | A: Date | B: Customer | C: Invoice | D: Amount US\$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| 2 | 2 Sep | Kudzai Primary School | S101 | 640 |
| 3 | 5 Sep | Hope Clinic | S102 | 385 |
| 4 | 11 Sep | Mutare Lodge | S103 | 1,120 |
| 5 | 18 Sep | Kudzai Primary School | S104 | 275 |
| 6 | 26 Sep | Tafara Stores | S105 | 930 |
| 7 | 30 Sep | **Total** |  | **3,350** |

1. Cell D7 contains the formula `=SUM(D2:D6)`, so the total of US\$3,350 is calculated automatically. If invoice S103 is corrected later, the total changes by itself.
2. A filter on column B shows that Kudzai Primary School was invoiced 640 + 275 = US\$915 in the month, which is the amount to debit to its account.
3. The total of US\$3,350 is posted to the credit of the sales account. In accounting software this posting, and the debits to the five customer accounts, would happen automatically.
4. The spreadsheet does **not** check that the right amount was typed: if 1,120 had been keyed as 1,210, the formula would add the wrong figure perfectly.
:::

## Advantages and limitations

| | Manual methods | Digital methods |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost | Cheap: books and pens | Hardware, software or subscriptions, electricity, internet |
| Speed | Slow; every posting written by hand | Fast; one entry updates many records |
| Accuracy | Arithmetic and copying errors are common | Calculations are automatic, but input errors still pass through |
| Skills | Basic book-keeping knowledge | Training in the program is also needed |
| Reports | Prepared by hand at the period end | Statements, aged receivables and other reports at any time |
| Power and failure | Work continues during power cuts | Needs electricity; data can be lost if not backed up |
| Security | Books can be lost, stolen or damaged by fire or water | Passwords and access levels, but risk of viruses and hacking |
| Storage | Many files and books take space | Large amounts stored on a device or online |

For the **original entry** of transactions, digital methods save time when there are many similar transactions, such as credit sales to regular customers, and they can generate the source document (invoice) at the same moment the transaction is recorded. Manual entry remains sensible for a very small business, for example a market vendor with a handful of daily cash sales, or as a back-up where power cuts are frequent.

For the **ledger**, digital methods remove most arithmetic errors because balances are calculated, not added by hand. However, the principle of **garbage in, garbage out** applies: a wrong figure or wrong account chosen at the input stage is carried into every later record and statement.

:::worked-solution{title="Example 2: is the software worth it for Farai?"}
Farai's assistant spends about 40 hours a month on the manual books and is paid US\$2.50 an hour. With accounting software the work would take about 12 hours a month, the subscription is US\$35 a month, and a laptop costs US\$480.

| Monthly cost | Manual US\$ | Software US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Assistant's time | 100 | 30 |
| Subscription | 0 | 35 |
| **Total** | **100** | **65** |

1. Manual: 40 × 2.50 = US\$100. Software: 12 × 2.50 + 35 = US\$65.
2. Monthly saving: 100 − 65 = US\$35.
3. The laptop would be paid for by the savings in 480 ÷ 35 = 13.7 months, so a little over a year.
4. Other factors: faster statements to schools, fewer errors, and reports on overdue accounts; but Farai must also plan for power cuts, training and backing up the data.
:::

## Choosing a method

There is no single right answer: it depends on the number of transactions, the cost, the skills of the staff and the reliability of power and internet. Many small Zimbabwean businesses use a mixture, for example a receipt book for cash takings and a spreadsheet for credit customers. Whichever method is used, the accounting principles are the same: every transaction needs a source document, a debit and a credit.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a computer makes the records error-free"}
Software removes arithmetic errors, but not input errors such as the wrong amount, the wrong account or a missing invoice.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving one-word answers"}
"Fast" or "expensive" is not enough. Explain: "Digital methods are faster because one entry updates the journal, the ledger and the trial balance at once."
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming a software package"}
The syllabuses do not examine specific programs. Describe features such as automatic posting, formulas and reports instead.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In an "evaluate" question, give advantages **and** limitations of the method, then a conclusion linked to the business in the question (its size, number of transactions, staff skills, power supply).
:::

:::exam-tip
Remember the phrase **garbage in, garbage out**: it is the best single argument that digital records still need careful original entry.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Manual methods record every stage by hand; digital methods record each transaction once and process it automatically.
- Spreadsheets use formulas to calculate totals and balances; accounting software also posts to the ledger and prepares statements.
- Digital methods are faster, give reports at any time and avoid arithmetic errors, but cost money, need power, training and back-ups, and still suffer from input errors.
- Manual methods are cheap and work without power, but are slow and error-prone.
- The best choice depends on the size and circumstances of the business.
:::
