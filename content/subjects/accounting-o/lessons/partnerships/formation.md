## Introduction

Rudo has run a small broiler project in Chinhoyi for three years. Demand from butcheries and school tuck shops keeps growing, but she cannot afford a second fowl run, and she has no one to take over when she travels to buy day-old chicks. Her friend Farai has savings, some equipment and experience of selling to hotels. Instead of borrowing from a bank, Rudo and Farai decide to go into business **together**. They have formed a **partnership**.

Partnerships are common in Zimbabwe: transport operators sharing kombis, accountants and lawyers in practice together, family hardware shops and farming projects. In the exam you must be able to explain why a partnership is formed, weigh up its advantages and disadvantages, and list what the partners should agree in writing. That agreement then drives every calculation in the appropriation and current accounts that follow, so this lesson is the foundation for the rest of the topic.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards use **partnership**, **partnership agreement**, **interest on capital**, **interest on drawings**, **partners' salaries** and **profit-sharing ratio**. ZIMSEC papers usually say **net profit** where Cambridge says **profit for the year**. Neither board examines dissolution, the admission or retirement of a partner, or changes in the profit-sharing ratio.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Partnership"}
A business owned by two or more people who carry it on together with a view to making a profit, sharing the profits and the risks.
:::

:::definition{term="Partnership agreement"}
A written (or spoken) agreement between the partners that sets out how the partnership will be financed and run, and how profits and losses will be shared.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit-sharing ratio"}
The proportions in which the partners share the profit or loss left after interest on capital, salaries and interest on drawings, for example 3 : 2.
:::

:::definition{term="Unlimited liability"}
The owners are personally responsible for all the debts of the business; if the business cannot pay, the partners' private possessions can be used.
:::

## Why form a partnership?

A sole trader who wants to grow usually meets three limits: too little **capital**, too few **skills**, and too much **work and risk** for one person. Bringing in a partner deals with all three:

- **More capital.** Each partner contributes money or assets, so the business can buy more equipment or inventory without a loan.
- **More skills and ideas.** One partner may be good at production, another at selling or keeping records.
- **Shared workload and decisions.** The business can keep running when one partner is ill or away.
- **Shared losses.** A loss is borne by all the partners, not by one person.
- **Easy to set up.** Unlike a limited company, no registration of shares is needed, and the accounts stay private.

The disadvantages are just as important:

- **Unlimited liability** (in an ordinary partnership): each partner can lose personal possessions to pay the business's debts, including debts caused by the other partner.
- **Shared profits.** Each partner receives only part of the profit.
- **Disagreements.** Partners may argue over decisions, workload or drawings.
- **Limited capital compared with a company.** The partners cannot raise money by selling shares to the public.
- **Lack of continuity.** The death or departure of a partner can bring the partnership to an end.

## The partnership agreement

To avoid arguments, partners should put their arrangements in writing. A partnership agreement usually covers:

1. **Capital**: how much each partner contributes.
2. **Profit-sharing ratio**: how the remaining profit or loss is divided.
3. **Interest on capital**: a percentage paid on each partner's capital, which rewards the partner who has invested more.
4. **Interest on drawings**: a charge on money taken out during the year, which discourages early or heavy drawings.
5. **Partners' salaries**: an amount for a partner who works more in the business than the others.
6. **Interest on partners' loans**: the rate paid when a partner lends money to the business in addition to capital.
7. Other matters such as how much each partner may draw, the duties of each partner, and how accounts are kept and checked.

Interest on capital, salaries and interest on drawings are **not** expenses of the business. They are ways of **sharing out the profit**, so they appear in the appropriation account, not in the income statement. Interest on a **loan** from a partner is different: it is the cost of borrowed money and is treated as an **expense** in the income statement, exactly as interest on a bank loan would be.

### When there is no agreement

If the partners never agreed these matters, the rules generally applied in the exam (taken from partnership law, such as the UK Partnership Act 1890 that many textbooks follow) are: profits and losses are shared **equally**; no interest on capital; no salaries; no interest on drawings; and interest on a partner's loan at 5% a year. Read the question carefully: a stated agreement always overrides these rules.

## Recording the formation

When the partnership starts, each partner's contribution is credited to a **separate capital account**. Cash or bank, and any assets brought in, are debited.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: forming R & F Poultry]
On 1 January 2025 Rudo and Farai formed R & F Poultry. Rudo paid US\$12,000 into the business bank account and brought in a delivery van valued at US\$9,500. Farai paid US\$8,000 into the bank and brought in equipment valued at US\$4,500.

1. Debit each asset received: bank 12,000 + 8,000 = 20,000; motor van 9,500; equipment 4,500.
2. Credit each partner's capital account with the total value he or she brought in: Rudo 12,000 + 9,500 = 21,500; Farai 8,000 + 4,500 = 12,500.
3. Check: debits 34,000 = credits 34,000.

## Why the agreement matters: a worked comparison

Partners often contribute different amounts of capital and different amounts of work. An agreement lets the share of profit reflect both.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: sharing the 2025 profit of R & F Poultry]
The agreement states: interest on capital at 5% a year; Farai, who does all the selling, receives a salary of US\$3,000; the remaining profit is shared Rudo 3 : Farai 2. The profit for 2025 was US\$18,400.

1. Interest on capital: Rudo 5% × 21,500 = 1,075; Farai 5% × 12,500 = 625. Total 1,700.
2. Salary: Farai 3,000.
3. Residual profit: 18,400 − 1,700 − 3,000 = 13,700.
4. Share 3 : 2: Rudo 13,700 × 3/5 = 8,220; Farai 13,700 × 2/5 = 5,480.

|  | Rudo US\$ | Farai US\$ | Total US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |

The total shared out is always the profit for the year. The agreement only changes **who** receives it: Rudo is rewarded for her larger capital and Farai for his extra work.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating interest on capital or salaries as expenses"}
They are appropriations of profit. They go in the appropriation account, after the profit for the year has been calculated.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating interest on a partner's loan as an appropriation"}
A partner's loan is a liability of the business. The interest on it is an expense in the income statement, deducted before the profit for the year.
:::

:::mistake{title="Applying the profit-sharing ratio to the whole profit"}
The ratio is applied only to what is left after interest on capital and salaries have been deducted and interest on drawings added.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming partners have limited liability"}
In an ordinary partnership every partner has unlimited liability for the debts of the business.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Advantages and disadvantages" questions are usually comparisons: of a partnership with a sole trader (more capital, shared work, but shared profit and disagreements) or with a limited company (easier to form, private, but unlimited liability and less capital). Name the comparison in your answer.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked for the contents of a partnership agreement, give items that affect the accounts: capital, profit-sharing ratio, interest on capital, interest on drawings, salaries and interest on loans.
:::

:::exam-tip
If a question says "there is no partnership agreement", share the profit equally and give no interest on capital and no salaries.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A partnership is owned by two or more people who share its profits, risks and decisions.
- Partnerships bring more capital, more skills and shared work, but partners share profits, may disagree and have unlimited liability.
- The partnership agreement sets out capital, the profit-sharing ratio, interest on capital and drawings, salaries and interest on partners' loans.
- Interest on capital, salaries and interest on drawings are appropriations of profit; interest on a partner's loan is an expense.
- On formation, debit the assets brought in and credit each partner's capital account.
- Without an agreement, profits and losses are shared equally with no interest on capital and no salaries.
:::
