## Introduction

Mutare Youth Football Club trains about sixty boys and girls every Saturday. It is run by volunteer parents, and its aim is to give young people a safe place to play, not to make money for owners. Even so, the club handles cash: members pay subscriptions, local businesses give donations, spectators pay at the gate, and the club pays for ground rent, kit and travel to away matches. At the annual general meeting the members want to know where the money came from and where it went.

Clubs, societies, churches, school associations and burial societies are **not-for-profit organisations** (ZIMSEC calls them **non-trading concerns**). Their treasurers usually keep a simple cash record, summarised at the year end in a **receipts and payments account**. ZIMSEC introduces this in Form 2; Cambridge 0452 includes it in the topic of clubs and societies. It is the starting point for the income and expenditure account that follows in later lessons.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC: **non-trading concerns** or **not-for-profit making organisations**. Cambridge: **clubs and societies**. Both boards use **receipts and payments account**, **income and expenditure account**, **accumulated fund**, **surplus** and **deficit**.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Not-for-profit organisation"}
An organisation set up to provide a service or pursue an interest for its members or the community, not to earn profit for owners.
:::

:::definition{term="Subscriptions"}
The amounts members pay, usually every year, to belong to the club.
:::

:::definition{term="Accumulated fund"}
The capital of a club: its total assets less its total liabilities. It is built up from past surpluses.
:::

:::definition{term="Surplus"}
The excess of a club's income over its expenditure for the year (the club's equivalent of profit).
:::

:::definition{term="Deficit"}
The excess of a club's expenditure over its income for the year (the club's equivalent of a loss).
:::

:::definition{term="Receipts and payments account"}
A summary of the club's cash and bank transactions for the year, showing the opening balance, all money received, all money paid and the closing balance.
:::

## Sources of income

Clubs typically receive money from:

- **subscriptions** from members, and **entrance fees** from new members;
- **donations** from members, businesses or well-wishers;
- **fundraising events** such as dinners, raffles, concerts and sponsored walks;
- **revenue-generating activities** such as a refreshment bar, a tuck shop or a poultry project;
- **gate takings**, competition fees, hire of the club's hall or equipment;
- **interest** on savings and **grants** from sponsors or local authorities.

## Preparing a receipts and payments account

A receipts and payments account is a summary of the cash book. It is set out like a ledger account:

1. **Debit side (receipts):** the opening cash and bank balance (balance b/d), then every amount received during the year, whatever it was for.
2. **Credit side (payments):** every amount paid during the year, whether for running expenses or for new equipment.
3. **Balance c/d:** the difference, which is the cash and bank balance at the year end. Bring it down on the debit side on the first day of the new year.

It records **only cash movements**: amounts owing, prepaid or in arrears are ignored, there is no depreciation, and capital and revenue items are mixed together. If the opening balance is an **overdraft**, it is shown as a balance b/d on the credit side.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: receipts and payments account of Mutare Youth Football Club]
At 1 January 2025 the club had US\$1,240 in the bank. During 2025 it received subscriptions US\$6,850, donations US\$1,500, gate takings US\$2,310 and US\$3,200 from tickets for a fundraising dinner. It paid ground rent US\$2,400, football kit US\$3,150, a coach's honorarium US\$1,800, travel to matches US\$2,670, fundraising dinner costs US\$1,380 and new goal posts US\$1,950.

1. Total receipts including the opening balance: 1,240 + 6,850 + 1,500 + 2,310 + 3,200 = 15,100.
2. Total payments: 2,400 + 3,150 + 1,800 + 2,670 + 1,380 + 1,950 = 13,350.
3. Closing balance: 15,100 − 13,350 = 1,750.

## Limitations of a receipts and payments account

The account tells members how much cash the club has, but not whether it is covering its costs:

- it ignores **amounts owing and prepaid**, such as subscriptions in arrears or rent unpaid at the year end;
- it mixes **capital and revenue** items: the goal posts will last for years, yet they are treated like the year's travel costs;
- there is **no depreciation** of equipment;
- it shows **no surplus or deficit** for the year;
- it does not list the club's **assets and liabilities**, so it does not show the accumulated fund.

For these reasons, larger clubs also prepare an **income and expenditure account** (the club's version of an income statement) and a **statement of financial position**.

## Finding the opening accumulated fund

To prepare those statements you need the accumulated fund at the start of the year. Like a sole trader's opening capital, it is found with a **statement of affairs**: list every asset and liability at that date, then

**accumulated fund = total assets − total liabilities**.

Subscriptions in arrears (owed by members) are an asset; subscriptions paid in advance (owed to members as future membership) are a liability.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: accumulated fund of Mutare Youth Football Club]
At 1 January 2025 the club owned equipment valued at US\$4,800 and had US\$1,240 in the bank (from Example 1). Members owed US\$300 of subscriptions for 2024; other members had paid US\$150 in advance for 2025. Ground rent of US\$400 was owing.

1. Assets: 4,800 + 300 + 1,240 = 6,340.
2. Liabilities: 150 + 400 = 550.
3. Accumulated fund: 6,340 − 550 = 5,790.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Leaving out the opening balance"}
The receipts side starts with the cash and bank balance brought down from the previous year (unless it is an overdraft, which goes on the payments side).
:::

:::mistake{title="Adjusting for amounts owing"}
A receipts and payments account records only money actually received and paid. Arrears, prepayments and accruals belong in the income and expenditure account.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the closing balance a surplus"}
The closing balance is cash in hand and at the bank, not a surplus. A club can have plenty of cash and still run at a deficit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating subscriptions in advance as an asset"}
Members who have paid in advance are owed a year's membership: it is a liability of the club.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Head the account with the club's name and "Receipts and payments account for the year ended …". The balance c/d and b/d lines each earn marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
When a question asks for "limitations", give points that the income and expenditure account fixes: no accruals or prepayments, capital items mixed with revenue items, no depreciation, no surplus or deficit.
:::

:::exam-tip
If the opening bank balance is missing, find it as the balancing figure: closing balance + payments − receipts.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Clubs and societies are not-for-profit organisations (ZIMSEC: non-trading concerns).
- Their capital is the accumulated fund; a surplus or deficit replaces profit or loss.
- Income comes from subscriptions, donations, fundraising, revenue-generating activities and similar sources.
- A receipts and payments account summarises cash and bank: opening balance + receipts − payments = closing balance.
- It ignores accruals, prepayments and depreciation and mixes capital with revenue items.
- Opening accumulated fund = assets − liabilities at the start of the year.
:::
