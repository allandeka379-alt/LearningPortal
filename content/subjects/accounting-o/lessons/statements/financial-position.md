## Introduction

The income statement tells Tendai how much profit his Gweru hardware shop made during the year. It does not tell him what the business **owns** and **owes** at the end of the year. A bank considering a loan, a supplier deciding whether to give credit, or Tendai himself planning to open a second branch in Kwekwe all need that picture. It is given by the **statement of financial position**.

The statement of financial position lists the balances that remain in the ledger after the income statement has been prepared: assets, liabilities and capital. It is a snapshot at one date, and it always shows the accounting equation in action: **assets − liabilities = capital**. In both ZIMSEC and Cambridge Paper 2 you will be asked to prepare it in full, usually straight after an income statement.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards say **statement of financial position** (older books: balance sheet). ZIMSEC papers often say debtors and creditors for **trade receivables** and **trade payables**; Cambridge uses only the second pair, and calls accrued and prepaid amounts **other payables** and **other receivables**. Cambridge says profit for the year where ZIMSEC says net profit.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Statement of financial position"}
A statement of the assets, liabilities and capital of a business at a particular date.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-current assets"}
Assets bought to be used in the business for more than one year, not for resale: premises, machinery, vehicles, fixtures (tangible), and goodwill or licences (intangible: they have no physical form).
:::

:::definition{term="Current assets"}
Assets that are cash or will become cash within a year: inventory, trade receivables, other receivables (prepayments), bank and cash.
:::

:::definition{term="Current liabilities"}
Amounts owed that must be paid within a year: trade payables, other payables (accruals) and a bank overdraft.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-current liabilities"}
Amounts owed that are not due for payment within a year, such as a long-term bank loan.
:::

:::definition{term="Working capital (net current assets)"}
Current assets less current liabilities: the funds available to run the business from day to day.
:::

## The layout

The vertical layout used by both boards has two halves that must agree:

1. **Net assets:** non-current assets (cost, accumulated depreciation and net book value), plus working capital (current assets less current liabilities), less non-current liabilities.
2. **Capital:** opening capital, plus any capital introduced, plus profit for the year (or less a loss), less drawings, gives closing capital.

Current assets are normally listed in order of liquidity, from the least liquid (inventory) to the most liquid (cash). The closing inventory used in the income statement is the inventory shown here.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: Tendai's Hardware]
Tendai's profit for the year ended 31 December 2025 was US\$18,785 and his drawings were US\$9,600. His capital at 1 January 2025 was US\$69,150. At 31 December 2025 his balances were: premises at cost US\$60,000; fixtures and fittings at cost US\$14,000 (accumulated depreciation US\$5,600); motor van at cost US\$22,000 (accumulated depreciation US\$8,800); inventory US\$8,900; trade receivables US\$6,450; bank US\$3,215; cash US\$410; trade payables US\$7,240; bank loan repayable in 2030 US\$15,000.

1. Net book value of non-current assets: 96,000 − 14,400 = 81,600.
2. Current assets: 8,900 + 6,450 + 3,215 + 410 = 18,975.
3. Working capital: 18,975 − 7,240 = 11,735.
4. Net assets: 81,600 + 11,735 − 15,000 = 78,335.
5. Closing capital: 69,150 + 18,785 − 9,600 = 78,335. The two halves agree.

## Drawings

**Drawings** are anything of value the owner takes out of the business for private use. They reduce capital and are never an expense. They include:

- **cash or bank** withdrawals;
- **goods** taken from inventory for the family: debit drawings, credit purchases, at cost;
- **other assets** taken home, such as a laptop;
- **private expenses paid by the business**, such as the owner's home electricity bill or a child's school fees paid from the business bank account: the private part is removed from the expense and added to drawings.

## Capital introduced

If the owner puts more money (or a vehicle, for example) into the business during the year, it is **capital introduced**. It is added to opening capital, separately from profit.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: drawings of several kinds at Farai's Grocery]
Farai runs a grocery in Chinhoyi. His capital at 1 January 2025 was US\$9,950. During the year he paid in a further US\$2,000 of his savings. His profit for the year was US\$12,390. He withdrew US\$7,200 in cash, took groceries costing US\$650 for his family, and the business paid his home electricity bill of US\$400 (already removed from the business's electricity expense). His balances at 31 December 2025 were: goodwill US\$5,000; shop equipment at cost US\$9,600 (accumulated depreciation US\$3,840); delivery motorcycle at cost US\$4,800 (accumulated depreciation US\$1,200); inventory US\$5,340; trade receivables US\$1,870; cash US\$260; trade payables US\$4,115; bank overdraft US\$1,385; accrued expenses US\$240.

1. Total drawings: 7,200 + 650 + 400 = 8,250.
2. Net book value: 19,400 − 5,040 = 14,360.
3. Working capital: 7,470 − 5,740 = 1,730. The overdraft is a current liability, not a negative asset.
4. Net assets: 14,360 + 1,730 = 16,090.
5. Closing capital: 9,950 + 2,000 + 12,390 − 8,250 = 16,090.

## Why working capital matters

Working capital shows whether the business can pay its short-term debts. Farai's working capital of US\$1,730 is positive but small: if a large supplier demands payment, he may struggle. A business can make a good profit and still fail if it runs out of working capital, because suppliers and the bank must be paid in cash.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating drawings as an expense"}
Drawings never go in the income statement. They are deducted from capital in the statement of financial position.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing a bank overdraft as a current asset"}
An overdraft is money owed to the bank and repayable on demand: it is a current liability.
:::

:::mistake{title="Showing non-current assets at cost only"}
Show cost, accumulated depreciation and net book value. Only the net book value is added into the total.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting a long-term loan in current liabilities"}
A loan repayable after more than a year is a non-current liability. Only the part due within twelve months is current.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
The heading says "at 31 December 2025", not "for the year ended": this statement shows one moment in time.
:::

:::exam-tip
If your statement does not balance, do not force it with a balancing figure. Examiners give marks line by line, so check each subtotal, then move on.
:::

:::exam-tip
When an item of drawings is hidden in the notes (goods taken, private bills paid by the business), adjust both statements: remove it from purchases or the expense, and add it to drawings.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The statement of financial position shows assets, liabilities and capital at one date.
- Non-current assets are shown at cost less accumulated depreciation; intangible assets such as goodwill are non-current too.
- Working capital = current assets − current liabilities; an overdraft is a current liability.
- Closing capital = opening capital + capital introduced + profit (− loss) − drawings, and equals net assets.
- Drawings include cash, goods, other assets and private expenses paid by the business.
:::
