## Introduction

Rudo runs a stationery wholesaler in Masvingo. She supplies exercise books, pens and printing paper on credit to schools, print shops and small traders across the province, and she buys on credit from a dozen manufacturers and importers. In her first year she kept every account in one large book. By the second year the book had over a hundred customer accounts, and whenever her clerk was updating a customer's account, nobody else could record a wage payment or a supplier's invoice.

The answer is to **divide the ledger**: put the accounts of credit customers in one book, the accounts of credit suppliers in another, and everything else in a third. Each part can then be kept by a different person, errors are easier to trace, and the owner can see at once how much customers owe and how much the business owes its suppliers.

Both boards expect you to name the divisions, say which accounts each holds, and explain why a business divides its ledger. In structured questions you may be asked which ledger a given account is found in, or to post a journal to the correct ledgers.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ledger"}
The book (or computer file) that contains all the accounts of a business. It is the book of final entry: every transaction ends up in two ledger accounts.
:::

:::definition{term="Sales ledger (trade receivables ledger)"}
The division of the ledger that holds the personal accounts of credit customers, the people and businesses that owe the business money for goods or services bought on credit.
:::

:::definition{term="Purchases ledger (trade payables ledger)"}
The division of the ledger that holds the personal accounts of credit suppliers, to whom the business owes money for goods or services bought on credit.
:::

:::definition{term="Nominal ledger (general ledger)"}
The division that holds all the other accounts: income and expense accounts (nominal accounts), asset accounts such as premises and equipment (real accounts), and the owner's capital and drawings accounts, loans and the like.
:::

## The three main divisions

| Division | Accounts it holds | Typical balance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sales ledger (trade receivables ledger) | One account for each credit customer | Debit (the customer owes the business) |
| Purchases ledger (trade payables ledger) | One account for each credit supplier | Credit (the business owes the supplier) |
| Nominal ledger (general ledger) | Sales, purchases, returns, expenses, income, non-current assets, inventory, capital, drawings, loans | Debit or credit, depending on the account |

![The ledger is divided into three parts: the sales ledger holds each credit customer's account (normally a debit balance), the purchases ledger holds each credit supplier's account (normally a credit balance), and the general ledger holds all other accounts; all are posted from the books of prime entry.](media:diagrams/ledger-divisions.png "Figure: the three divisions of the ledger")

The **cash book** is a special case. It records cash and bank transactions as a book of prime entry, but its cash and bank columns are also the cash and bank accounts, so it forms part of the double entry. Many businesses treat it as a separate division of the ledger. The petty cash book works the same way for small payments.

Note that the **sales account** and **purchases account** themselves are in the nominal ledger, not in the sales and purchases ledgers. The sales ledger holds only the customers' accounts; the total of credit sales goes to the sales account in the nominal ledger.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: which ledger?]
Tatenda owns a hardware shop in Gweru. Classify each of his accounts by the division of the ledger in which it is kept.

| Account | Division of the ledger | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chinhoyi Builders (buys cement on credit) | Sales ledger | Credit customer |
| Bulawayo Steel Supplies (supplies roofing sheets on credit) | Purchases ledger | Credit supplier |
| Sales | Nominal ledger | Income account |
| Purchases | Nominal ledger | Expense account (cost of goods for resale) |
| Delivery van | Nominal ledger | Asset (real account) |
| Wages | Nominal ledger | Expense account |
| Capital | Nominal ledger | Owner's account |
| Bank | Cash book | Records money in and out of the bank account |

1. Ask first: is this a **person or business** that buys from, or sells to, Tatenda **on credit**? If yes, it goes in the sales or purchases ledger.
2. Everything else (income, expenses, assets, capital, drawings, loans) belongs in the nominal ledger.
3. A cash customer has no personal account at all: the cash sale is recorded in the cash book and the sales account.
:::

## Posting to the divided ledger

The divisions change **where** accounts are kept, not the rules of double entry. Each book of prime entry is posted like this:

- **Sales journal**: each invoice to the debit of the customer's account in the **sales ledger**; the total to the credit of the sales account in the **nominal ledger**.
- **Purchases journal**: each invoice to the credit of the supplier's account in the **purchases ledger**; the total to the debit of the purchases account in the **nominal ledger**.
- **Returns inwards journal**: each credit note to the credit of the customer (sales ledger); the total to the debit of returns inwards (nominal ledger).
- **Returns outwards journal**: each item to the debit of the supplier (purchases ledger); the total to the credit of returns outwards (nominal ledger).
- **Cash book**: receipts from customers are credited to their sales ledger accounts; payments to suppliers are debited to their purchases ledger accounts; other items go to the nominal ledger.

## Why divide the ledger?

- **Division of work**: different clerks can work on different ledgers at the same time.
- **Control and fraud prevention**: one person does not handle every record, so errors and fraud are harder to hide. Control accounts (studied later) check each ledger's arithmetic.
- **Locating errors**: when the trial balance does not agree, the error can be traced to one ledger.
- **Information**: the owner can quickly see total trade receivables and trade payables, and follow up slow-paying customers.
- **Convenience**: a large business may further divide its sales ledger, for example by region (Harare customers A to M, N to Z) or by area of the country.

## Ledgers kept digitally

Most businesses now keep their ledgers on accounting software. The same divisions still exist, although the software may call them modules: a **customers** (receivables) module, a **suppliers** (payables) module and a **general ledger** or chart of accounts. When an invoice is entered once, the software posts the debit to the customer and the credit to sales automatically, balances every account continuously, and can print a list of customer balances or an aged list of who has owed money longest. Advantages include speed, fewer arithmetic errors and up-to-date balances; risks include data loss, entering a transaction under the wrong customer, and unauthorised access, so passwords and regular backups are essential.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting the sales account in the sales ledger"}
The sales ledger holds only the **customers'** personal accounts. The sales account, like purchases, is a nominal account in the nominal (general) ledger.
:::

:::mistake{title="Opening accounts for cash customers"}
Only credit customers and credit suppliers need personal accounts. A cash sale is recorded in the cash book and the sales account.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking a divided ledger changes the double entry"}
Every debit still has an equal credit. Dividing the ledger only changes where the accounts are kept.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Use the name the question uses. A ZIMSEC paper may say "trade receivables ledger"; a Cambridge paper says "sales ledger". Both are correct for the same book, and giving both names is safe.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked for reasons for dividing the ledger, give **different** reasons (division of work, control, locating errors, information) rather than one reason in several forms.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The ledger is divided into the sales (trade receivables) ledger, the purchases (trade payables) ledger and the nominal (general) ledger; the cash book holds the cash and bank accounts.
- The sales ledger holds credit customers' accounts; the purchases ledger holds credit suppliers' accounts; every other account is in the nominal ledger.
- Individual invoices are posted to personal accounts; journal totals go to the sales, purchases and returns accounts in the nominal ledger.
- Dividing the ledger allows division of work, better control, easier location of errors and quicker information.
- Accounting software keeps the same divisions as customer, supplier and general ledger modules and posts both sides automatically.
:::
