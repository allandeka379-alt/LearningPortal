## Introduction

Tendai Moyo has just opened Moyo Hardware in Gweru. In his first month he puts in his savings, buys cement and paint on credit, pays rent, sells to cash customers and gives credit to a builder he trusts. At the end of the month he wants to know how much is in the bank, how much he owes and how much he is owed. The **double entry system** is the method that lets him answer all three questions from one set of records.

Double entry is the foundation of everything else in accounting: the trial balance, the financial statements and the correction of errors all depend on it. Both ZIMSEC and Cambridge set questions that ask you to record transactions in ledger accounts, so the rules in this lesson must become automatic.

You should already know the accounting equation: assets = capital + liabilities.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
A credit customer is a **trade receivable** (debtor) and a credit supplier is a **trade payable** (creditor) in both syllabi. ZIMSEC keeps their accounts in the **trade receivables and trade payables ledgers**; Cambridge calls these the **sales and purchases ledgers**.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Double entry"}
The system in which every transaction is recorded twice, as a debit in one account and a credit of the same amount in another account.
:::

:::definition{term="Ledger account"}
A record of all the transactions of one type, or with one person, kept in two sides: the debit side (left) and the credit side (right).
:::

:::definition{term="Debit and credit"}
**Debit** (Dr) means an entry on the left-hand side of an account; **credit** (Cr) means an entry on the right-hand side. They are positions, not "good" or "bad" news.
:::

## Why two entries?

Every transaction has two aspects. When Tendai pays US\$300 rent by bank transfer, the business **receives** the use of the shop (an expense) and **gives** money from the bank. One account records what comes in (the receiving account is debited) and another records what goes out (the giving account is credited). Because the two entries are always equal, total debits always equal total credits, which is what makes the trial balance possible.

This is the **duality** concept, and it also keeps the accounting equation in balance after every transaction.

## The rules of debit and credit

| Type of account | Increase | Decrease | Normal balance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Asset (bank, cash, inventory, equipment, trade receivables) | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Expense (purchases, rent, wages, electricity) | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Drawings | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Liability (trade payables, loans, bank overdraft) | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Capital | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Income (sales, commission received, rent received) | Credit | Debit | Credit |

![The rule of debit and credit on a T account: debit entries increase assets, expenses and drawings and decrease liabilities, capital and income; credit entries increase liabilities, capital and income and decrease assets, expenses and drawings.](media:diagrams/debit-credit-rules-t-account.png "Figure: which side of a T account increases each type of account")

:::keyfact{title="A memory aid"}
**DEAD CLIC**: **D**ebit to increase **E**xpenses, **A**ssets and **D**rawings; **C**redit to increase **L**iabilities, **I**ncome and **C**apital.
:::

A simple three-step method works for every transaction:

1. Name the **two accounts** affected.
2. Decide what **type** each account is and whether it increases or decreases.
3. Apply the rules: the account that increases an asset or expense is debited; the account that increases a liability, capital or income is credited (and the reverse for decreases).

### Goods for resale

Goods bought to resell are never debited to an "inventory" or "goods" account during the year. They go to the **purchases** account. Goods sold go to the **sales** account. Goods returned by customers go to **returns inwards** (debit) and goods sent back to suppliers to **returns outwards** (credit).

### Cash or credit?

- Cash or bank transactions: one entry is in the **cash** or **bank** account.
- Credit transactions: one entry is in the **personal account** of the customer or supplier, never in cash or bank. The cash or bank entry comes later, when the debt is paid.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: analysing Moyo Hardware's transactions]
Moyo Hardware's transactions for March 2025 are analysed below.

| Date | Transaction | Account debited | Account credited | US\$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| 1 Mar | Tendai paid his savings into a business bank account | Bank | Capital | 8,000 |
| 3 Mar | Bought goods on credit from Kwekwe Cement Suppliers | Purchases | Kwekwe Cement Suppliers | 2,400 |
| 5 Mar | Bought shelving (fixtures) and paid by bank transfer | Fixtures and fittings | Bank | 1,500 |
| 8 Mar | Sold goods for cash | Cash | Sales | 900 |
| 12 Mar | Sold goods on credit to Rudo Chikore | Rudo Chikore | Sales | 650 |
| 15 Mar | Paid rent by bank transfer | Rent | Bank | 300 |
| 20 Mar | Paid Kwekwe Cement Suppliers on account by bank transfer | Kwekwe Cement Suppliers | Bank | 1,000 |
| 26 Mar | Rudo Chikore paid part of her debt in cash | Cash | Rudo Chikore | 400 |

1. 1 Mar: the asset bank increases (debit); capital increases (credit).
2. 3 Mar: goods for resale go to purchases (debit); the business now owes the supplier, a liability that increases (credit).
3. 5 Mar: shelving is a non-current asset kept for use, not for resale, so fixtures and fittings is debited; bank decreases (credit).
4. 12 Mar: Rudo now owes the business: her account (an asset) is debited and sales credited.
5. 20 Mar: paying the supplier reduces the liability (debit) and the bank (credit).
6. 26 Mar: cash increases (debit); Rudo owes less, so her account is credited.
:::

## Classifying accounts: personal, real and nominal

Accounts are often grouped into three classes:

- **Personal accounts** are for people and organisations the business deals with: trade receivables (Rudo Chikore), trade payables (Kwekwe Cement Suppliers), lenders and the owner's **capital** and **drawings** accounts.
- **Real accounts** are for things the business owns: premises, motor vehicles, fixtures and fittings, equipment, inventory, cash and bank.
- **Nominal accounts** are for income, expenses, gains and losses: sales, purchases, returns, rent, wages, discounts allowed and received, commission received.

Personal and real accounts usually carry their balances into the next year. Nominal accounts are closed at the year end by transferring their balances to the income statement.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Debiting the giver"}
Students often debit bank when paying money out. Money leaving the bank is a **decrease** in an asset, so bank is **credited**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the cash or bank account for credit transactions"}
A credit sale does not touch cash or bank. Debit the customer's personal account and credit sales.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling non-current assets purchases"}
Shelving, delivery vans and tills are bought to be **used**, not resold. Debit the asset account (fixtures and fittings, motor vehicles, equipment), not purchases.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In the details column write the name of the **other** account in the double entry. Examiners award a mark for the correct date, details and amount in each entry.
:::

:::exam-tip
Before writing anything, jot the two accounts and Dr / Cr next to each transaction. A quick check that every debit has an equal credit saves marks later.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Every transaction is recorded twice: a debit in one account and an equal credit in another.
- Debit increases assets, expenses and drawings; credit increases liabilities, income and capital.
- Goods for resale go to purchases and sales; non-current assets go to their own asset accounts.
- Credit transactions use the personal accounts of customers and suppliers, not cash or bank.
- Accounts are personal, real or nominal; nominal accounts are closed to the income statement each year.
:::
