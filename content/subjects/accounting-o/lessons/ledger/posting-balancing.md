## Introduction

Chipo keeps broiler chickens on a smallholding outside Chinhoyi and sells them to restaurants and market vendors. By the end of each month her bank account has a long list of entries on both sides: feed bought, wages paid, takings banked, money from customers. On its own the list does not tell her what she needs to know: **how much is in the bank now?** Balancing the account answers that question in one figure, and the balance becomes the starting point for next month.

In the examination you will be asked to post transactions, balance accounts, show the balance brought down on the correct side, and explain what an entry or a balance tells the owner. Neat, correctly dated balancing earns easy marks, and a balance on the wrong side loses them.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards accept the **T account** (two-sided) and the **three-column running balance** format. ZIMSEC expects **folio columns** (page references) in ledger work; Cambridge candidates do not need them. The first books are **subsidiary books** (ZIMSEC) or **books of prime entry** (Cambridge).
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Posting"}
Transferring the entries from the books of prime entry (journals and cash book) to the ledger accounts.
:::

:::definition{term="Balance"}
The difference between the totals of the two sides of an account. A **debit balance** means the debit side is larger; a **credit balance** means the credit side is larger.
:::

:::definition{term="Balance carried down (c/d) and brought down (b/d)"}
The balance c/d is entered on the smaller side to make both sides total the same at the end of a period. The same amount is brought down (b/d) on the opposite side at the start of the next period.
:::

## Posting from the books of prime entry

Transactions are first recorded in a book of prime entry, then posted:

- **Sales journal**: each invoice is posted to the **debit** of the customer's account; the period total is posted to the **credit** of the sales account.
- **Purchases journal**: each invoice is posted to the **credit** of the supplier's account; the total is posted to the **debit** of purchases.
- **Returns journals**: individual entries to the personal accounts, totals to returns inwards (debit) or returns outwards (credit).
- **Cash book**: it is itself a ledger account for cash and bank, so only the other half of each entry is posted (for example, a payment of rent is posted to the debit of rent).

### Folio columns (ZIMSEC)

A folio is a page reference. When an entry is posted, the ledger page is written in the folio column of the book of prime entry, and the book's page (for example SJ4 for sales journal page 4) is written in the folio column of the ledger account. Folios show that an entry has been posted and make it easy to trace. Cambridge does not require folio columns.

## Balancing a T account

Follow the same five steps every time:

1. Add up each side and find the **larger total**.
2. Write that total on **both** sides, on the same line, double-underlined (shown in bold here).
3. On the smaller side, enter the difference as **Balance c/d**, dated the last day of the period.
4. Bring the balance down on the **opposite** side as **Balance b/d**, dated the first day of the next period.
5. If both sides are equal, simply total them: there is no balance.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: balancing Chipo's bank account]
Chipo's bank account for May 2025 is shown after balancing.

**Bank account**

| Date | Details | US\$ | Date | Details | US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- | --- | ---: |
| 1 May | Balance b/d | 2,350 | 9 May | Purchases | 860 |
| 4 May | Sales | 1,180 | 14 May | Wages | 420 |
| 19 May | Farai Ncube | 640 | 23 May | Motor repairs | 275 |
| 30 May | Sales | 935 | 28 May | Drawings | 300 |
|  |  |  | 31 May | Balance c/d | 3,250 |
|  |  | **5,105** |  |  | **5,105** |
| 1 Jun | Balance b/d | 3,250 |  |  |  |

1. Debit side: $2,350 + 1,180 + 640 + 935 = 5,105$.
2. Credit side before balancing: $860 + 420 + 275 + 300 = 1,855$.
3. Balance c/d on the credit side: $5,105 - 1,855 = 3,250$, dated 31 May.
4. Both totals are US\$5,105.
5. The balance b/d of US\$3,250 is on the **debit** side on 1 June: Chipo has US\$3,250 in the bank.
:::

## The three-column running balance format

Many businesses and all accounting software show accounts with a **Debit**, **Credit** and **Balance** column. The balance is updated after every entry and marked Dr or Cr, so the account never needs to be "balanced off". Chipo's bank account in this format is:

**Bank account**

| Date | Details | Debit US\$ | Credit US\$ | Balance US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 May | Balance |  |  | 2,350 Dr |
| 4 May | Sales | 1,180 |  | 3,530 Dr |
| 9 May | Purchases |  | 860 | 2,670 Dr |
| 14 May | Wages |  | 420 | 2,250 Dr |
| 19 May | Farai Ncube | 640 |  | 2,890 Dr |
| 23 May | Motor repairs |  | 275 | 2,615 Dr |
| 28 May | Drawings |  | 300 | 2,315 Dr |
| 30 May | Sales | 935 |  | 3,250 Dr |

The closing balance, US\$3,250 Dr, is the same as the T account. Entries must be in **date order** for the running balance to be correct.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a supplier's account in running balance format]
Chipo buys feed on credit from Gweru Feeds. In May: balance owed on 1 May US\$1,420; purchases on 6 May US\$980; feed returned on 10 May US\$120; paid by bank on 20 May US\$1,300; purchases on 27 May US\$640.

**Gweru Feeds account**

| Date | Details | Debit US\$ | Credit US\$ | Balance US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 May | Balance |  | 1,420 | 1,420 Cr |
| 6 May | Purchases |  | 980 | 2,400 Cr |
| 10 May | Returns outwards | 120 |  | 2,280 Cr |
| 20 May | Bank | 1,300 |  | 980 Cr |
| 27 May | Purchases |  | 640 | 1,620 Cr |

1. Purchases on credit increase the liability: credit column, balance up.
2. Returns and payments reduce it: debit column, balance down.
3. The closing balance is US\$1,620 **Cr**: Chipo owes Gweru Feeds US\$1,620.
4. In T form the balance c/d of US\$1,620 would be on the **debit** side (totals US\$3,040) and the balance b/d on the credit side on 1 June.
:::

## Interpreting entries and balances

Examiners often show you an account and ask what an entry or balance means. Read the details column: it names the other account.

| Account and balance | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Customer's account, debit balance | The customer owes the business (a trade receivable) |
| Supplier's account, credit balance | The business owes the supplier (a trade payable) |
| Supplier's account, debit balance | The business has overpaid or returned goods after paying; the supplier owes the business |
| Bank account, debit balance | Money in the bank (an asset) |
| Bank account, credit balance | A bank overdraft (a liability) |
| Expense account, debit balance | Total spent on that expense so far this year |
| Income account, credit balance | Total earned from that income so far this year |

For example, an entry "Returns inwards US\$75" on the credit side of a customer's account means the customer returned goods worth US\$75 and now owes that much less.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Bringing the balance down on the same side"}
The balance c/d goes on the **smaller** side; the balance b/d goes on the **other** side. A bank account with more receipts than payments must start the next month with a **debit** balance b/d.
:::

:::mistake{title="Totals on different lines"}
The two totals must be level with each other, even if one side has more entries. Leave blank lines on the shorter side.
:::

:::mistake{title="Running balances out of date order"}
In the three-column format each balance depends on the one before, so entries must be in date order and every balance must be labelled Dr or Cr.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Date every balance: c/d on the last day of the period, b/d on the first day of the next. Missing dates cost marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to "explain the balance", give **what** it is (asset, liability, income or expense) and **who owes whom**, for example "Tatenda owes the business US\$410, a trade receivable".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Post individual items to personal accounts and period totals to sales, purchases and returns accounts.
- To balance: total the larger side, put the difference on the smaller side as balance c/d, and bring it down on the opposite side.
- The running balance format shows the balance after every entry, marked Dr or Cr.
- A debit balance is an asset, expense or drawings; a credit balance is a liability, capital or income.
- ZIMSEC uses folio columns; Cambridge does not require them.
:::
