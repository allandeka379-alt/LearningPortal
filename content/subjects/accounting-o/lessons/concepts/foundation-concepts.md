## Introduction

Farai runs a tuck shop at a busy bus stop in Harare. He pays some of his family's bills from the shop's bank account, values the shop's fridge at whatever he thinks it would sell for, and leaves the loyalty of his regular customers out of the books because he "can't put a number on it". Are these choices allowed? Two accountants given the same records should produce the same profit, and that is only possible if they follow the same basic rules.

These rules are the **accounting concepts** (also called principles or conventions). They are not laws, but every set of financial statements is built on them. This lesson covers the five **foundation** concepts, which decide **what** is recorded and at **what value**: business entity, duality, money measurement, historic cost and going concern. The companion lesson covers matching, prudence, consistency, materiality and realisation, which shape the year-end adjustments.

In the examination you must be able to **name** the concept that applies to a situation, **explain** it, and say how the accounts should be corrected when it has been broken. One-mark multiple-choice questions on concepts appear in almost every Paper 1.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge lists"}
Cambridge 0452 lists all five concepts in this lesson. The ZIMSEC syllabus lists **going concern**, **historical cost** and **double entry (duality)**; business entity and money measurement are still used in ZIMSEC answers but are named only by Cambridge. ZIMSEC says "historical cost"; Cambridge says "historic cost": they mean the same thing.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Business entity"}
The business is treated as separate from its owner. Only the transactions of the business are recorded; the owner's private transactions are not, except where they affect the business (capital introduced and drawings).
:::

:::definition{term="Duality (double entry)"}
Every transaction has two aspects, which are recorded as a debit in one account and an equal credit in another. It is the basis of the accounting equation: assets = capital + liabilities.
:::

:::definition{term="Money measurement"}
Only items that can be measured reliably in money are recorded in the accounts.
:::

:::definition{term="Historic (historical) cost"}
Assets are recorded at what they cost when they were acquired, not at their current market value.
:::

:::definition{term="Going concern"}
The business is assumed to continue trading for the foreseeable future, so assets are valued on that basis rather than at what they would raise if the business closed down.
:::

## Business entity

The owner and the business are different "persons" in the books. When Farai puts US\$3,000 of his savings into the shop's bank account, the business records capital, a debt to Farai. When he takes money or goods for himself, the business records **drawings**. But his family's household bills are **not business expenses**: if they are paid from the business bank account they must be treated as drawings.

A breach of the business entity concept usually **understates profit** (private expenses charged as business expenses) or hides drawings.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: correcting a breach of the business entity concept]
Farai's draft profit for 2025 was US\$7,420. A review shows that three of Farai's private bills were paid from the business bank account and charged as business expenses: electricity at his home US\$360, his children's school fees US\$950 and his personal phone bill US\$145.

**Farai's Tuck Shop: corrected profit for the year ended 31 December 2025**

|  | US\$ | US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Draft profit |  | 7,420 |
| Add back private expenses wrongly charged: |  |  |
| Electricity at Farai's home | 360 |  |
| Children's school fees | 950 |  |
| Farai's personal phone bill | 145 |  |
|  |  | 1,455 |
| **Corrected profit** |  | 8,875 |

The correcting entry moves the amounts out of the expense accounts and into drawings:

**Journal**

| Date | Details | Debit US\$ | Credit US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| 31 Dec | Drawings | 1,455 |  |
|  | Electricity |  | 360 |
|  | Sundry expenses |  | 950 |
|  | Telephone |  | 145 |
|  | *Private expenses paid from the business bank account transferred to drawings* |  |  |

1. Private expenses: $360 + 950 + 145 = 1,455$.
2. Removing them from expenses raises profit: $7,420 + 1,455 = 8,875$.
3. Drawings rise by US\$1,455, so Farai's closing capital is unchanged: profit is higher by the same amount as the extra drawings.
:::

## Duality (double entry)

Duality says every transaction affects two things. Buying a fridge for cash increases one asset and decreases another; borrowing from a bank increases an asset and a liability. This is why every debit has an equal credit, why the trial balance totals agree, and why the accounting equation always balances. Duality is the concept behind the whole double entry system.

## Money measurement

Only what can be measured in money is recorded. A loyal customer base, a skilled and motivated staff, a good location or the owner's reputation may be valuable, but they cannot be measured reliably, so they do not appear as assets. This is a **limitation** of financial statements: they do not show everything that matters about a business. Examiners often ask for one item that money measurement leaves out.

## Historic cost

Assets are recorded at their **cost**: the price paid plus costs to get them ready for use (delivery, installation, legal fees on premises). Cost is objective and can be checked against an invoice, whereas a market value is an opinion that changes. Non-current assets are then shown at cost less accumulated depreciation.

## Going concern

Because the business is expected to continue, its assets are worth what they are worth **in use**, not what they would fetch in a forced sale. Premises used for the next twenty years are kept at cost; specialised fittings that would be almost worthless if the shop closed are still shown at cost less depreciation. If a business is **not** a going concern (it is about to close), assets are revalued at what they would realise on sale, usually much less.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: which value goes in the statement of financial position?]
Chipo's bakery in Mutare owns three assets. An adviser gives three possible values for each:

| Asset | Historic cost US\$ | Current market value US\$ | Forced-sale value if closing US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Premises | 48,000 | 61,000 | 39,000 |
| Delivery vehicle | 14,500 | 12,800 | 9,000 |
| Inventory | 6,200 | 7,100 | 3,100 |
| **Total** | **68,700** | **80,900** | **51,100** |

1. The bakery is trading normally and expects to continue: it is a **going concern**, so forced-sale values (total US\$51,100) are not used.
2. Under **historic cost**, the premises stay at US\$48,000 even though they could be sold for more. The delivery vehicle is shown at its cost of US\$14,500 less accumulated depreciation.
3. Inventory is at cost, US\$6,200 (it would only be reduced if it could be sold for less than cost: the prudence concept).
4. If Chipo decided to close the bakery next month, the going concern assumption would no longer hold and the forced-sale values would be relevant.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing business entity with drawings rules"}
Drawings are not a breach of the entity concept: they are how the concept is applied. The breach is recording a private expense **as a business expense**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Revaluing assets to market value"}
Under historic cost, a rise in the market value of premises is not recorded in the normal accounts. Writing "the premises are worth more now" is not a reason to change the figure.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying money measurement excludes 'things without value'"}
Good staff and loyal customers have great value; they are excluded because the value cannot be **measured reliably in money**.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When a question says "name the concept", give the exact name (for example "business entity", not "separate business"). When it says "explain", add **what the concept means** and **how it applies** to the situation given.
:::

:::exam-tip
Link concepts to the statement: going concern and historic cost explain the values in the statement of financial position; business entity explains why private bills are drawings, not expenses.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Business entity: the business is separate from its owner; private expenses paid by the business are drawings.
- Duality: every transaction has two equal aspects, recorded as a debit and a credit.
- Money measurement: only items measurable in money are recorded, so staff skill and customer loyalty are left out.
- Historic cost: assets are recorded at what they cost, not at current market value.
- Going concern: the business is assumed to continue, so assets are not valued at forced-sale prices.
:::
