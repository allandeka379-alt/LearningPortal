## Introduction

Rudo owns a hair salon in Bulawayo. At the end of December she notices that the December electricity bill has not arrived yet, she has paid January's rent in advance, a box of hair products has passed its use-by date, and a customer has paid a deposit for a wedding party booked for February. If she simply adds up the money received and paid in 2025, her profit will be wrong. Which amounts belong to 2025?

The answer comes from five **applied** accounting concepts: **matching**, **prudence**, **consistency**, **materiality** and **realisation**. The foundation concepts (business entity, duality, money measurement, historic cost and going concern) decide what is recorded; these five decide **when** income and expenses are recognised and **how cautiously** values are set. They are the reasons behind accruals and prepayments, depreciation, allowances for doubtful debts and inventory valuation, which you meet in the adjustments topics.

Expect to name the concept behind an adjustment, to say which concept has been ignored in a short case, and to calculate the corrected profit.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge lists"}
ZIMSEC lists **matching (accrual)**, **prudence** and **consistency**. Cambridge 0452 lists all five, adding **materiality** and **realisation**. ZIMSEC often calls the matching concept the **accruals** concept. ZIMSEC: provision for doubtful debts; Cambridge: allowance for irrecoverable debts.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Matching (accruals)"}
Income and expenses are matched to the period in which they are earned or incurred, not the period in which the money is received or paid.
:::

:::definition{term="Prudence"}
Profits and assets are not overstated, and liabilities and losses are not understated. Losses are recognised as soon as they are expected; gains only when they are reasonably certain.
:::

:::definition{term="Consistency"}
The same accounting methods are used from one period to the next, so results can be compared. A method is changed only for a good reason, and the change is disclosed.
:::

:::definition{term="Materiality"}
Items whose amount is too small to affect a user's decisions need not be treated in strict detail; a small item can be treated in the simplest way.
:::

:::definition{term="Realisation"}
Revenue is recognised when it is earned, normally when the goods are delivered or the service is performed and the customer accepts liability to pay, not when an order or deposit is received.
:::

## Matching (accruals)

Profit is income **earned** in a year less the expenses **incurred** to earn it in that year. Cash timing is irrelevant. This is why:

- an expense **owing** at the year end (an accrual) is **added** to the amount paid;
- an expense **paid in advance** (a prepayment) is **deducted**;
- non-current assets are **depreciated**, spreading their cost over the years that use them;
- closing inventory is deducted from purchases, because those goods will be sold next year.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: matching expenses to 2025]
Rudo paid US\$1,140 for electricity during 2025; the December bill of US\$175 is still owing at 31 December. She paid rent of US\$4,200 in 2025, which includes US\$350 for January 2026.

| Expense | Paid in 2025 US\$ | Adjustment US\$ | Expense for 2025 US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Electricity | 1,140 | add owing 175 | 1,315 |
| Rent | 4,200 | less prepaid (350) | 3,850 |

1. Electricity used in 2025 includes December, though it is not yet paid: $1,140 + 175 = 1,315$.
2. The January rent belongs to 2026: $4,200 - 350 = 3,850$.
3. The US\$175 owing appears as a current liability (other payables) and the US\$350 as a current asset (other receivables) in the statement of financial position.
:::

## Prudence

Accounting should be cautious. If inventory can only be sold for less than it cost, it is written down to its **net realisable value**; if a customer is unlikely to pay, an **allowance (provision) for doubtful debts** is made. Gains are not recorded until they are realised: a rise in the value of premises is not added to profit. Prudence is about avoiding **over**statement; it does not mean deliberately understating profit, which would also mislead.

## Consistency

If Tatenda depreciates his delivery van by the reducing balance method in 2025, he should use the same method in 2026. Switching methods each year would change the profit for reasons that have nothing to do with how the business performed, and users could not compare the years. A change is allowed when the new method gives a fairer view, but the effect must be explained.

## Materiality

A stapler costing US\$12 will be used for years, so strictly it is a non-current asset to be depreciated. But the amount is so small that nobody's decision would change, so it is simply charged as an expense. What is material depends on the size of the business: US\$500 may be material for a tuck shop and immaterial for a large wholesaler. Many businesses set a limit, for example "items under US\$100 are expensed".

## Realisation

Revenue is recognised when the goods pass to the customer (or the service is done) and the customer becomes liable to pay, **even if payment comes later**. A credit sale delivered in December is December's revenue; a deposit received in December for goods to be delivered in February is **not** revenue in December: it is a liability (the business owes the goods or the money back).

:::worked-solution[Example 2: correcting a profit for ignored concepts]
Tatenda's draft profit for the year ended 31 December 2025 is US\$13,480. Three problems are found:

- A customer's deposit of US\$900 for roofing sheets to be delivered in January 2026 was included in sales.
- Damaged paint that cost US\$2,600 is included in closing inventory at cost; it can now be sold for only US\$1,750.
- A calculator costing US\$45 was recorded as a non-current asset.

**Tatenda's Hardware: corrected profit for the year ended 31 December 2025**

|  | US\$ | US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Draft profit |  | 13,480 |
| Less: customer's deposit for goods not yet delivered (realisation) | 900 |  |
| Less: write-down of damaged inventory to net realisable value (prudence) | 850 |  |
| Less: calculator recorded as a non-current asset (materiality) | 45 |  |
|  |  | (1,795) |
| **Corrected profit** |  | 11,685 |

1. Realisation: the goods have not been delivered, so the US\$900 is not yet earned; sales fall by US\$900.
2. Prudence: inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Write-down $2,600 - 1,750 = 850$; lower closing inventory raises cost of sales and lowers profit.
3. Materiality: a US\$45 item is too small to depreciate; it is charged as an expense, reducing profit by US\$45.
4. Total reduction $900 + 850 + 45 = 1,795$; corrected profit $13,480 - 1,795 = 11,685$.
:::

## Concepts and the adjustments they explain

| Adjustment | Main concept |
| --- | --- |
| Accrued and prepaid expenses and income | Matching (accruals) |
| Depreciation of non-current assets | Matching (also consistency in the choice of method) |
| Allowance (provision) for doubtful debts | Prudence |
| Inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value | Prudence |
| Same depreciation method each year | Consistency |
| Small items of equipment charged as expenses | Materiality |
| Credit sales recognised when goods are delivered | Realisation |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking matching is about cash"}
Matching ignores **when** money moves. An expense incurred but unpaid belongs to this year; an expense paid in advance belongs to next year.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using prudence to hide profit"}
Prudence means not overstating. Deliberately making profit as low as possible (for example, an allowance far bigger than the expected bad debts) breaks prudence's purpose and misleads users.
:::

:::mistake{title="Recording orders as sales"}
An order or a deposit is not revenue. Under realisation, revenue arises when the goods are delivered or the service performed.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "explain how the concept applies", write two sentences: what the concept says, then what it means **for this item** (for example, "Matching: the US\$175 owing relates to 2025, so it is added to the electricity expense.").
:::

:::exam-tip
In a profit correction, state for each item whether profit goes **up** or **down** and by how much before you total the effect.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Matching (accruals): income and expenses belong to the period in which they are earned or incurred; it explains accruals, prepayments and depreciation.
- Prudence: do not overstate assets or profit; recognise expected losses now; it explains inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value and the allowance for doubtful debts.
- Consistency: use the same methods each year so results can be compared.
- Materiality: very small items can be treated in the simplest way, such as expensing a stapler.
- Realisation: revenue is recognised when goods are delivered or services performed, not when an order or deposit is received.
:::
