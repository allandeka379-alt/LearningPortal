## Introduction

Every subject has its own vocabulary, and accounting is no different. When a carpenter in Mutare says "I have a lot of money in the business", an accountant wants to know: is it in the bank, tied up in timber, owed by customers, or already spent on a pick-up truck? Is it the carpenter's own money or borrowed? The basic terms in this lesson let you answer those questions precisely.

These words appear in almost every question you will meet, from Form 1 to the final examination. Paper 1 multiple-choice questions often test a single definition or ask you to classify one item. Structured questions expect you to sort a list of balances into assets, liabilities, income and expenses before you can prepare any statement. If the classification is wrong, every total that follows is wrong, so it pays to learn these terms properly now.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Business"}
An organisation that provides goods or services to customers, usually with the aim of making a profit.
:::

:::definition{term="Transaction"}
An event that changes the financial position of a business and can be measured in money, such as a sale, a purchase, a payment or a receipt.
:::

:::definition{term="Book-keeping"}
The detailed, day-to-day recording of financial transactions in the books of the business.
:::

:::definition{term="Accounting"}
Using the records to prepare financial statements, and analysing and interpreting them so that people can make decisions.
:::

:::definition{term="Capital"}
The owner's investment in the business: the amount the business owes to its owner.
:::

:::definition{term="Assets"}
Resources owned or controlled by the business from which it expects to benefit.
:::

:::definition{term="Liabilities"}
Amounts owed by the business to people or organisations other than the owner.
:::

:::definition{term="Income"}
Money earned by the business from its normal activities, such as sales of goods, fees for services, rent received and commission received.
:::

:::definition{term="Expenses"}
The costs of running the business in a period, such as wages, rent, electricity, fuel and insurance.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit and loss"}
Profit is income greater than expenses. A loss is expenses greater than income.
:::

:::definition{term="Drawings"}
Money, goods or other assets taken out of the business by the owner for personal use.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards use the terms in this lesson. Cambridge often calls capital **owner's equity**. You may also meet older words: **debtors** for trade receivables, **creditors** for trade payables and **stock** for inventory.
:::

## Assets: non-current and current

Assets are divided by how long the business expects to keep them.

**Non-current assets** are kept for more than a year to help run the business, not for resale: land and buildings, machinery, motor vehicles, fixtures and fittings, office equipment and computers.

**Current assets** are cash, or items that are expected to turn into cash within a year: inventory (goods held for resale), trade receivables (customers who owe the business for goods sold on credit), other receivables, bank and cash.

The same item can be classified differently in different businesses. A pick-up truck is a non-current asset for a carpenter who uses it to deliver furniture, but it is inventory (a current asset) for a car dealer who bought it to sell.

## Liabilities: non-current and current

**Non-current liabilities** are due for payment after more than twelve months, such as a long-term bank loan or a mortgage.

**Current liabilities** are due within twelve months: trade payables (suppliers owed for goods bought on credit), other payables, a bank overdraft, and the part of a loan due within the year.

:::worked-solution{title="Example 1: classifying the balances of a carpentry workshop"}
Farai owns a carpentry workshop in Mutare. At 31 December 2025 he lists these items.

| Item | US\$ | Classification |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Workshop building | 12,000 | Non-current asset |
| Woodworking machinery | 4,300 | Non-current asset |
| Pick-up truck | 7,500 | Non-current asset |
| Inventory of timber | 1,640 | Current asset |
| Trade receivables (customers who owe for furniture) | 920 | Current asset |
| Bank | 1,385 | Current asset |
| Trade payables (owed to a timber supplier) | 1,060 | Current liability |
| Loan repayable in 2030 | 6,000 | Non-current liability |

1. Non-current assets: 12,000 + 4,300 + 7,500 = US\$23,800.
2. Current assets: 1,640 + 920 + 1,385 = US\$3,945.
3. Total assets: 23,800 + 3,945 = US\$27,745.
4. Total liabilities: 1,060 + 6,000 = US\$7,060.
5. Capital = assets − liabilities = 27,745 − 7,060 = US\$20,685.
:::

## Income, expenses, profit and drawings

Assets and liabilities describe the business at **one moment**. Income and expenses describe what happened **over a period**, such as a month or a year. The difference between them is the profit or loss for that period.

Be careful with three kinds of payment that are **not** expenses:

- Buying a non-current asset (a new machine) is **capital expenditure**: the machine is an asset, not a cost of the period.
- Repaying a loan reduces a liability; only the interest on the loan is an expense.
- **Drawings** are taken by the owner for personal use. They reduce capital, not profit.

:::worked-solution{title="Example 2: a kombi operator's month"}
Sipho runs a kombi between Bulawayo city centre and the western suburbs. His records for November 2025 show the following.

| Item | US\$ | Classification |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Fares received | 3,480 | Income |
| Fuel | 1,260 | Expense |
| Driver's wages | 640 | Expense |
| Repairs to the kombi | 215 | Expense |
| Licence and insurance | 180 | Expense |
| Money Sipho took for groceries | 400 | Drawings |

1. Income: US\$3,480.
2. Expenses: 1,260 + 640 + 215 + 180 = US\$2,295.
3. Profit = income − expenses = 3,480 − 2,295 = US\$1,185.
4. The US\$400 for groceries is drawings. It is left out of the profit calculation and is deducted from Sipho's capital instead.
:::

If Sipho had wrongly treated the groceries as an expense, he would have reported a profit of only US\$785 and understated how well the kombi performed.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing book-keeping with accounting"}
Book-keeping is recording. Accounting goes further: summarising the records into statements and interpreting them. A clerk entering invoices is book-keeping; a report explaining why profit fell is accounting.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling inventory a non-current asset"}
Goods bought for resale are inventory, a **current** asset, however long they sit on the shelf. Non-current assets are kept to run the business.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating drawings or loan repayments as expenses"}
Neither reduces profit. Drawings reduce capital; a loan repayment reduces a liability. Only loan **interest** is an expense.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing a bank overdraft as an asset"}
An overdraft is money owed **to** the bank, so it is a current liability. A positive bank balance is a current asset.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Define" questions need a complete sentence that would make sense to someone who has never studied accounting. Add an example from the question's business if you can: "Liabilities are amounts the business owes to outsiders, for example the loan from the bank."
:::

:::exam-tip
When a question gives a list of balances, label each one (NCA, CA, CL, NCL, I, E, D) in the margin before you start any statement. It takes a minute and prevents most classification errors.
:::

:::exam-tip
Think about the purpose of the item in *this* business. Motor vehicles, computers and furniture can be inventory for a dealer but non-current assets for everyone else.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A transaction is any event, measurable in money, that changes the financial position of the business.
- Book-keeping records transactions; accounting summarises, analyses and interprets them.
- Assets are non-current (kept to run the business) or current (cash or turning into cash within a year); liabilities are non-current (due after a year) or current.
- Capital is what the business owes its owner; drawings reduce it.
- Profit = income − expenses for a period; drawings, loan repayments and purchases of non-current assets are not expenses.
:::
