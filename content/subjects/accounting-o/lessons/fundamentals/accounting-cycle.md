## Introduction

Tatenda opens a small grocery shop in Kwekwe. In his first month he banks his savings, buys goods from a wholesaler, sells to customers, pays rent and pays part of what he owes. At the end of the month he wants to know one thing: did he make a profit?

The answer does not come from one notebook. Information flows through a fixed sequence of steps called the **accounting cycle**. Each step takes the output of the step before and organises it a little more, until hundreds of separate transactions become one short income statement and one statement of financial position. Then the cycle starts again for the next period.

The ZIMSEC syllabus asks you to define, illustrate and explain the stages **in order**. Questions often give a diagram with gaps, ask you to name the stage after a given one, or ask you to trace a single transaction from start to finish.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Accounting cycle"}
The sequence of stages by which financial transactions are recorded, classified and summarised into financial statements, repeated for every accounting period.
:::

:::definition{term="Source document"}
The original document that provides evidence of a transaction, such as an invoice, receipt, credit note, cheque counterfoil or bank statement.
:::

:::definition{term="Subsidiary book (book of original entry)"}
A book in which transactions are first listed from their source documents before being posted to the ledger: the sales, purchases and returns journals, the cash book, the petty cash book and the general journal.
:::

:::definition{term="Ledger"}
The main book of accounts, containing an account for each asset, liability, item of capital, income and expense, where every transaction is recorded by double entry.
:::

:::definition{term="Trial balance"}
A list of the balances on the ledger accounts at a date, in debit and credit columns, used to check that the double entry is arithmetically correct.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC calls the books where transactions are first recorded **subsidiary books** or **books of original entry**. Cambridge calls them **books of prime entry**. They are the same books.
:::

## The six stages

| Stage | What happens | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Transaction | An event measurable in money takes place | Goods are bought on credit |
| 2. Source document | The transaction is evidenced on paper or electronically | The supplier's invoice is received |
| 3. Subsidiary book | The document is listed in the correct book of original entry | The invoice is entered in the purchases journal |
| 4. Ledger | Totals or individual items are posted by double entry | Debit purchases account; credit the supplier's account |
| 5. Trial balance | Ledger balances are listed to check arithmetic | Purchases appears in the debit column |
| 6. Financial statements | Balances are summarised to show profit and position | Purchases is used to find cost of sales in the income statement |

After the statements are prepared, the balances on asset, liability and capital accounts are carried forward, and the cycle begins again with the next period's transactions.

Each stage has a purpose:

- **Source documents** prove that the transaction happened and give the details (date, amount, parties). Without them nothing should be recorded.
- **Subsidiary books** sort transactions by type, so that, for example, all credit sales are in one place. They save time, because only totals of similar items need to be posted to the ledger.
- The **ledger** classifies the effect of each transaction on each account using double entry.
- The **trial balance** checks that total debits equal total credits. It does not prove that there are no errors, but it finds many of them.
- The **financial statements** summarise the period: the income statement shows profit or loss for the period, and the statement of financial position shows assets, liabilities and capital at the end of it.

:::worked-solution{title="Example 1: tracing one credit sale through the cycle"}
On 12 June 2025 Chipo, who sells school uniforms in Gweru, sells 20 blazers on credit to Kudzai Primary School for US\$900.

| Stage | Chipo's record |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Transaction | Credit sale of blazers, US\$900 |
| 2. Source document | Sales invoice sent to the school (Chipo keeps a copy) |
| 3. Subsidiary book | Entered in the sales journal |
| 4. Ledger | Debit Kudzai Primary School account US\$900; credit sales account (with the journal total) |
| 5. Trial balance | Kudzai Primary School appears as a debit balance if still unpaid; sales as a credit balance |
| 6. Financial statements | Sales appear in the income statement; the amount owed appears as a trade receivable in the statement of financial position |

1. Notice that one transaction affects **two** ledger accounts (double entry) and **both** financial statements.
2. If Chipo had sold for cash, the source document would be a receipt or till roll and the subsidiary book would be the cash book.
:::

## A full month through the cycle

The next example takes five transactions all the way through. Tatenda sells all his goods by the end of the month, so he has no closing inventory.

:::worked-solution{title="Example 2: Tatenda Traders, March 2025"}
**Stages 1 to 3: transactions, documents and subsidiary books**

| Date | Transaction | Source document | Subsidiary book |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Mar | Paid US\$5,000 of his savings into a business bank account | Bank deposit slip | Cash book |
| 3 Mar | Bought goods on credit from Moyo Wholesalers, US\$1,800 | Purchase invoice | Purchases journal |
| 8 Mar | Banked cash sales, US\$2,650 | Till rolls and deposit slip | Cash book |
| 15 Mar | Paid rent by bank transfer, US\$400 | Transfer confirmation | Cash book |
| 20 Mar | Paid Moyo Wholesalers US\$1,000 by bank transfer | Transfer confirmation | Cash book |

**Stage 4: the ledger (two of the accounts)**

**Bank account**

| Date | Details | US\$ | Date | Details | US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- | --- | ---: |
| 1 Mar | Capital | 5,000 | 15 Mar | Rent | 400 |
| 8 Mar | Sales | 2,650 | 20 Mar | Moyo Wholesalers | 1,000 |
|  |  |  | 31 Mar | Balance c/d | 6,250 |
|  |  | **7,650** |  |  | **7,650** |
| 1 Apr | Balance b/d | 6,250 |  |  |  |

**Moyo Wholesalers account**

| Date | Details | US\$ | Date | Details | US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- | --- | ---: |
| 20 Mar | Bank | 1,000 | 3 Mar | Purchases | 1,800 |
| 31 Mar | Balance c/d | 800 |  |  |  |
|  |  | **1,800** |  |  | **1,800** |
|  |  |  | 1 Apr | Balance b/d | 800 |

The other accounts (capital, purchases, sales, rent) each have one entry.

**Stage 5: the trial balance**

**Tatenda Traders**

**Trial balance at 31 March 2025**

| Account | Debit US\$ | Credit US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Bank | 6,250 |  |
| Purchases | 1,800 |  |
| Rent | 400 |  |
| Capital |  | 5,000 |
| Sales |  | 2,650 |
| Moyo Wholesalers |  | 800 |
| **Total** | **8,450** | **8,450** |

**Stage 6: the income statement**

**Tatenda Traders**

**Income statement**

**for the month ended 31 March 2025**

|  | US\$ | US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Sales |  | 2,650 |
| Less cost of sales (purchases) |  | (1,800) |
| **Gross profit** |  | 850 |
| Less rent |  | (400) |
| **Profit for the month** |  | 450 |

1. The bank balance is 5,000 + 2,650 − 400 − 1,000 = US\$6,250.
2. The trial balance totals agree at US\$8,450, so the arithmetic of the double entry is correct.
3. Profit = 2,650 − 1,800 − 400 = US\$450. Tatenda's statement of financial position would show bank US\$6,250 as an asset, Moyo Wholesalers US\$800 as a current liability and capital of 5,000 + 450 = US\$5,450, which agrees: 6,250 = 5,450 + 800.
:::

## Manual and computerised cycles

In a computerised system the same stages still exist, but some happen automatically: once an invoice is keyed in, the software updates the ledger accounts, the trial balance and the draft statements. The source document and the first entry still need care, because a wrong entry flows through every later stage.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting the stages in the wrong order"}
A common error is to place the ledger before the subsidiary books. Remember: documents are listed in the subsidiary books **first**, then posted to the ledger.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking the trial balance proves the books are correct"}
The trial balance checks arithmetic only. Some errors, such as entering a transaction in the wrong customer's account, leave the totals equal.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that the cycle repeats"}
The cycle is a loop, not a line. The closing balances of one period become the opening balances of the next.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Learn the order with a short phrase: **T**ransactions, **S**ource documents, **S**ubsidiary books, **L**edger, **T**rial balance, **F**inancial statements.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to "illustrate" the cycle, draw it as a loop of labelled boxes with arrows, and add one example under each box.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The accounting cycle has six stages: transaction, source document, subsidiary book, ledger, trial balance, financial statements.
- Source documents are the evidence; subsidiary books sort transactions by type; the ledger records double entry.
- The trial balance checks the arithmetic of the ledger but does not reveal every error.
- The financial statements summarise the period; closing balances carry forward and the cycle repeats.
:::
