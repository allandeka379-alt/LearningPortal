## Introduction

Chipo sells tomatoes, onions and leafy vegetables from a stall at Mbare Musika in Harare. Every morning she buys fresh produce from farmers' lorries, and every evening she goes home with a bag of notes and coins. Some weeks the bag feels heavy, some weeks light. Is the stall making a profit? Would she be better off selling only tomatoes, or opening on Sundays? Without records she can only guess.

This lesson explains what accounting is for. It is the first topic in both syllabuses, and examiners test it with "state", "explain" and "distinguish" questions: the difference between book-keeping and accounting, the reasons for keeping records, why profit is measured, and how the information helps owners and managers. These ideas also give the reasons behind everything else you will learn, from source documents to ratio analysis.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Book-keeping"}
The detailed, day-to-day recording of the financial transactions of a business in its books, such as journals, ledgers or a computer system.
:::

:::definition{term="Accounting"}
The process of classifying and summarising the recorded transactions into financial statements, and then analysing and interpreting the results so that people can make decisions.
:::

:::definition{term="Financial statements"}
Summaries prepared from the records, mainly the income statement (statement of profit or loss), which shows the profit or loss for a period, and the statement of financial position, which shows assets, liabilities and capital at a date.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit"}
The amount by which the income of a period is greater than the expenses of the same period. If expenses are greater, the result is a loss.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC usually says **income statement** (or trading and profit and loss account) and **net profit**. Cambridge says **statement of profit or loss** and **profit for the year**. They mean the same thing.
:::

## Book-keeping and accounting

Book-keeping and accounting are two stages of the same job. Book-keeping comes first: every sale, purchase, payment and receipt is written down (or keyed in) from its source document. It is careful, routine work, and it must be accurate and complete.

Accounting builds on the records. The accountant groups similar transactions together, prepares financial statements, and then asks *what do these figures tell us?* Is the business more profitable than last year? Can it pay its debts? Which product earns the most? Accounting turns **data** into **information**.

| Book-keeping | Accounting |
| --- | --- |
| Records individual transactions | Summarises transactions into statements |
| Routine and day-to-day | Done at the end of a period (month, year) |
| Answers "what happened?" | Answers "what does it mean, and what should we do?" |
| Example: writing a credit sale in the sales journal | Example: explaining why profit fell and advising on prices |

## Why businesses keep records

A business keeps accounting records so that it can:

- **know what it owns and owes**, such as the cash in hand, the amount in the bank, the inventory, and who owes the business money and whom it owes;
- **measure its profit or loss** for each period;
- **control its assets**, for example by checking cash counted against cash recorded, which helps detect errors and theft;
- **provide evidence** to banks when asking for a loan, to the tax authority (ZIMRA) for tax, and in disputes with customers or suppliers;
- **plan and make decisions** using facts instead of guesses.

Memory is not enough. Even a small trader makes hundreds of transactions a month, and a mistake of a few dollars a day adds up.

:::worked-solution{title="Example 1: from Chipo's records to her weekly profit"}
Chipo started to write down, each evening, her sales, the produce she bought that morning and her daily market fee. She sells all her produce each day, so she has no inventory left over. Her notebook for one week shows:

| Day | Sales US\$ | Produce bought US\$ | Market fee US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Monday | 86 | 52 | 2 |
| Tuesday | 74 | 45 | 2 |
| Wednesday | 92 | 58 | 2 |
| Thursday | 68 | 40 | 2 |
| Friday | 115 | 70 | 2 |
| Saturday | 140 | 88 | 2 |
| **Total** | **575** | **353** | **12** |

1. The daily entries are **book-keeping**: each one records what happened.
2. Adding them up and working out the result is **accounting**: income US\$575 − (produce US\$353 + fees US\$12) = profit of US\$210 for the week.
3. Interpreting the figures is also accounting: Friday and Saturday together brought in US\$255 of the US\$575 sales, so those are the days when an extra helper or extra stock would be most useful.
:::

## Why measure profit or loss?

Profit is the main reason most businesses exist, and measuring it properly matters because:

- it shows whether the business is **worth continuing**, or whether the owner would earn more elsewhere;
- it tells the owner how much can safely be **taken as drawings** without running the business down;
- retained profit is a source of finance for **expansion**, such as a second stall or a new machine;
- lenders want to see that profits are high enough to **repay loans and interest**;
- **tax** is calculated on profit;
- comparing profit over time or with other businesses shows whether performance is **improving**.

Note that profit is not the same as cash. A business can have money in the bank and still be making a loss, for example if it has just borrowed, or make a profit and be short of cash because customers have not yet paid.

## Monitoring progress and making decisions

Accounting information is most useful when it is compared: this year with last year, this month with the budget, one product with another. The comparison shows **trends** and raises **questions**, and the answers lead to decisions.

:::worked-solution{title="Example 2: Farai's bakery compares two half-years"}
Farai runs a bakery in Bulawayo. His accountant prepares summaries for the two halves of 2025.

|  | January–June US\$ | July–December US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Sales | 18,400 | 21,900 |
| Ingredients | 7,360 | 9,855 |
| Wages | 4,200 | 4,800 |
| Electricity | 1,150 | 1,640 |
| Rent | 1,800 | 1,800 |
| **Total expenses** | **14,510** | **18,095** |
| **Profit** | **3,890** | **3,805** |

1. Sales rose by 21,900 − 18,400 = US\$3,500, yet profit fell by 3,890 − 3,805 = US\$85. Monitoring shows that growth has not improved results.
2. Ingredients cost US\$40 for every US\$100 of sales in the first half (7,360 ÷ 18,400 = 0.40) but US\$45 in the second half (9,855 ÷ 21,900 = 0.45). Electricity rose by US\$490.
3. Possible decisions: compare flour suppliers or buy in bulk, review selling prices, and check whether a gas oven would reduce the power bill. The records do not make the decision, but they show Farai where to look.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying book-keeping and accounting are the same"}
In a "distinguish" question you must state both sides: book-keeping **records**; accounting **summarises, analyses and interprets** the records.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing profit with cash"}
Having more money in the bank does not prove that a business is profitable. Profit is income less expenses for a period; cash can come from loans or capital.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving vague reasons"}
"To know how the business is doing" earns little. Be specific: "to calculate the profit or loss so that the owner knows how much she can take as drawings".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Explain why" questions need a reason **and** a consequence. "Records help control cash, because cash counted can be compared with cash recorded, so theft or errors are found quickly."
:::

:::exam-tip
Use the business in the question. If it is a market vendor, mention stock bought daily and cash takings; if it needs a loan, mention evidence for the bank.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Book-keeping records transactions day by day; accounting summarises, analyses and interprets them.
- Records show what the business owns and owes, measure profit, help control assets and provide evidence for banks and ZIMRA.
- Profit is measured to judge performance, set safe drawings, finance growth, support loan applications and calculate tax.
- Comparing figures over time reveals trends that guide decisions.
- Profit and cash are different: a profitable business can be short of cash.
:::
