## Introduction

Tendai has saved US\$3,000 from piece jobs and wants to open a tuck shop near a secondary school in Masvingo. Before she sells a single packet of biscuits she already has a business with resources (the money in the bank) and a source for those resources (her own savings). Every day after that, something she does will change what the business owns and what it owes.

The **accounting equation** is the simple rule that keeps track of all of this. It says that everything a business owns has been paid for either by the owner or by someone the business owes money to. It is the foundation of double entry book-keeping and of the statement of financial position (balance sheet), so it appears early in both the ZIMSEC and the Cambridge syllabuses. Expect multiple-choice questions that ask for a missing figure or the effect of a transaction, and structured questions where you show the equation after a series of transactions and then prepare a statement of financial position.

Before you start, make sure you know the basic terms: assets, liabilities, capital, profit and drawings.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Assets"}
Resources owned or controlled by the business that it expects to benefit from, such as premises, equipment, vehicles, inventory, money owed by customers (trade receivables), bank and cash.
:::

:::definition{term="Liabilities"}
Amounts the business owes to people outside the business, such as suppliers (trade payables), loans and a bank overdraft.
:::

:::definition{term="Capital (owner's equity)"}
The amount the business owes to its owner: the resources the owner has put in, plus profits kept in the business, less drawings and losses.
:::

:::formula{name="The accounting equation"}
$$
\text{Assets} = \text{Capital} + \text{Liabilities}
$$
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards use **capital** for what the business owes its owner. Cambridge also calls it **owner's equity**. The statement of financial position is sometimes still called a **balance sheet**.
:::

## Why the equation always balances

The business is treated as separate from its owner (the **business entity** idea). So when Tendai puts US\$3,000 into the business, the business has an asset of US\$3,000 in the bank, and it also "owes" Tendai US\$3,000 as capital. Both sides of the equation go up by the same amount.

Every transaction has **two effects** (the dual aspect). Because of this, the equation stays in balance after every transaction. A transaction can:

- increase an asset and increase a liability or capital (buying goods on credit);
- decrease an asset and decrease a liability or capital (paying a supplier);
- increase one asset and decrease another asset (buying equipment for cash);
- increase one liability and decrease another (rare at this level).

### Rearranging the equation

You can rearrange the equation to find any one figure when you know the other two:

- Capital = Assets − Liabilities
- Liabilities = Assets − Capital
- Assets = Capital + Liabilities

Capital worked out as assets minus liabilities is often called **net assets**: what would be left for the owner if every asset were turned into cash at its book value and every liability were paid.

:::worked-solution{title="Example 1: finding the missing figure"}
Three small businesses each have one figure missing. Find it by rearranging the equation.

| Business | Assets US\$ | Liabilities US\$ | Capital US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Chipo's hair salon, Harare | 8,400 | 1,900 | 6,500 |
| Sipho's transport service, Bulawayo | 36,250 | 12,750 | 23,500 |
| Nyasha's market stall, Mutare | 2,860 | 715 | 2,145 |

1. Chipo: capital is missing, so capital = assets − liabilities = 8,400 − 1,900 = US\$6,500.
2. Sipho: assets are missing, so assets = capital + liabilities = 23,500 + 12,750 = US\$36,250.
3. Nyasha: liabilities are missing, so liabilities = assets − capital = 2,860 − 2,145 = US\$715.
4. Check each row: assets always equal capital plus liabilities.
:::

## The effect of transactions

The best way to see the equation working is to follow a business through its first month. For each transaction ask two questions: *which two items change?* and *does each one go up or down?*

:::worked-solution{title="Example 2: Tendai's Tuck Shop in December 2025"}
Tendai's transactions for December 2025 were:

- 1 Dec: pays US\$3,000 of her savings into a business bank account.
- 3 Dec: buys shop fittings for US\$800, paying by bank transfer.
- 5 Dec: buys inventory for US\$1,200 on credit from Farai Wholesalers.
- 10 Dec: receives a three-year loan of US\$2,000 from a bank.
- 18 Dec: pays Farai Wholesalers US\$700 by bank transfer.
- 22 Dec: sells goods that cost US\$400 for US\$650; the money is banked.
- 28 Dec: takes US\$150 from the business bank account for personal use.

The table shows every balance (US\$) after each transaction.

| Date | Bank | Shop fittings | Inventory | Total assets | Trade payables | Loan | Capital | Liabilities + capital |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 Dec | 3,000 | 0 | 0 | **3,000** | 0 | 0 | 3,000 | **3,000** |
| 3 Dec | 2,200 | 800 | 0 | **3,000** | 0 | 0 | 3,000 | **3,000** |
| 5 Dec | 2,200 | 800 | 1,200 | **4,200** | 1,200 | 0 | 3,000 | **4,200** |
| 10 Dec | 4,200 | 800 | 1,200 | **6,200** | 1,200 | 2,000 | 3,000 | **6,200** |
| 18 Dec | 3,500 | 800 | 1,200 | **5,500** | 500 | 2,000 | 3,000 | **5,500** |
| 22 Dec | 4,150 | 800 | 800 | **5,750** | 500 | 2,000 | 3,250 | **5,750** |
| 28 Dec | 4,000 | 800 | 800 | **5,600** | 500 | 2,000 | 3,100 | **5,600** |

1. 1 Dec: an asset (bank) and capital both rise by US\$3,000.
2. 3 Dec: one asset rises (fittings +800) and another falls (bank −800). Total assets do not change.
3. 5 Dec: an asset (inventory) and a liability (trade payables) both rise by US\$1,200.
4. 10 Dec: an asset (bank) and a liability (loan) both rise by US\$2,000.
5. 18 Dec: an asset (bank) and a liability (trade payables) both fall by US\$700.
6. 22 Dec: inventory falls by US\$400 and bank rises by US\$650, so assets rise by US\$250. That increase is the **profit** on the sale, and profit belongs to the owner, so capital rises by US\$250.
7. 28 Dec: money taken for personal use is **drawings**. Bank and capital both fall by US\$150.
:::

Notice the 22 December line. Profit is the only reason capital grows without the owner putting in more money, and a loss or drawings are what make it shrink. That gives a second, very useful form of the equation:

:::formula{name="Closing capital"}
$$
\text{Closing capital} = \text{Opening capital} + \text{Capital introduced} + \text{Profit} - \text{Drawings}
$$
:::

A loss is subtracted instead of adding a profit. This form is used again later for incomplete records.

## From the equation to a statement of financial position

A **statement of financial position** is the accounting equation set out in detail at one date. The top half lists the assets; the bottom half shows where they came from: capital and liabilities. Assets are grouped as **non-current** (kept for more than a year to run the business) and **current** (cash or expected to turn into cash within a year). Liabilities are grouped the same way.

:::worked-solution{title="Example 3: Tendai's statement of financial position"}
Using the closing balances from Example 2:

**Tendai's Tuck Shop**

**Statement of financial position**

**at 31 December 2025**

|  | US\$ | US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| **Non-current assets** |  |  |
| Shop fittings |  | 800 |
| **Current assets** |  |  |
| Inventory | 800 |  |
| Bank | 4,000 |  |
|  |  | 4,800 |
| **Total assets** |  | **5,600** |
|  |  |  |
| **Capital** |  |  |
| Capital introduced | 3,000 |  |
| Add profit for the month | 250 |  |
|  | 3,250 |  |
| Less drawings | (150) |  |
|  |  | 3,100 |
| **Non-current liabilities** |  |  |
| Bank loan |  | 2,000 |
| **Current liabilities** |  |  |
| Trade payables |  | 500 |
| **Total capital and liabilities** |  | **5,600** |

1. Total assets: 800 + 4,800 = US\$5,600.
2. Closing capital: 3,000 + 250 − 150 = US\$3,100, the same as the capital column in Example 2.
3. Capital and liabilities: 3,100 + 2,000 + 500 = US\$5,600, which equals total assets.
:::

The loan is a non-current liability because it is repayable after more than a year; the amount owed to Farai Wholesalers is current because it will be paid soon.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating drawings as an expense"}
Drawings are not a business expense and do not reduce profit. They reduce **capital** directly (and an asset such as bank, cash or inventory).
:::

:::mistake{title="Changing only one item"}
Every transaction changes at least two items. If your equation does not balance after a transaction, you have missed the second effect.
:::

:::mistake{title="Recording the selling price as profit"}
When goods costing US\$400 are sold for US\$650, capital rises by the profit of US\$250, not by US\$650. The inventory given up (US\$400) must also come off assets.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a loan capital"}
Money lent by a bank or a relative is a **liability**, even if it was used to start the business. Only the owner's own investment is capital.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "state the effect" questions, name both items and the direction: "Inventory increases by US\$1,200; trade payables increase by US\$1,200." One word such as "increase" without the item earns nothing.
:::

:::exam-tip
In a multiple-choice question about a missing figure, write the equation in the margin and substitute. Many distractors come from adding where you should subtract.
:::

:::exam-tip
After a series of transactions, check that total assets equal capital plus liabilities before you write the statement. If they do not, look first at sales (profit) and drawings.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Assets = Capital + Liabilities, at every moment in the life of a business.
- Rearrange it to find a missing figure: Capital = Assets − Liabilities; Liabilities = Assets − Capital.
- Every transaction has two effects, so the equation always stays in balance.
- Profit increases capital; losses and drawings decrease it: closing capital = opening capital + capital introduced + profit − drawings.
- A statement of financial position is the equation set out in detail at one date, with assets and liabilities split into non-current and current.
:::
