## Introduction

When Rudo's poultry project in Chinhoyi prepares its year-end accounts, she is not the only person who wants to read them. Her bank is deciding whether to lend her money for a new broiler house. The feed supplier wants to know if she can pay on time. Her two workers wonder whether the project can afford a pay rise. ZIMRA needs the profit figure to work out her tax.

Each of these people asks a different question, so each looks at different parts of the accounts. Both syllabuses expect you to name the users (also called **interested parties** or **stakeholders**) and, more importantly, to explain **what decision** each one makes with the information. Cambridge examiners in particular reward answers that link a user to a specific figure and a specific decision.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="User of accounting information"}
Any person or organisation that reads a business's accounts to make a decision about it. Also called an interested party or stakeholder.
:::

:::definition{term="Internal user"}
A user inside the business, such as the owner or a manager, who can see detailed records at any time.
:::

:::definition{term="External user"}
A user outside the business, such as a bank, supplier or the tax authority, who usually sees only the financial statements or the figures the business chooses to share.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC speaks of **users of accounting information** and includes **households** (families keeping their own budgets). Cambridge speaks of **interested parties**. Both cover owners, managers, lenders, suppliers, customers, employees, investors and government.
:::

## Who uses accounting information, and why

The key to every answer is: *user → information → decision*.

| User | Information they look at | Decision they make |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Owner (sole trader or partners) | Profit, capital, drawings | Whether to continue, expand or close; how much to draw |
| Managers | Detailed costs, sales by product, budgets | Pricing, cost control, which products to stock |
| Banks and other lenders | Profit over several years, cash and liquidity, assets for security | Whether to lend, how much, and at what interest |
| Suppliers (trade payables) | Liquidity and payment record | Whether to sell on credit and how much credit to allow |
| Customers | Stability and profit of the business | Whether the business will keep supplying goods, spares and guarantees |
| Employees and trade unions | Profit and future prospects | Whether jobs are secure; whether to ask for higher wages |
| Investors and shareholders | Profit, dividends, growth | Whether to buy, keep or sell shares |
| Government and ZIMRA | Profit, sales, wages paid | Tax payable; economic statistics and policy |
| Club members (clubs and societies) | Income and expenditure, accumulated fund | Whether subscriptions are well used; whether to raise them |
| The public and environmental groups | Spending on the community and the environment | Whether to support or campaign against the business |
| Households (ZIMSEC) | Family income and spending records | How to budget, save and avoid debt |

Owners and managers are the same person in most small businesses: a vendor both owns and manages her stall. In a limited company the shareholders own the business, but directors and managers run it.

## Lenders

A bank lends only if it expects to be repaid with interest. It studies the **profit** over several years (is it steady or rising?), how much of that profit the owner **keeps in the business** after drawings, and what **assets** could be offered as security.

:::worked-solution{title="Example 1: the bank looks at Rudo's poultry project"}
Rudo asks her bank for a loan of US\$12,000, to be repaid in four equal instalments of US\$3,000 a year, with interest of 8% a year (US\$960 in the first year). She hands over a summary of her accounts.

| Year | Profit US\$ | Drawings US\$ | Profit kept in the business US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2023 | 7,420 | 4,800 | 2,620 |
| 2024 | 8,960 | 5,100 | 3,860 |
| 2025 | 10,350 | 5,400 | 4,950 |

1. The yearly cost of the loan in the first year is 3,000 + 960 = US\$3,960.
2. Profit kept in 2025 was 10,350 − 5,400 = US\$4,950, which is more than US\$3,960, so the repayments can be met from profit if results stay at this level.
3. Profit has risen every year (7,420 → 8,960 → 10,350), which suggests the project is stable.
4. The bank may still ask for security, such as the broiler houses, and may advise Rudo not to raise her drawings while the loan is outstanding.
:::

## Suppliers and customers

A supplier who sells on credit is in effect lending goods. It wants to know whether the business has enough cash and near-cash assets (receivables, bank) to pay its debts as they fall due. A customer who pays a deposit, or relies on spare parts and guarantees, wants to know that the business will still exist next year.

:::worked-solution{title="Example 2: a wholesaler decides whether to give credit"}
Thandeka runs a grocery shop in Bulawayo and asks a wholesaler for US\$3,000 of goods on 30 days' credit. The wholesaler asks to see her current assets and current liabilities.

| Item | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Inventory | 6,150 |
| Trade receivables | 1,840 |
| Bank | 410 |
| Trade payables | 5,960 |
| Bank overdraft | 2,200 |

1. Current assets: 6,150 + 1,840 + 410 = US\$8,400. Current liabilities: 5,960 + 2,200 = US\$8,160. The current assets only just cover the current liabilities.
2. Leaving out inventory, which must be sold before it becomes cash, the shop has 1,840 + 410 = US\$2,250 to meet debts of US\$8,160.
3. The wholesaler may decide to give a smaller amount of credit, ask for part payment in cash, or ask for evidence of her monthly takings first.
4. Thandeka's own accounts have helped an **outsider** make a decision about her business.
:::

## Other users

**Employees** read the accounts to judge job security and whether a pay rise is affordable: a rising profit strengthens their case. **Investors** compare profit and dividends with what they could earn elsewhere. **The government** uses accounts for tax: ZIMRA assesses income tax on profit, and sales figures matter for VAT. **The public**, local communities and environmental groups are interested in jobs, community projects and pollution, which some larger businesses now report on. **Club members** read the income and expenditure account to see whether their subscriptions are well spent.

ZIMSEC adds **households**. A family that records its income and spending can plan a budget for school fees, food and transport, and avoid borrowing it cannot repay. The same habits of recording and summarising that a business uses help a household make decisions.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Naming a user with no use"}
"The bank" alone earns little. Say what it looks at and what it decides: "The bank checks that profit is high enough to cover the loan repayments before deciding to lend."
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving every user the same reason"}
"To see if the business is profitable" fits almost everyone. Make each use specific: suppliers care about liquidity, employees about job security, ZIMRA about tax.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing owners and managers in a company"}
In a limited company the shareholders own the business, while the directors and managers run it. Their information needs differ.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For a 2-mark "explain" question about one user, write **what they look at** (1 mark) and **the decision** (1 mark).
:::

:::exam-tip
Read the stimulus. If it mentions a loan application, the user is a lender; if it mentions credit terms, it is a supplier. Use the figures given to support your answer.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Users (interested parties) read accounts to make decisions about a business.
- Internal users (owners, managers) see detailed records; external users usually see only the statements.
- Lenders look at profit and security; suppliers at liquidity; employees at job security and pay; investors at returns.
- ZIMRA uses profit to assess tax; club members judge how subscriptions are used; the public looks at social and environmental effects.
- ZIMSEC includes households, which use records to budget.
- Always link user → information → decision.
:::
