## Introduction

Walk down any street in Mutare and you will pass every kind of business. A tuck shop buys sweets and drinks and sells them on. A furniture workshop turns timber into beds and tables. A hair salon sells a service: nothing is handed over except the finished hairstyle. Further on there is a law firm run by three partners, and a bottling plant owned by hundreds of shareholders.

Accounting records look different for each of these. A manufacturer needs a manufacturing account, a trader needs a trading section with cost of sales, and a service business has neither. The form of ownership decides who provides the capital, who takes the profit and who pays the debts if things go wrong. Examiners ask you to state and distinguish the types of activity and of organisation, and to give the advantages and disadvantages of being a sole trader.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Trading business"}
A business that buys goods and resells them, usually at a higher price, without changing them. Examples: a grocery shop, a hardware shop, a market vendor.
:::

:::definition{term="Manufacturing business"}
A business that converts raw materials into finished goods and sells them. Examples: a furniture maker, a bakery, a clothing factory.
:::

:::definition{term="Service business"}
A business that sells skills or work rather than goods. Examples: a hair salon, a driving school, a transport operator, a doctor.
:::

:::definition{term="Sole trader"}
A business owned by one person, who provides the capital, makes the decisions, keeps all the profit and has unlimited liability for its debts.
:::

:::definition{term="Partnership"}
A business owned by two or more people (normally up to twenty) who share the capital, the management and the profits or losses in an agreed way.
:::

:::definition{term="Limited company"}
A business owned by shareholders. It is a separate legal person, and the liability of each shareholder is limited to the amount invested in shares.
:::

:::definition{term="Unlimited liability"}
The owner is personally responsible for all the debts of the business, so private possessions can be sold to pay business creditors.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC often says **sole proprietor** (or sole proprietorship); Cambridge says **sole trader**. Both mean a business with one owner. ZIMSEC also counts informal **vendors** and small to medium enterprises (SMEs) as sole traders.
:::

## Three types of business activity

The activity decides what the business sells and therefore which accounts it prepares.

- A **trading** business earns its gross profit from the difference between what it pays for goods and what it sells them for. Its main current asset is often inventory.
- A **manufacturing** business also buys materials, but it pays workers and runs machines to turn them into products. Its costs include raw materials, factory wages and factory overheads.
- A **service** business sells time and skill. It has no goods for resale, so it has no cost of sales; its income is called fees, commission or takings.

Some businesses do more than one thing. A bakery that also sells soft drinks made by someone else is manufacturing (bread) and trading (drinks). In the exam, classify by the main activity unless the question asks about a particular item.

:::worked-solution{title="Example 1: classifying businesses in Masvingo"}
Classify each business by its main activity and give a reason.

| Business | Activity | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rudo's grocery shop | Trading | Buys goods from wholesalers and resells them unchanged |
| Farai's furniture workshop | Manufacturing | Turns timber, glue and varnish into beds and wardrobes |
| Nyasha's driving school | Service | Sells lessons (skill and time), not goods |
| A maize mill grinding maize into mealie-meal for sale | Manufacturing | Changes grain into a different product |
| Sipho's kombi transport | Service | Sells journeys; passengers receive no goods |

1. Ask: *does the business sell goods?* If not, it is a service business.
2. If it sells goods, ask: *does it change them before selling?* If yes, it is manufacturing; if no, it is trading.
:::

## Forms of business organisation

The table compares the three forms you must know. Partnerships and companies are studied in more detail later in the course.

| Feature | Sole trader | Partnership | Limited company |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Owners | One person | 2 to 20 partners | Shareholders (no maximum) |
| Capital from | The owner's savings and loans | All the partners | Selling shares |
| Liability | Unlimited | Usually unlimited | Limited to the shares bought |
| Control | The owner alone | Shared by the partners | Directors, elected by shareholders |
| Profit | All kept by the owner | Shared in the agreed ratio | Paid as dividends; some retained |
| Separate legal person? | No | No | Yes |
| Example | A vendor, a tuck shop | A firm of accountants | A bottling company |

A partnership is usually governed by a **partnership agreement** that states the capital each partner provides and how profits are shared. A limited company is formed by registering it, and it continues even when shareholders sell their shares or die.

## Advantages and disadvantages of a sole trader

Most businesses in Zimbabwe, from market vendors to small workshops, are sole traders. The form is popular because it is simple, but it carries risks.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Easy and cheap to start, with few legal formalities | Unlimited liability: personal assets are at risk |
| The owner makes all decisions quickly | Capital is limited to the owner's savings and what she can borrow |
| The owner keeps all the profit | Long hours and no one to share the work or the decisions |
| Close contact with customers; personal service | The business may close if the owner is ill or dies |
| Financial affairs stay private | Difficult to expand; banks may see it as risky |

:::worked-solution{title="Example 2: what unlimited liability means for Tendai"}
Tendai ran a hardware shop in Chinhoyi as a sole trader. Sales fell, and he decided to close. He sold every business asset and compared the cash raised with what the business owed.

**Tendai Hardware: position on closure**

|  | US\$ | US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Cash raised from selling the business assets |  |  |
| Inventory | 9,800 |  |
| Delivery truck | 6,300 |  |
| Shop fittings | 1,450 |  |
| Bank | 1,050 |  |
|  |  | 18,600 |
| Less business debts |  |  |
| Trade payables | 9,450 |  |
| Bank loan | 14,000 |  |
|  |  | (23,450) |
| **Shortfall to be paid from Tendai's own assets** |  | (4,850) |

1. The business assets raised US\$18,600, but the business owed US\$23,450.
2. The shortfall is 23,450 − 18,600 = US\$4,850.
3. Because a sole trader has **unlimited liability**, the creditors can claim the US\$4,850 from Tendai personally: his savings, his car or even his house.
4. If the shop had been a limited company, Tendai as a shareholder would have lost only the money he paid for his shares. The creditors would have had to bear the shortfall themselves.
:::

## Choosing a form of organisation

An owner chooses the form that fits the size and risk of the business. A vendor with a few hundred dollars of stock gains little from the cost of forming a company. A business that needs a large amount of capital, for example to build a factory, may form a partnership to bring in more owners and skills, or a limited company so that many investors can buy shares without risking their homes.

Remember that the **business entity** idea applies to every form: the accounts record the business only, even for a sole trader whose business is not a separate legal person. That is why drawings are recorded when the owner takes money for personal use.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling a service business a trader"}
A hair salon may sell a few bottles of shampoo, but its main income is fees for hairdressing. Classify by the main activity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a sole trader's liability is limited"}
Only shareholders of a limited company have limited liability. A sole trader, and normally every partner, can lose personal possessions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up legal and accounting separateness"}
A sole trader's business is not a separate legal person, but it **is** treated as separate in the accounts (business entity). Both statements are true.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In a "distinguish" question, write one sentence for each item using the **same feature**: "A sole trader has one owner, while a partnership has two to twenty owners."
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked for advantages or disadvantages of a sole trader, link each point to the business in the question: "Rudo can decide quickly to stock school uniforms in January because she does not need to consult anyone."
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Trading businesses resell goods; manufacturing businesses make goods; service businesses sell skills.
- A sole trader has one owner who keeps all the profit and has unlimited liability.
- A partnership has 2 to 20 owners who share capital, control and profit in an agreed ratio; liability is usually unlimited.
- A limited company is owned by shareholders, is a separate legal person, and its shareholders have limited liability.
- Sole traders are easy to start and keep all profits, but face unlimited liability, limited capital and heavy workloads.
:::
