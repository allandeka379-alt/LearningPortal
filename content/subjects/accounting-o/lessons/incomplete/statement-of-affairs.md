## Introduction

Rudo runs a tuck shop outside a secondary school in Masvingo. She banks her takings, keeps her bank statements and a notebook of what customers owe her, but she has never kept a full set of double-entry books. At the end of the year she still needs to know whether the shop made a profit, perhaps because a bank wants to see her results before lending her money for a fridge.

Many small Zimbabwean businesses work like this: market vendors, small transport operators, poultry and garden projects. Their records are **incomplete**. Accountants can still work out the profit, either by reconstructing the accounts (the next two lessons) or, when very little information exists, by comparing the owner's capital at the start and end of the year. This lesson covers the second method. Both ZIMSEC (Form 4) and Cambridge 0452 examine it, usually as the first part of a longer incomplete-records question.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC uses the heading **single entry and incomplete records** and calls the final figure **net profit**; Cambridge 0452 says **incomplete records** and **profit for the year**. Trade receivables and trade payables are often called debtors and creditors in ZIMSEC questions. Both boards use the name **statement of affairs**.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Incomplete records (single entry)"}
Accounting records in which transactions are not all recorded using double entry. Usually only a cash book or bank records and lists of amounts owed are kept.
:::

:::definition{term="Statement of affairs"}
A statement listing the assets and liabilities of a business at a given date, prepared when there is no complete ledger. Assets less liabilities gives the owner's capital at that date.
:::

:::definition{term="Capital introduced"}
Additional money or other resources that the owner puts into the business during the year. It increases capital but is not profit.
:::

:::definition{term="Drawings"}
Cash, goods or other assets taken out of the business by the owner for private use. They reduce capital but are not an expense.
:::

## Why some businesses keep incomplete records

Small traders keep incomplete records because full double entry takes time and knowledge that the owner may not have, and because paying a bookkeeper costs money. A one-person business that knows its customers personally may feel it does not need detailed accounts.

**Advantages** of keeping incomplete records:

- They are cheap and quick to keep.
- They need little accounting knowledge.
- They may be enough for a very small business with few transactions.

**Disadvantages:**

- There is no trial balance, so arithmetical errors are not found.
- The owner cannot easily see which expenses are rising or how much each customer owes.
- Profit can only be estimated; an income statement can be prepared only after a lot of reconstruction.
- Theft or loss of cash and goods is hard to detect.
- Lenders and tax authorities may not accept the figures, so borrowing is harder.

## The statement of affairs

A statement of affairs is laid out like a statement of financial position. It lists every asset and every liability the business has on one date, using the **accounting equation**:

**Capital = assets − liabilities**

Values come from whatever evidence exists: a physical count of inventory, the bank statement, lists of customers who owe money and suppliers who are owed, and estimates of what equipment is worth.

## Profit from the change in capital

If capital at the end of the year is higher than at the start, the business has grown. But capital also changes for reasons that are not profit:

- **Drawings** make closing capital smaller, so they must be **added back**.
- **Capital introduced** makes closing capital larger, so it must be **deducted**.

**Profit for the year = closing capital − opening capital + drawings − capital introduced**

A negative answer is a **loss**. This method gives the profit figure but not how it was earned: there is no revenue, cost of sales or list of expenses.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: Rudo's tuck shop]
At 1 January 2025 Rudo had shop equipment worth US\$2,400, inventory US\$1,850, trade receivables US\$620, money in the bank US\$1,340 and cash US\$180. She owed suppliers US\$970 and had expenses owing of US\$90.

At 31 December 2025 the equipment was valued at US\$2,160, inventory US\$2,430, trade receivables US\$810, bank US\$2,275 and cash US\$240. Trade payables were US\$1,180 and expenses owing US\$130. During the year Rudo took US\$300 a month for herself (US\$3,600).

1. Opening capital: assets 2,400 + 3,990 = 6,390, less liabilities 1,060 = 5,330.
2. Closing capital: assets 2,160 + 5,755 = 7,915, less liabilities 1,310 = 6,605.
3. Profit = 6,605 − 5,330 + 3,600 = 4,875.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a loss, a loan and capital introduced]
Farai keeps broiler chickens near Chinhoyi. At 1 January 2025: fowl runs and equipment US\$7,800, inventory of feed and birds US\$2,650, trade receivables US\$1,420, bank US\$930; trade payables US\$1,760.

At 31 December 2025: fowl runs and equipment US\$10,900, inventory US\$3,140, trade receivables US\$1,980, cash US\$210; trade payables US\$2,240, bank overdraft US\$860 and a loan of US\$4,000 repayable in 2029. During the year Farai paid an inheritance of US\$2,500 into the business and took drawings of US\$4,150.

1. Opening capital: 7,800 + 5,000 − 1,760 = 11,040.
2. Closing capital: 10,900 + 5,330 − 3,100 − 4,000 = 9,130. The overdraft is a current liability; the loan is a non-current liability.
3. Result = 9,130 − 11,040 + 4,150 − 2,500 = −260, a loss of US\$260.

## Points to watch when listing assets and liabilities

- **Bank overdraft** is a liability, even though it appears in the "bank" records.
- **Expenses owing** (accrued) are liabilities; **expenses paid in advance** (prepaid) are assets.
- Goods taken by the owner are drawings at **cost**. Add them to cash drawings before using the formula.
- A loan is a liability whoever gave it, but money the **owner** put in is capital introduced, not a loan.
- Non-current assets are shown at their value on the date of the statement (after depreciation) if the question gives it.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Subtracting drawings instead of adding them back"}
Drawings have already reduced closing capital. To find the profit you must add them back. Subtracting them counts the drawings twice.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating capital introduced as profit"}
A lump sum paid in by the owner increases capital but was not earned by trading. Deduct it, or the profit is overstated by that amount.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing a bank overdraft as an asset"}
An overdraft means the business owes the bank. Put it with current liabilities; there is then no "bank" asset on that date.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the opening statement"}
Questions sometimes give only the opening assets and liabilities as a list. You must prepare a statement of affairs at the start date too: the opening capital is not given directly.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Head each statement of affairs with the business name, "Statement of affairs" and the date ("at 1 January 2025"). Its final line is capital, which examiners usually award a mark for on its own.
:::

:::exam-tip
Set out the profit calculation as a small statement with labels (closing capital, add drawings, less capital introduced, less opening capital). A bare figure earns few marks if it is wrong; labelled working earns method marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
"State **two** disadvantages of incomplete records" wants short, distinct points: no trial balance check, no detailed income statement, hard to detect theft, harder to obtain loans.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Incomplete (single-entry) records are cheap and simple but give no arithmetical check and little detail.
- A statement of affairs lists assets and liabilities on one date: capital = assets − liabilities.
- Prepare one at the start and one at the end of the year.
- Profit = closing capital − opening capital + drawings − capital introduced; a negative result is a loss.
- An overdraft and expenses owing are liabilities; prepayments are assets; goods taken are drawings at cost.
:::
