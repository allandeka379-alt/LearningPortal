## Introduction

Nyasha sells vegetables and groceries from a busy stall at a market in Harare and pays a bookkeeper, Sipho, to keep her records. One evening she asks him to "forget" some of the cash takings so that her profit, and her tax, will be lower, and to put her family's electricity bill through the business. Sipho knows how to do it. Should he?

Accounting is only useful if people can **trust** the figures. Banks lend on the strength of financial statements, suppliers give credit, the government collects tax, and owners make decisions. If accountants bend the rules for whoever pays them, none of these users can rely on the numbers. That is why accountants follow an **ethical framework**: a set of principles that guide behaviour when there is pressure to do the wrong thing.

Both boards examine ethics. Cambridge asks you to explain the need for an ethical framework, its fundamental principles and its significance to stakeholders and society. ZIMSEC asks you to define business ethics, identify the professional ethics in accounting and apply them to day-to-day business transactions.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge principles"}
Cambridge 0452 names five fundamental principles: **integrity**, **objectivity**, **professional competence and due care**, **confidentiality** and **professional behaviour**. ZIMSEC lists **professionalism**, **integrity**, **confidentiality**, **competence**, **objectivity** and **compliance**, and links business ethics to **Unhu/Ubuntu/Vumunhu**.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Business ethics"}
The moral principles that guide how a business and its people behave: doing what is right and fair in dealings with customers, suppliers, employees, the government and society, not only what is legal or profitable.
:::

:::definition{term="Ethical framework"}
A set of principles (often a written code of ethics) that accountants follow so that their work can be trusted, and that helps them decide what to do when they face pressure or a conflict of interest.
:::

:::definition{term="Stakeholders"}
Everyone with an interest in the business and its accounts: owners, managers, employees, lenders, suppliers, customers, the government (tax authority) and the community.
:::

## The fundamental principles

| Principle | What it means | Example of a breach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Integrity | Being honest and straightforward in all professional and business relationships | Recording false sales to impress a lender |
| Objectivity | Not allowing bias, conflicts of interest or pressure from others to affect judgement | Valuing a relative's debt as fully collectable to please the family |
| Professional competence and due care | Keeping knowledge and skills up to date and working carefully | Preparing accounts using rules one has not learned, with many errors |
| Confidentiality | Not disclosing information about a client or employer without permission (unless the law requires it), and not using it for personal gain | Telling a competitor how much a client's business earns |
| Professional behaviour | Obeying laws and regulations and avoiding anything that discredits the profession | Helping a client hide income from the tax authority |

ZIMSEC's **compliance** means following the laws, regulations and accounting rules that apply, for example tax law and the requirement to keep proper records. **Professionalism** covers competence, careful work and conduct that brings credit to the job.

## Unhu/Ubuntu/Vumunhu

The ZIMSEC syllabus connects business ethics to **Unhu/Ubuntu/Vumunhu**, the African philosophy often summed up as "I am because we are". It values honesty, respect, fairness, responsibility to others and care for the community. In business this means treating customers and workers fairly, paying debts and taxes honestly, not exploiting people's ignorance, and recognising that a business depends on the society around it. An accountant who refuses to falsify records is not only following a code: they are acting with unhu towards the people who will rely on those records.

## Why an ethical framework is needed

- **Trust**: users of financial statements cannot check every figure themselves; they rely on the honesty of those who prepare them.
- **Pressure**: owners, managers or clients may push for a higher (or lower) profit; principles give the accountant a clear basis to refuse.
- **Conflicts of interest**: an accountant may have personal links to a client or a transaction; objectivity requires these to be declared or avoided.
- **Protection of the public**: false accounts can lead banks to lend wrongly, suppliers to lose money and the government to lose tax revenue that pays for schools and clinics.
- **Reputation**: one dishonest accountant damages trust in the whole profession.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: identifying the principle in each situation]
Rudo is an accounts clerk at a transport company in Gweru. Decide which principle is at stake and what she should do.

| Situation | Principle at stake | Ethical action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Her cousin applies for credit and asks her to approve it quickly without the usual checks | Objectivity | Declare the family link and let someone else decide, using the normal checks |
| A friend asks how much the company paid its drivers last month | Confidentiality | Refuse politely: the information belongs to the employer |
| She is asked to prepare the payroll using new tax tables she has not studied | Professional competence and due care | Learn the new rules or ask for training before doing the work |
| The manager tells her to date a sale made on 2 January as 31 December to raise this year's profit | Integrity (and compliance) | Refuse and record the sale in the correct period |
| A supplier offers her US\$100 to place orders with them | Objectivity and integrity | Refuse the gift and report it to her manager |

1. Read the situation and ask what pressure or temptation is involved.
2. Name the principle that the pressure threatens.
3. State a practical action that removes the threat: refuse, declare, ask for help, report.
:::

## Ethics and the figures

Unethical accounting changes the numbers that people rely on. Inflating profit can win a loan the business cannot repay; understating profit cheats the tax authority and, through it, the community. Applying the accounting concepts honestly (matching, prudence, business entity) is part of acting ethically.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: the cost of an unethical request]
Nyasha's records show a profit of US\$9,150 for 2025, prepared as she asked. Sipho knows that cash sales of US\$2,400 were left out and that US\$780 of her private household bills were recorded as business expenses.

**Nyasha's Market Stall: profit for the year ended 31 December 2025**

|  | US\$ | US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Profit as the owner wanted it reported |  | 9,150 |
| Add: cash sales left out of the records | 2,400 |  |
| Add: private expenses recorded as business expenses | 780 |  |
|  |  | 3,180 |
| **Profit following the ethical principles** |  | 12,330 |

1. The omitted sales and the private expenses together understate profit by $2,400 + 780 = 3,180$.
2. The honest profit is $9,150 + 3,180 = 12,330$; the reported figure is about 25.8% too low.
3. **Integrity** and **compliance (professional behaviour)** are breached: the records are false and tax law is broken. The **business entity** concept is also broken by the private bills.
4. Sipho should explain the principles to Nyasha, record all sales and treat the private bills as drawings. If she insists, he should decline to continue the work rather than prepare false accounts.
:::

## Significance to stakeholders and society

| Stakeholder | Why ethical accounting matters to them |
| --- | --- |
| Owner | Reliable figures for decisions; avoids penalties for false records |
| Lenders (banks) | Lend on true profit and assets, so loans can be repaid |
| Suppliers | Give credit to businesses that can really pay |
| Employees | Fair and correct wages and deductions; job security in a sound business |
| Government | Collects the correct tax to fund public services |
| Community | Fair dealing, honest prices and businesses that last |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing confidentiality with secrecy from the law"}
Confidentiality does not allow an accountant to hide wrongdoing from the authorities when the law requires disclosure. It protects information from being shared without permission or a legal duty.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming a principle without applying it"}
"Integrity" alone earns little. Say what the principle means and what the person should do **in this case**.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "advise" or "evaluate" questions, give a clear recommendation (refuse, declare, report, seek training) and support it with the principle and its effect on a stakeholder.
:::

:::exam-tip
Use the board's own names: Cambridge's five principles, or ZIMSEC's professionalism, integrity, confidentiality, competence, objectivity and compliance.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Business ethics are moral principles for doing what is right and fair, not only what is legal or profitable.
- An ethical framework is needed because users must trust figures they cannot check and accountants face pressure and conflicts of interest.
- The principles: integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality and professional behaviour; ZIMSEC adds professionalism and compliance and links ethics to Unhu/Ubuntu/Vumunhu.
- Unethical accounting misstates profit and harms owners, lenders, suppliers, employees, government and society.
- In a case, identify the pressure, name the principle and give a practical ethical action.
:::
