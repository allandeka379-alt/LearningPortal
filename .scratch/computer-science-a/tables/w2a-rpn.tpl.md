## Introduction

The expression `(8 + 4) * 3` needs brackets because the order in which the operators are applied is not clear from the symbols alone. Writing `8 4 + 3 *` removes the need for brackets, and removes the need for rules about precedence at the time of calculation: the operators simply act in the order they are written. This is **Reverse Polish Notation (RPN)**, also called **postfix** notation, in which each operator comes **after** its operands. A compiler converts expressions into this form because a computer can then evaluate them with a **stack** without looking ahead.

This lesson explains why RPN suits a stack machine, how to convert **infix** to postfix (the shunting-yard idea), how to convert postfix back to infix and how to evaluate a postfix expression with a stack. Every conversion and evaluation was done by code.

You should already know the operations on a stack (push and pop) and the precedence of the arithmetic operators.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Infix notation"}
The usual way of writing an expression, with each operator between its two operands, for example `3 + 4`. Brackets and precedence rules decide the order of the operations.
:::

:::definition{term="Reverse Polish Notation (postfix)"}
A way of writing an expression in which each operator comes after its operands, for example `3 4 +`. It needs no brackets and no precedence rules.
:::

:::definition{term="Operator precedence"}
The rule that decides which operator is applied first when there are no brackets: multiplication and division before addition and subtraction.
:::

## Why RPN suits a stack machine

To evaluate a postfix expression, read the tokens from left to right:

- if the token is a **number**, push it on the stack;
- if the token is an **operator**, pop the top two values, apply the operator and push the result.

At the end, the single value on the stack is the answer. The processor never has to look ahead or remember brackets, and each value is used as soon as it is available, so evaluation is simple and fast. Calculators and the code generated by compilers use this idea.

## Evaluating postfix

:::worked-solution{title="Evaluating 8 4 + 3 * 10 5 / -"}
{{evA}}

1. `8 4 +` leaves 12; then `3 *` gives 36.
2. `10 5 /` gives 2.
3. The final `-` subtracts: 36 - 2.
4. The value is **{{valA}}**.

For the operators `-` and `/` the **second** value popped is the right-hand operand: with the stack `36, 2` the subtraction is 36 - 2, not 2 - 36.
:::

## Converting infix to postfix

Use an **operator stack** (the shunting-yard method). Read the infix tokens from left to right:

1. **Operand** (number or variable): add it straight to the output.
2. **Operator:** while the operator on top of the stack has **higher or equal precedence**, pop it to the output; then push the new operator.
3. **Opening bracket:** push it.
4. **Closing bracket:** pop operators to the output until the opening bracket is reached, then discard both brackets.
5. At the end, pop all the remaining operators to the output.

:::worked-solution{title="Converting ( 8 + 4 ) * 3 - 10 / 5"}
{{shuntA}}

The postfix expression is:

{{rpnA}}

1. The brackets force the `+` to be applied before the `*`, so `+` is popped when `)` arrives.
2. When `-` is read, the `*` on the stack has higher precedence and is popped to the output first.
3. When `/` is read, the `-` has lower precedence, so `/` is pushed on top of it.
4. At the end the stack is emptied: `/` then `-`.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Converting 12 - 3 * 2 + 18 / 6"}
{{shuntB}}

The postfix expression is:

{{rpnB}}

1. `*` is pushed on top of `-` because it has higher precedence.
2. When `+` is read, both `*` and `-` are popped because they have precedence higher than or equal to `+`.
3. Evaluating it gives {{valB}}.
:::

Exponentiation (`^`) groups from the right: `2 ^ 3 ^ 2` means `2 ^ (3 ^ 2)` and becomes `{{pow}}`.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reversing the operands of - and /"}
After popping two values, the one popped **first** is the right-hand operand. With `10 5 /` the stack holds 10 below 5, so the result is 10 ÷ 5, not 5 ÷ 10.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaving operators on the stack at the end"}
When the infix tokens are used up, every operator still on the stack must be popped to the output.
:::

:::mistake{title="Popping on lower precedence"}
Pop the operator on top of the stack only when its precedence is **higher or equal** to the new operator's, not when it is lower.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing brackets in the postfix answer"}
Postfix never contains brackets. They are used only to decide the order while converting.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- In RPN every operator follows its operands, so no brackets or precedence rules are needed to evaluate it.
- Evaluation pushes numbers and, for each operator, pops two values, applies it and pushes the result.
- Infix is converted using an operator stack: operands go to the output, operators wait on the stack until a lower-precedence operator or the end.
- Brackets are pushed and popped as markers and never appear in the postfix output.
- Postfix is converted to infix by building bracketed strings on a stack.
:::
